## Overview

Respiratory failure is defined as the inability of the respiratory system to maintain adequate gas exchange, resulting in arterial hypoxaemia (PaO₂ <60 mmHg), with or without hypercapnia (PaCO₂ >45 mmHg). It is a physiological diagnosis requiring blood gas analysis — clinical assessment and pulse oximetry alone are insufficient. Understanding the type and mechanism of failure is essential because it determines both the aetiology and the correct intervention.

---

## Pathophysiology

**Type 1 Respiratory Failure** (hypoxaemic): PaO₂ <60 mmHg, PaCO₂ normal or low. The primary problem is oxygenation — the lungs cannot transfer oxygen from alveoli to blood. The patient hyperventilates to compensate, keeping or lowering PaCO₂.

Mechanisms:
- **V/Q mismatch** (most common) — perfusion exceeds ventilation in affected areas; blood passes through underventilated alveoli without being oxygenated. The mechanism in pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, PE, and ARDS. Supplemental oxygen improves V/Q mismatch by raising the FiO₂ in underventilated areas.
- **Shunt** — blood bypasses ventilated alveoli entirely (intracardiac right-to-left shunt, or complete alveolar consolidation/collapse). Supplemental oxygen **cannot** improve a true shunt — the blood never contacts the oxygen.
- **Diffusion impairment** — thickened alveolar membrane in ILD reduces gas transfer. Primarily manifests on exertion when transit time through the pulmonary capillary is reduced.
- **Hypoventilation** — reduces alveolar oxygen tension; always accompanied by ↑PaCO₂ (see Type 2).

**Type 2 Respiratory Failure** (hypercapnic/ventilatory): PaO₂ <60 mmHg + PaCO₂ >45 mmHg. The primary problem is ventilation — the lungs (or the pump driving them) cannot achieve sufficient minute ventilation to eliminate CO₂ or maintain alveolar oxygen.

Mechanisms — the respiratory pump fails when:
- **Reduced drive:** Brainstem injury, opiate or sedative toxicity, obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS)
- **Neuromuscular failure:** Myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), spinal cord injury, motor neurone disease, phrenic nerve palsy
- **Chest wall/mechanical failure:** Severe obesity (OHS), kyphoscoliosis, flail chest
- **Airway obstruction with fatigue:** Severe COPD exacerbation, severe asthma, upper airway obstruction

**Acid-base interpretation:**

| ABG Pattern | Interpretation |
|------------|---------------|
| Low pH, high PaCO₂, high HCO₃ | Acute-on-chronic type 2 failure — metabolic compensation reflects pre-existing chronic hypercapnia |
| Normal pH, high PaCO₂, high HCO₃ | Compensated chronic type 2 failure — this is the patient's baseline (e.g., COPD) |
| Low pH, high PaCO₂, normal HCO₃ | Acute type 2 failure — no compensation yet; this is a rapid deterioration |
| Low pH, low PaCO₂, low HCO₃ | Metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation |
| Low pH, normal PaCO₂, normal HCO₃ | Mixed picture — assess clinical context |

The bicarbonate is the key to chronicity. A raised HCO₃ in a hypercapnic patient indicates the kidneys have had time to retain bicarbonate — this is a **chronic** process. In acute type 2 failure, the HCO₃ is normal because renal compensation takes 3–5 days.

**ARDS (Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome):** A specific, severe form of type 1 failure. Defined by the Berlin criteria: onset within 1 week of a clinical insult; bilateral infiltrates on CXR/CT not explained by effusion, collapse, or nodules; respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; and PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio <300 mmHg (mild), <200 mmHg (moderate), or <100 mmHg (severe). Common causes include pneumonia, sepsis, aspiration, and trauma. Management requires lung-protective ventilation (tidal volume 6 mL/kg ideal body weight), proning (>16 hours/day in severe ARDS), and ECMO for refractory cases.

## Clinical Presentation

**Type 1:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, tachycardia, cyanosis. Mental status is often preserved until hypoxaemia is severe (PaO₂ <40 mmHg). Signs of the underlying cause dominate (crepitations in pulmonary oedema, unilateral reduced breath sounds in pneumonia).

**Type 2:** Dyspnoea, but also — critically — **features of hypercapnia**: headache (CO₂ is a vasodilator), confusion, drowsiness, asterixis (flapping tremor of outstretched hands), warm peripheries and bounding pulse (vasodilation), and ultimately coma. CO₂ narcosis can develop insidiously. The presence of hypercapnic encephalopathy (confusion, asterixis) indicates severe ventilatory failure requiring immediate intervention.

**Clinical scenarios by mechanism:**

| Type | Clinical Context | Mechanism |
|------|----------------|-----------|
| Type 1 | Pneumonia, pulmonary oedema, ARDS, PE | V/Q mismatch or shunt |
| Type 1 | Severe atelectasis, large pleural effusion | Shunt |
| Type 1 | ILD on exercise | Diffusion impairment |
| Type 2 | COPD/asthma exacerbation | Airway obstruction + respiratory muscle fatigue |
| Type 2 | Opiate overdose, post-operative sedation | Reduced central drive |
| Type 2 | GBS, myasthenia crisis, MND | Neuromuscular failure |
| Type 2 | Severe obesity (OHS), kyphoscoliosis | Mechanical disadvantage |

## Diagnosis

**ABG is mandatory** — clinical assessment and pulse oximetry alone are insufficient to determine the type of respiratory failure, quantify hypercapnia, or assess acid-base status. SpO₂ of 90% could represent PaO₂ of 60 mmHg (acceptable) or a chronic compensated state in a CO₂ retainer — the ABG distinguishes these.

The **alveolar-arterial (A-a) gradient** localises the problem:
- Normal A-a gradient with hypoxaemia → hypoventilation (look for elevated PaCO₂) — the lung parenchyma is normal, the pump has failed
- Elevated A-a gradient with hypoxaemia → V/Q mismatch, shunt, or diffusion impairment — the lungs themselves are abnormal

Normal A-a gradient = (Age/4) + 4 (in mmHg, increases with age).

**Further investigations directed by type:**
- CXR: consolidation, pulmonary oedema, pleural effusion, hyperinflation, pneumothorax
- FBC, U&E, CRP, cultures if infectious cause
- CTPA if PE suspected
- Echo if cardiogenic cause suspected
- Spirometry and nerve conduction studies/EMG if neuromuscular disease suspected

## Management

### Type 1 Respiratory Failure

Treat the underlying cause. Supplemental oxygen to maintain SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% if known or suspected CO₂ retainer — use Venturi mask). **Never give uncontrolled high-flow oxygen to a COPD patient.**

**Venturi mask — precise oxygen delivery:**

| Mask Colour | FiO₂ Delivered |
|------------|---------------|
| Blue | 24% |
| White | 28% |
| Yellow | 35% |
| Red | 40% |
| Green | 60% |

Use Venturi masks whenever a precise FiO₂ is needed. Standard reservoir masks cannot guarantee FiO₂.

**High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO/AIRVO):** Delivers up to 60 L/min of heated, humidified, blended gas. Advantages: reduces dead space by washing CO₂ from the nasopharynx; provides approximately 1 cmH₂O of CPAP per 10 L/min flow; better tolerated than tight-fitting CPAP masks for prolonged use. Used in type 1 failure; not a substitute for NIV in type 2.

**CPAP** — continuous positive airway pressure throughout the respiratory cycle; maintains alveolar recruitment; preferred for cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (reduces preload and afterload by increasing intrathoracic pressure) and moderate hypoxaemic failure. Does not actively assist ventilation — the patient must drive all breaths themselves.

**Intubation and invasive ventilation:** If FiO₂ >0.6 required to maintain adequate SpO₂, if the patient cannot protect their airway, or if the patient is exhausting. Use lung-protective settings in ARDS (tidal volume 6 mL/kg IBW, plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O).

### Type 2 Respiratory Failure

**Controlled oxygen — the critical step.** Avoid hyperoxia in chronic CO₂ retainers. Target SpO₂ **88–92%**. The mechanism is not simply "removing hypoxic drive" — it also involves reversal of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction (worsening V/Q mismatch) and the **Haldane effect** (CO₂ displacement from haemoglobin at higher PaO₂ raises dissolved CO₂). Start with 24% (blue Venturi); recheck ABG at 30–60 minutes after any oxygen change.

**Treat the precipitant** — antibiotics for infection, bronchodilators and steroids for COPD exacerbation, naloxone for opiate overdose (0.4mg IV, may need repeated doses or infusion as naloxone has shorter half-life than most opiates), neostigmine for myasthenia crisis.

**Non-invasive ventilation (NIV — BiPAP)** is the cornerstone of management for type 2 respiratory failure in COPD:

| Setting | Typical Values |
|---------|---------------|
| IPAP | 12–20 cmH₂O |
| EPAP | 4–6 cmH₂O |
| FiO₂ | Titrate to SpO₂ 88–92% |
| Backup rate | 12–16 breaths/min |

**Indications for NIV:** pH 7.25–7.35 with PaCO₂ >45 mmHg in COPD exacerbation; type 2 failure from other causes of hypercapnia (OHS, chest wall disease, neuromuscular disease). NIV reduces intubation rates and mortality in COPD exacerbation — this is evidence grade A.

**NIV contraindications:** Reduced conscious level (GCS <8 — cannot protect airway), copious or unmanageable secretions, undrained pneumothorax, facial trauma making mask fit impossible, vomiting, haemodynamic instability (BP <90 mmHg), recent upper GI or airway surgery.

**Target:** pH rising toward 7.35, PaCO₂ falling, patient comfortable. Recheck ABG at 1 hour. If no improvement on NIV after 1–2 hours — escalate.

**Intubation and invasive ventilation:** When NIV fails or is contraindicated. Requires careful consideration of goals of care and reversibility, particularly in end-stage COPD — a ceiling of care discussion before deterioration is far preferable to an emergency decision.

**Neuromuscular causes:** Serial measurement of **vital capacity** (not ABG alone) — VC <1.5L indicates need for elective intubation before crisis develops. In GBS and myasthenia, patients can be remarkably alert and communicative right up until the point of respiratory arrest. The falling VC is the trigger to intubate electively and safely; waiting for a rising PaCO₂ is too late.

## Complications

- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)** — common in intubated patients; use VAP bundle (head-of-bed elevation, oral hygiene, daily sedation holds)
- **Oxygen toxicity** — prolonged hyperoxia worsens V/Q mismatch and causes absorption atelectasis; avoid FiO₂ >0.6 for prolonged periods
- **Barotrauma / volutrauma** — pneumothorax, pneumomediastinum from invasive ventilation; prevent with lung-protective settings
- **ICU-acquired weakness** — muscle wasting from immobility, steroids, neuromuscular blockade; early physiotherapy reduces risk
- **Post-ICU syndrome** — cognitive impairment, PTSD, and physical deconditioning following critical illness

## Clinical Insight

Oxygen is a drug. In type 2 respiratory failure with chronic hypercapnia, high-flow oxygen is potentially lethal. The mechanism is not simply "removing hypoxic drive" — it also involves the Haldane effect and reversal of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, both of which worsen CO₂ retention. Use Venturi masks to deliver precise FiO₂. Start at 24% (blue Venturi). Titrate to SpO₂ 88–92%. Recheck the ABG 30–60 minutes after any oxygen adjustment. If you see a patient deteriorating on a non-rebreather mask, the oxygen may be part of the problem.

NIV should be started early in COPD exacerbation with type 2 failure — before the patient becomes severely acidotic. Once pH falls below 7.25, NIV becomes less effective and intubation more likely. The window where NIV works best is pH 7.25–7.35. Waiting until the patient is comatose to start NIV is too late.

Neuromuscular causes of type 2 failure (GBS, myasthenia) are missed because these patients can be remarkably alert and communicative right up until the point of respiratory arrest. The critical monitoring parameter is serial vital capacity — a falling VC, not a worsening ABG, is the trigger to intubate electively and safely before crisis develops.
