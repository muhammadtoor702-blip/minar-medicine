## Overview

Environmental emergencies — hypothermia, heat stroke, drowning, and decompression sickness — represent physiological extremes imposed by the external environment. They share a common principle: core physiology has been fundamentally disrupted, and standard resuscitation algorithms must be adapted accordingly. Hypothermia deserves particular emphasis because it is simultaneously a reversible cause of cardiac arrest and one of the most commonly mismanaged emergencies in the field. The phrase that governs all resuscitation decisions in severe hypothermia is "**not dead until warm and dead**" — patients with apparent death from hypothermia (no pulse, fixed dilated pupils, GCS 3) can make full neurological recovery after rewarming to normothermia. This is not sentiment; it is physiology.

## Pathophysiology

### Hypothermia — Physiology of Cold

**Definition:** Core temperature <35°C. The Swiss staging system is the most clinically useful:

| Stage | Core Temp | Clinical Features |
|-------|-----------|-------------------|
| HT I (Mild) | 32–35°C | Shivering, impaired coordination, confusion, tachycardia |
| HT II (Moderate) | 28–32°C | Shivering stops (exhaustion of mechanism), progressive impaired consciousness, bradycardia, J waves on ECG |
| HT III (Severe) | 24–28°C | Unconscious, no shivering, cardiac arrhythmias (AF → VF risk), hypotension |
| HT IV (Cardiac arrest) | <24°C | No vital signs; VF or asystole |

**Mechanisms of harm:**
- **Shivering thermogenesis** (HT I): Skeletal muscle tremor increases metabolic heat production up to 5× basal rate — effective short-term defence
- **Cardiovascular:** Cold depresses SA node and conduction → bradycardia; cold cardiomyopathy → reduced cardiac output; below 28–30°C → susceptibility to VF (cold myocardium is highly arrhythmogenic); below 20°C → asystole
- **Osborn J wave:** Characteristic ECG finding in hypothermia — positive deflection at the junction of QRS and ST segment (J point); appears at temperatures <32°C; amplitude increases as temperature falls; pathognomonic of hypothermia but not specific to it
- **Haematological:** Blood viscosity increases → microvascular sludging; coagulopathy (clotting enzymes work poorly at cold temperatures — standard clotting tests done at 37°C in the lab may appear normal even when patient is coagulopathic)
- **Cerebral protection:** Cold reduces cerebral metabolic rate by ~6–7% per 1°C below 37°C → at 18°C, CMRO₂ is reduced to <20% of normal → brain tolerates prolonged ischaemia without permanent damage (basis for surgical deep hypothermic circulatory arrest in cardiac surgery)

### Heat Stroke — Hyperthermia Physiology

**Classic heat stroke:** Passive hyperthermia from hot environment (typically elderly, immobile, without air conditioning during heat wave); core temperature >40°C; anhidrosis (sweat gland failure).

**Exertional heat stroke:** Active hyperthermia from intense exercise in hot/humid conditions (athletes, military recruits, construction workers); core temperature >40°C; diaphoresis may be present; younger patients.

**Physiological cascade:** Core temperature >40°C → direct protein denaturation; endothelial cell injury → systemic inflammatory response → MODS (AKI, hepatic failure, coagulopathy, ARDS); CNS most sensitive → cerebellar Purkinje cell injury → cerebellar ataxia (lasting sequelae).

**Distinction from heat exhaustion:** Heat exhaustion = hyperthermia <40°C + profuse sweating + pallor + weakness/fainting but **no CNS dysfunction** (no confusion, no GCS impairment). Heat stroke = temperature >40°C + **altered consciousness** (defining feature). This distinction determines immediate management.

### Drowning — Mechanisms

**Primary mechanism:** Laryngospasm → breath-holding → hypoxia → loss of consciousness → passive inhalation of water → secondary hypoxia/hypercapnia → cardiac arrest.

**Mammalian dive reflex:** Particularly prominent in children and in cold water. Cold water contact with face → apnoea + bradycardia + peripheral vasoconstriction + preferential perfusion of brain and heart → extends survivable submersion time. Basis for cases of full neurological recovery after cold-water drowning of >30 minutes.

**Wet vs dry drowning:** 85% aspirate water (wet); 15% have persistent laryngospasm (dry — no aspiration). This distinction is less clinically important than resuscitation quality.

**Secondary drowning/aquatic post-immersion syndrome:** Pulmonary oedema can develop up to 24–72h after submersion (ARDS-like mechanism from aspirated water); all drowning patients require observation even if initially asymptomatic.

### Decompression Sickness (DCS)

**Mechanism:** SCUBA diving → breathing compressed air at depth (partial pressure of N₂ rises proportionally) → nitrogen dissolves in tissues (Henry's law). Rapid ascent → pressure drops → dissolved N₂ comes out of solution as bubbles in tissues/blood → embolic and mechanical injury.

**Type 1 DCS:** Musculoskeletal (joint/limb pain "the bends"), skin (marbling), lymphatic obstruction; not immediately life-threatening.

**Type 2 DCS:** Neurological (spinal cord most common — paraplegia; cerebral — stroke-like), pulmonary ("the chokes" — pulmonary emboli from venous bubbles), cardiovascular (arterial gas embolism via patent foramen ovale).

**Arterial gas embolism (AGE):** Air enters pulmonary veins during rapid ascent (pulmonary barotrauma) → arterial circulation → coronary or cerebral emboli → MI or stroke-like presentation on surfacing. Most serious diving emergency.

## Clinical Presentation

### Hypothermia — Assessment

**Temperature measurement:** Rectal or oesophageal (gold standard — most accurate core temperature); tympanic unreliable in cold patients; oral/axillary inaccurate. **Hypothermia thermometers measuring down to 15°C are essential** — standard clinical thermometers only read down to 34°C.

**The hypothermic patient in cardiac arrest:**
- VF on ECG (most common shockable rhythm) or asystole
- Absent carotid pulse — mandatory 1-minute check (cold, bradycardic pulse easily missed)
- Fixed dilated pupils — do NOT take as sign of death in hypothermia
- Rigor mortis — can occur in severe hypothermia without death (cold stiffness); true rigor only after hours of cardiac arrest
- **Serum potassium >12 mEq/L:** The most reliable predictor of irreversible death in hypothermic cardiac arrest — hyperkalemia indicates cell death (irreversible ischaemia); if K⁺ >12 mEq/L, resuscitation unlikely to succeed

### Heat Stroke — Recognition

- Core temperature >40°C (rectal)
- **Altered consciousness: confusion → delirium → seizure → coma** (defining feature of heat stroke vs heat exhaustion)
- Hot, dry skin (classic heat stroke) or diaphoretic (exertional)
- Tachycardia, hypotension
- **Rhabdomyolysis:** Myalgia, dark "cola-coloured" urine (myoglobinuria) → AKI

### Drowning — Triage

All drowning victims — even those who appear well — require at least 4–6 hours of observation (secondary drowning risk). Assess:
- SpO₂, respiratory rate, work of breathing
- GCS
- Core temperature (cold-water drowning → concurrent hypothermia)
- ECG (dysrhythmia from hypoxia/hypothermia)

## Diagnosis

### Hypothermia Investigations

- **Rectal temperature** (essential)
- **Serum potassium** (K⁺ >12 mEq/L = irreversible cell death)
- **Blood gas:** Hypothermia causes spurious results — gas analysers run samples at 37°C; values should be corrected for patient temperature
- **ECG:** Osborn J waves (pathognomonic at <32°C), PR and QT prolongation, bradycardia, AF
- **FBC, coagulation:** Hypothermic coagulopathy
- **Glucose:** Hypothyroidism, hypoglycaemia as precipitant; glucose consumption reduced in cold
- **TFTs:** Hypothyroidism as precipitant of hypothermia in elderly
- **Blood alcohol:** Ethanol causes vasodilation (accelerates heat loss) + impairs shivering + clouds presentation

### Heat Stroke Investigations

- **Core temperature** (rectal)
- **LFTs:** Hepatic injury — AST/ALT rise within hours; often dramatic (>1,000 IU/L)
- **CK:** Rhabdomyolysis — extremely elevated in exertional heat stroke
- **U&E:** AKI (from rhabdomyolysis + hypovolaemia)
- **FBC + coagulation:** DIC (common in severe heat stroke)
- **Glucose:** Hypoglycaemia from glycogen depletion in exertional

## Management

### Hypothermia — Rewarming Strategy

**General principles:**
- Handle gently — unnecessary movement of a cold heart triggers VF
- Remove wet clothing; insulate to prevent further heat loss
- Horizontal positioning (standing causes peripheral vasoconstriction release → "rescue collapse")

**Rewarming methods by severity:**

**HT I (Mild, >32°C):**
- Passive external rewarming (remove wet clothes, warm blankets, warm environment)
- Warm sweet drinks (if alert and can swallow safely)

**HT II–III (Moderate-Severe, no cardiac arrest):**
- **Active external rewarming:** Warm air blanket (Bair Hugger), chemical heat packs to axilla/groin/chest (never directly on skin)
- **Active internal rewarming:** Warmed IV fluids (42°C); warmed humidified oxygen (42°C); bladder irrigation; pleural lavage (via chest drains, effective but invasive)

**HT IV (Cardiac arrest <28–30°C):**
- **CPR as per standard ALS protocol** — compress at standard rate and depth
- **Defibrillation:** Maximum 3 shocks for VF if core temperature <30°C — cold myocardium less responsive; do not delay further shocks if temperature rising; once >30°C, defibrillate as per standard protocol
- **Drug therapy:** Adrenaline/amiodarone may be ineffective and accumulate at cold temperatures — most guidelines recommend withholding or spacing at double intervals until core temperature >30°C
- **TARGET: ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation) or CPB (cardiopulmonary bypass)** — the gold standard for rewarming in hypothermic cardiac arrest; can raise core temperature at ~1–2°C/min; ECMO simultaneously supports circulation; only available at specialist centres
- Transfer to ECMO-capable centre while performing continuous CPR
- **CPR should continue until core temperature is >35°C** — only then can the decision not to resuscitate be made

### Heat Stroke — Emergency Cooling

**Time is critical:** The longer the duration of temperature >40°C, the greater the degree of organ injury. Target: core temperature <38.9°C within 30 minutes.

**Most effective cooling methods (fastest temperature reduction):**
1. **Cold water immersion** (ice water bath): Rate of cooling ~0.2°C/min — most effective available method; used for exertional heat stroke; logistically challenging in sick patients
2. **Evaporative cooling** (spray tepid water + fanning): Nearly as effective; more practical in A&E
3. **Ice packs** to neck, axilla, groin (areas of major vessels)
4. **Cold IV fluids** — modest cooling effect but corrects hypovolaemia

**Dantrolene is NOT indicated:** A common exam trap — dantrolene is the treatment for **malignant hyperthermia** (a pharmacogenetic disorder triggered by volatile anaesthetics/succinylcholine), NOT heat stroke. They share hyperthermia but have different mechanisms. Dantrolene (ryanodine receptor blocker → reduces Ca²⁺ release from SR → reduces muscle contracture) has no role in heat stroke.

**Active cooling stops when temperature reaches 38.5–39°C** to avoid overshoot hypothermia.

**Shivering suppression:** Shivering in heat stroke counterproductively generates more heat — benzodiazepines (lorazepam) suppress shivering and treat seizures simultaneously.

### Drowning — Resuscitation

- **Rescue breathing in water** if trained rescuer and feasible — reduces cardiac arrest rate
- On land: C-A-B sequence but start with **5 rescue breaths** (priority is hypoxia, unlike standard cardiac arrest)
- CPR as per ALS; defibrillate for shockable rhythms
- Concurrent hypothermia in cold-water drowning → rewarm simultaneously
- **NEVER abandon resuscitation** in cold-water drowning without hospital assessment — the dive reflex and cerebral protection from cold can sustain neurological viability well beyond the usual resuscitation timeframe

### Decompression Sickness — Recompression

- **100% O₂ by non-rebreather mask immediately** — accelerates nitrogen washout (Henry's law reversal); reduces bubble size
- **IV fluids** — hydration reduces blood viscosity and DCS risk
- **Recompression therapy in hyperbaric chamber** — definitive treatment; increases pressure to force nitrogen back into solution, then slow controlled decompression; reduces symptom burden and neurological sequelae; urgent transfer to hyperbaric facility
- Contact diving emergency services (DAN — Divers Alert Network) for advice

## Complications

- **Hypothermia — after rewarming:** Reperfusion injury; pulmonary oedema; coagulopathy; infection (aspiration, pressure sores)
- **Heat stroke:** Liver failure (can occur 24–72h after event despite cooling); AKI from rhabdomyolysis; DIC; ARDS; cerebellar dysfunction (persistent ataxia in survivors)
- **Drowning:** Secondary drowning (delayed pulmonary oedema — 4–72h); pneumonia (aspirated water → bacterial); anoxic brain injury; cervical spine injury (diving into shallow water — immobilise spine until excluded)
- **DCS:** Permanent neurological deficit (spinal cord injury); avascular necrosis of bone (chronic DCS — femoral head, shoulder joints from repeated bubble injury to bone); dysbaric osteonecrosis

## Clinical Insight

The physiological basis of the "not dead until warm and dead" principle is one of the most important concepts in emergency medicine, and it is frequently misunderstood. The principle does not apply universally — it specifically applies to **primary hypothermia** (cold as the primary cause of arrest). In a patient with severe trauma, haemorrhage, or asphyxia who is also cold (secondary hypothermia), the prognosis is entirely different. The key discriminator is serum potassium at the time of cardiac arrest. K⁺ <12 mEq/L → cells still intact → potential for full recovery with ECMO rewarming; K⁺ >12 mEq/L → cell lysis is irreversible regardless of temperature.

The dantrolene/heat stroke distinction is a perennial examination trap. The mechanism of malignant hyperthermia — uncontrolled skeletal muscle calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum via mutant RYR1 ryanodine receptors — is mechanistically entirely distinct from heat stroke, where the hyperthermia is environmental and the muscle injury is secondary, not primary. Dantrolene has never been shown to be effective in heat stroke in any clinical trial and could potentially harm by causing muscle weakness/respiratory depression in an already vulnerable patient.

The Osborn J wave is one of the most visually distinctive ECG findings in all of emergency medicine. When a cold patient with altered consciousness arrives in A&E, the ECG is simultaneously diagnostic (hypothermia), prognostic (severity correlates with J wave amplitude), and prognostically guiding (VF management). A common scenario is a patient with VF and J waves interpreted as a primary cardiac arrest — but if the patient is hypothermic, the VF may be driven by temperature, not by coronary disease, and the patient may need ECMO rewarming rather than emergency PCI.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 72F, found on cold kitchen floor, rectal temperature 26.4°C (HT III — severe hypothermia), GCS 6, J waves, bradycardia 38 bpm, intermittent pulselessness.

**Diagnosis: Severe/profound hypothermia (HT III–IV border) with peri-arrest state**

**Step 1 — Immediate assessment:**
- Check for pulse for 1 full minute (slow, bradycardic pulse easily missed)
- Attach cardiac monitor: J waves + bradycardia = severe hypothermia
- Core temperature confirmed: 26.4°C

**Step 2 — Is this cardiac arrest?**
- Intermittently pulseless → check rhythm: VF or asystole?
- If VF → CPR + up to 3 defibrillation attempts; if no response with temperature <30°C → continue CPR, withhold further shocks until >30°C
- If pulse present (even faint) → do NOT start CPR (CPR in beating hypothermic heart can precipitate VF)

**Step 3 — Investigations simultaneously:**
- Serum potassium urgently (K⁺ >12 mEq/L = prognostic of irreversible death)
- ABG (temperature-corrected), glucose, TFTs, FBC, coag, LFTs
- Blood cultures (hypothermia may mask sepsis as precipitant — consider thyroid crisis, hypoglycaemia, sepsis as precipitants)

**Step 4 — Rewarming (active, urgent):**
- Remove all wet/cold clothing; warm blankets
- Warmed IV fluids (40°C) running
- Warmed humidified O₂ (42°C) via non-rebreather or intubation
- Bair Hugger warm air blanket
- Target: raise temperature 1–2°C per hour

**Step 5 — If cardiac arrest develops or K⁺ <12 mEq/L:**
- Continue CPR; contact ECMO centre immediately
- **Transfer to ECMO-capable centre while CPR ongoing** — ECMO/CPB rewarming is the definitive treatment for hypothermic cardiac arrest
- Do not cease resuscitation until core temperature >35°C

**Step 6 — Address precipitants:**
- Why was she in an unheated flat? Hypothyroidism (check TFTs)? Cognitive impairment? Mobility issues?
- Safeguarding concerns — unheated home, isolated elderly woman

**Exam trigger:** *Hypothermic cardiac arrest with J waves* → CPR + maximum 3 defib attempts if VF until >30°C; check serum K⁺; transfer to ECMO centre; do NOT declare death until rewarmed to >35°C. The distractor is terminating resuscitation because of "fixed dilated pupils and no response to defibrillation" — this is wrong in primary hypothermia.
