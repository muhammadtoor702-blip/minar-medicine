## Overview

Menstrual disorders are among the most common reasons women seek gynaecological care. Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB) affects 1 in 3 women at some point in their reproductive lives, causes iron-deficiency anaemia, and significantly impairs quality of life. The diagnostic approach follows a systematic framework — identifying structural causes (PALM: polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, malignancy) versus non-structural causes (COEIN: coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial, iatrogenic, not yet classified) — which directly determines the treatment approach. The management spectrum ranges from simple pharmacological measures to definitive surgery, and the choice must be guided by the woman's symptoms, reproductive wishes, comorbidities, and personal preferences.

## Pathophysiology

### PALM-COEIN Classification (FIGO 2011)

**Structural causes (PALM):**
- **P — Polyp:** Focal endometrial hyperplasia forming a soft pedunculated mass; intermenstrual and post-coital bleeding; diagnosed on saline infusion sonohysterography or hysteroscopy; removed by hysteroscopic polypectomy
- **A — Adenomyosis:** Endometrial glands and stroma embedded within the myometrium → inflammatory response + impaired myometrial contractility during menstruation → heavy, prolonged, painful periods; MRI gold standard (heterogeneous myometrium, junctional zone thickness >12 mm); uterus symmetrically enlarged, tender ("globular uterus")
- **L — Leiomyoma (fibroids):** Most common uterine tumour; smooth muscle origin; oestrogen-dependent; subserosal (bulge outward, least effect on bleeding), intramural (within muscle), submucosal (bulge into cavity, cause greatest HMB via distorted endometrium and impaired contractile ability). Classified by FIGO leiomyoma subclassification (Type 0–8)
- **M — Malignancy/hyperplasia:** Endometrial cancer, endometrial hyperplasia with atypia, cervical cancer (CIN/invasive cancer causing post-coital or intermenstrual bleeding — different from HMB)

**Non-structural causes (COEIN):**
- **C — Coagulopathy:** Von Willebrand disease (most common — 10–13% of women presenting with HMB have vWD; often undiagnosed); platelet function disorders; anticoagulant therapy
- **O — Ovulatory dysfunction:** PCOS, hypothyroidism, hyperprolactinaemia, perimenopausal anovulation; anovulatory cycles → unopposed oestrogen → endometrial hyperproliferation → erratic, heavy bleeding
- **E — Endometrial:** Normal endometrial function disrupted — excess prostaglandins (PGE₂/PGF₂α) from the endometrium → failed vasoconstriction at menstruation → HMB; excessive fibrinolytic activity
- **I — Iatrogenic:** Hormonal contraception, IUD (copper coil — increases local prostaglandin production), anticoagulants, hormone replacement therapy, tamoxifen
- **N — Not yet classified:** No identifiable structural or hormonal cause

### Heavy Menstrual Bleeding — Mechanism

Normal menstruation relies on precise prostaglandin-mediated vasoconstriction (PGF₂α) and platelet plug formation to limit blood loss. When this fails:
- **Prostaglandin imbalance:** Submucosal fibroids and adenomyosis alter the endometrial prostaglandin profile → excess PGE₂ (vasodilator) relative to PGF₂α (vasoconstrictor) → impaired vascular plug formation
- **Endometrial fibrinolysis:** Excess tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) → premature clot dissolution → continued haemorrhage
- **Mechanically distorted cavity:** Submucosal fibroid or polyp increases endometrial surface area and disrupts normal regenerative haemostasis

## Clinical Presentation

### Heavy Menstrual Bleeding (HMB)

**Definition:** Excessive menstrual blood loss (MBL) that interferes with a woman's physical, emotional, social, and material quality of life. Objectively: ≥80 mL per cycle (alkaline haematin method — research only); subjectively, the patient's account of impaired quality of life is sufficient for clinical diagnosis.

**Clinical assessment:**
- **Duration:** How many days (normal ≤7 days; prolonged = adenomyosis, coagulopathy)
- **Frequency:** Cycle length (normal 21–35 days); shorter intervals or irregular = ovulatory dysfunction
- **Character:** Flooding (soaking through clothes/bedding), clots, frequency of pad changes (>8 pads per day = likely HMB)
- **Associated symptoms:** Pelvic pain (fibroids, adenomyosis, PID), pressure symptoms (large uterus), dyspareunia (adenomyosis, endometriosis)
- **Intermenstrual/post-coital bleeding (PCB):** If present → must exclude endometrial/cervical pathology
- **Anaemia symptoms:** Fatigue, exertional dyspnoea, pallor
- **Reproductive wishes:** Critical for treatment planning

**Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB):** Umbrella term for all abnormal menstrual bleeding — includes HMB, irregular bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, post-coital bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding.

### Primary Dysmenorrhoea vs. Secondary Dysmenorrhoea

**Primary dysmenorrhoea:** Painful menstruation without underlying pelvic pathology; onset within 1–2 years of menarche; PGF₂α-mediated uterine contractions; colicky pain radiating to back and thighs; responds well to NSAIDs (mefenamic acid, ibuprofen) and combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP).

**Secondary dysmenorrhoea:** Painful periods due to underlying pelvic pathology (endometriosis, adenomyosis, PID, fibroids); typically onset years after menarche; deeper, constant pain + dyspareunia; requires investigation (pelvic USS, laparoscopy if endometriosis suspected).

### Amenorrhoea

**Primary amenorrhoea:** No menstruation by age 15 (if secondary sexual characteristics present) or age 13 (if absent). Causes: Turner syndrome (45,XO — short stature, streak gonads, webbed neck), imperforate hymen, Müllerian agenesis (MRKH syndrome — absent uterus/upper vagina, normal 46,XX karyotype, normal ovaries), constitutional delay.

**Secondary amenorrhoea:** Cessation of menstruation for ≥3 months in a woman who was previously menstruating. Causes:
- **Physiological:** Pregnancy (must always exclude first), lactation, menopause
- **Hypothalamic:** Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (FHA) — stress, excessive exercise, low BMI/eating disorder → GnRH pulse suppression → low FSH/LH → low oestrogen; most common cause in athletes and women with eating disorders; treat underlying cause
- **Pituitary:** Hyperprolactinaemia (prolactinoma — galactorrhoea, headache, visual field defects); Sheehan's syndrome; craniopharyngioma
- **Thyroid/adrenal:** Hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, Cushing's, congenital adrenal hyperplasia
- **Ovarian:** PCOS (most common cause of oligomenorrhoea + amenorrhoea in reproductive age), premature ovarian insufficiency (POI — FSH >25 IU/L on two occasions 4 weeks apart, age <40; associated with Turner mosaicism, autoimmune disease, prior chemotherapy/radiotherapy)
- **Uterine:** Asherman's syndrome (intrauterine adhesions from curettage or infection → obliterated cavity → amenorrhoea)

## Diagnosis

**Haematology:** FBC (Hb, MCV — iron-deficiency anaemia from HMB); coagulation screen (PT, APTT, platelets) + von Willebrand factor (antigen and activity) if HMB from menarche or personal/family history of bleeding.

**Thyroid function:** TSH — hypothyroidism causes HMB/oligo-amenorrhoea.

**Hormones (for amenorrhoea/irregular cycles):** FSH, LH (raised FSH >25 = ovarian failure; raised LH:FSH ratio in PCOS), oestradiol, prolactin, testosterone (PCOS), DHEAS, 17-OH progesterone (late-onset CAH), beta-hCG (always exclude pregnancy first).

**Transvaginal ultrasound (TVS):** First-line imaging — endometrial thickness, uterine morphology (fibroids, adenomyosis features), ovarian morphology (PCOS morphology: ≥20 follicles per ovary OR ovarian volume >10 mL), free fluid.

**Saline infusion sonohysterography (SIS):** Saline instilled into uterine cavity + TVS — better visualisation of intracavitary lesions (submucosal fibroids, polyps) than TVS alone.

**Hysteroscopy ± biopsy:** Gold standard for intracavitary pathology; direct visualisation + biopsy. Endometrial biopsy (Pipelle) recommended for all women ≥45 with HMB and for younger women with risk factors for endometrial cancer (PCOS, obesity, unopposed oestrogen, tamoxifen, nulliparity).

## Management

### Heavy Menstrual Bleeding — Treatment Ladder

**Confirm: reproductive wishes? (critical for treatment choice)**

**First-line — Pharmacological:**

1. **Levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS / Mirena):** 52 mg LNG intrauterine device; releases 20 mcg LNG/day locally → endometrial atrophy → reduces MBL by 80–90%; contraceptive; effective for 5 years; the most effective medical treatment for HMB. NICE first-line for women with no structural pathology or small fibroids (<3 cm intramural). Side effects: irregular spotting first 3–6 months, amenorrhoea in 20–50% (desired by many).

2. **Tranexamic acid 1–1.5 g TDS orally** during menstruation: antifibrinolytic; reduces MBL by ~50%; non-hormonal (suitable if hormones contraindicated or unwanted); taken only during menstruation.

3. **NSAIDs (mefenamic acid 500 mg TDS or ibuprofen 400 mg TDS):** Reduce MBL by ~30% + treat dysmenorrhoea; prostaglandin synthesis inhibition; taken only during menstruation.

4. **Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP):** Reduces MBL by ~40%; regular predictable withdrawal bleed; also treats dysmenorrhoea and provides contraception; not suitable for women with hypertension, migraine with aura, smokers >35, personal/family history of VTE.

5. **Norethisterone 5 mg TID days 5–26 of cycle** (or medroxyprogesterone acetate): Progestogen for luteal phase defect or dysfunctional uterine bleeding; less effective than LNG-IUS.

**Second-line — For women who have failed medical treatment or have structural pathology:**

**Uterine fibroids:**
- **GnRH agonists** (leuprorelin, goserelin): Induce medical menopause → fibroids shrink 30–50%; used pre-operatively to reduce fibroid size and correct anaemia; maximum 6 months (bone density loss); not a long-term solution
- **Ulipristal acetate** (selective progesterone receptor modulator): Fibroid treatment; no longer licensed in EU/UK for periodic use due to rare hepatotoxicity cases; may return to market pending investigation
- **Uterine artery embolisation (UAE):** Radiological procedure under LA+sedation; bilateral uterine artery embolisation with microspheres → fibroid infarction and shrinkage; preserves uterus; 80–85% improvement in HMB; less invasive than surgery; suitable for women wishing to avoid surgery (not recommended if strong desire for future pregnancy — risk of placentation abnormalities)
- **Laparoscopic/hysteroscopic myomectomy:** Surgical removal of fibroids; preserves uterus; indicated for large or submucosal fibroids causing HMB; risk of recurrence (new fibroids grow)
- **HIFU (high-intensity focused ultrasound / MRI-guided):** Non-invasive fibroid ablation; limited availability

**Endometrial ablation:**
- Destruction of the endometrial lining; various modalities (microwave, balloon thermal, Novasure bipolar radiofrequency)
- Indicated for HMB without significant fibroids (>3 cm submucosal fibroids are a relative contraindication) and no desire for future pregnancy
- 80% patient satisfaction; 20–30% achieve amenorrhoea; failure rate ~15–20% requiring repeat ablation or hysterectomy
- Must counsel: not a form of contraception; risk of post-ablation tubal sterilisation syndrome (retrograde menstrual blood flow in a woman who has been sterilised → cyclical pelvic pain without visible bleeding)

**Hysterectomy:**
- Definitive treatment; only option with 100% success rate for HMB
- Total abdominal hysterectomy (TAH), vaginal hysterectomy (preferred — faster recovery), laparoscopic hysterectomy; total (removal of cervix) vs. subtotal (cervix preserved)
- Indicated when: medical treatment failed or declined; large fibroids where UAE/myomectomy unsuitable; adenomyosis; patient's preference for definitive treatment after completing family
- **This patient:** No desire for future pregnancy + fibroids + Hb 9.2 → hysterectomy is appropriate after medical/less invasive options discussed

## Complications

- **Iron-deficiency anaemia from HMB:** Hb 9.2 g/dL + MCV 71 = moderate iron-deficiency anaemia; treat with oral or IV iron pre-operatively; transfusion if symptomatic Hb <8 g/dL
- **Endometrial ablation failure/complications:** Haematometra (blood trapped in an obstructed cavity post-ablation); post-ablation tubal sterilisation syndrome; adhesion formation preventing future endometrial sampling (concern: missed endometrial cancer)
- **Fibroid degeneration:** Red degeneration (in pregnancy — acute pain from fibroid outgrowing its blood supply); calcification (postmenopausal)
- **Fibroids and fertility:** Submucosal fibroids significantly reduce IVF implantation rates; intramural fibroids >4 cm may reduce fertility; myomectomy improves fertility outcomes
- **Uterine rupture:** Risk in future pregnancy after myomectomy; requires CS delivery

## Clinical Insight

The LNG-IUS (Mirena) is probably the most under-used first-line treatment for HMB in the UK. It reduces menstrual blood loss by 80–90%, often achieving amenorrhoea, and is more effective than oral medications. Yet many women (and clinicians) default to tablets first. NICE guidance (NG88) is unambiguous: offer the LNG-IUS as first-line treatment for HMB when no structural pathology is present, after ruling out malignancy. The Mirena is also a contraceptive, treats dysmenorrhoea, and can be used as the progestogenic component of HRT permenopausally — its utility extends far beyond HMB.

The tranexamic acid mechanism is underestimated. It is not a hormonal treatment and does not affect the menstrual cycle — it simply reduces fibrinolysis during menstruation. Women who want to retain their fertility, cannot tolerate hormones, or are waiting for surgical treatment can use tranexamic acid every cycle to reduce blood loss by approximately half, buying time and correcting anaemia pre-operatively. It is safe, well-tolerated, and evidence-based (Aberdeen Menorrhagia Trial).

Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) is not a menstrual disorder, but it is the single most important differential diagnosis in a woman presenting with vaginal bleeding after 12 months of amenorrhoea. PMB must be investigated urgently to exclude endometrial cancer (which accounts for 10% of all PMB). NICE NG12: refer any woman with PMB on 2-week-wait pathway. TVS first (endometrial thickness >4 mm in a postmenopausal woman → endometrial biopsy). Never dismiss PMB as "normal."

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 38-year-old, HMB with flooding + clots + 8–10 pads/day. Family complete. Hb 9.2 g/dL. Multiple intramural fibroids (largest 4.2 cm). No structural endometrial pathology on USS.

**Step 1 — Exclude endometrial pathology:**
- TVS shows intramural fibroids only, no suspicious endometrial thickening
- No intermenstrual or post-coital bleeding
- Age 38: endometrial biopsy not mandatory if TVS normal (consider if PCOS/obesity risk factors)

**Step 2 — Treat iron-deficiency anaemia:**
- Hb 9.2 g/dL + MCV 71 fL = moderate iron deficiency from blood loss
- **IV iron** (ferric carboxymaltose or iron sucrose) — faster repletion than oral iron (2–4 weeks vs. 3 months); appropriate pre-operatively
- Oral ferrous sulphate 200 mg BD if IV not available

**Step 3 — Discuss treatment options (patient completing her family):**

Options in order of escalation:
1. **LNG-IUS (Mirena):** 80–90% reduction in MBL; preserves fertility option (she has completed family but preserves uterus); good first medical choice even with fibroids ≤3 cm — her fibroids are 4.2 cm intramural, so effectiveness may be reduced; worth trying
2. **Tranexamic acid** during menstruation: reduces MBL ~50%; non-hormonal; can be combined with LNG-IUS
3. **GnRH agonist + add-back therapy** (6 months): shrinks fibroids, corrects anaemia pre-surgery
4. **Uterine artery embolisation:** Suitable (no desire for pregnancy); 80–85% success; avoids surgery; discuss risk of premature menopause (~2%) from inadvertent ovarian embolisation
5. **Laparoscopic myomectomy:** Preserves uterus; fibroids likely to recur
6. **Hysterectomy:** Definitive; given completed family + failed/declining less invasive options + severity of symptoms → appropriate to offer; total laparoscopic hysterectomy preferred (fastest recovery)

**Step 4 — Shared decision-making:**
- Patient's preference matters most — discuss the full spectrum
- She has completed family and is severely symptomatic → many women in this situation prefer definitive treatment (hysterectomy) over multiple attempts at medical/UAE with uncertain outcome
- If she chooses hysterectomy: pre-operative GnRH agonist (3 months) to shrink fibroids and correct anaemia

**Exam trigger:** *38-year-old + HMB + uterine fibroids + Hb 9.2 g/dL + family complete* → investigate (TVS done, endometrial biopsy if risk factors), treat anaemia (IV iron), offer LNG-IUS first-line or surgical options given fibroid size. The distractor is offering hysterectomy as the first step — NICE guidance requires that medical/less invasive options are offered first and the patient's preferences explored.
