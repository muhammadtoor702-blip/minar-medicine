# Chronic Kidney Disease

## Clinical Scenario

A 61-year-old woman with 14 years of type 2 diabetes and hypertension attends for routine review. She has no urinary symptoms and feels reasonably well, though she has noticed increasing fatigue. Her eGFR has fallen progressively: 52 mL/min/1.73m² two years ago, 41 last year, and 31 today. Urine ACR is 84 mg/mmol. BP is 156/94 mmHg. Hb 9.4 g/dL, bicarbonate 18 mmol/L, phosphate 1.9 mmol/L. She is currently on metformin, ramipril 10mg, and amlodipine.

## Overview

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is defined as abnormalities of kidney structure or function, present for more than three months, with implications for health. It is classified by both GFR (G1–G5) and albuminuria (A1–A3), and staged accordingly using the CGA system. CKD affects approximately 10% of the global population, is largely asymptomatic until advanced stages, and carries a disproportionately high cardiovascular mortality — patients with CKD are far more likely to die of cardiovascular disease than to reach end-stage renal disease (ESRD).

## Pathophysiology

Regardless of the initial insult, CKD progresses through a common final pathway: nephron loss triggers compensatory hyperfiltration in remaining nephrons. This increased glomerular pressure and flow initially preserves overall GFR, but over time accelerates glomerular injury, fibrosis, and further nephron loss — a self-perpetuating cycle. Each lost nephron increases the burden on the survivors.

**Consequences of progressive nephron loss:**

As GFR falls, the kidney's capacity to regulate fluid, electrolytes, acid-base balance, and endocrine function progressively deteriorates:

- **Sodium and water retention** → hypertension and oedema
- **Reduced erythropoietin synthesis** → normocytic normochromic anaemia
- **Reduced 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D activation** → hypocalcaemia → secondary hyperparathyroidism → bone resorption + further phosphate release → vascular calcification (CKD-mineral bone disorder, CKD-MBD)
- **Reduced acid excretion** → metabolic acidosis
- **Reduced potassium excretion** → hyperkalaemia (particularly problematic with ACEi/ARB)
- **Uraemia** (late, GFR <10–15) → encephalopathy, pericarditis, platelet dysfunction, pruritus

**Most common causes of CKD:**

| Cause | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| Diabetic nephropathy | Most common cause of ESRD worldwide |
| Hypertensive nephrosclerosis | Second most common in developed world |
| Glomerulonephritis | Biopsy required to define subtype |
| Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) | Most common inherited kidney disease; large kidneys on imaging |
| Renovascular disease | Atherosclerotic or fibromuscular dysplasia |
| Recurrent pyelonephritis / reflux nephropathy | |

## Clinical Presentation

CKD is characteristically **silent** until the GFR falls below 25–30 mL/min/1.73m². Symptoms, when they appear, are non-specific and often attributed to other conditions.

**By GFR stage:**

| Stage | GFR (mL/min/1.73m²) | Typical Features |
|-------|---------------------|-----------------|
| G1 | ≥90 (with markers of damage) | Asymptomatic; abnormal ACR or structural disease |
| G2 | 60–89 | Asymptomatic; mild GFR reduction |
| G3a/3b | 30–59 | Fatigue, anaemia, early metabolic changes |
| G4 | 15–29 | Uraemic symptoms begin; metabolic complications |
| G5 | <15 | ESRD — uraemia, fluid overload, requires RRT |

**Uraemic features (G4–G5):** Fatigue, nausea, anorexia, cognitive slowing, peripheral neuropathy (restless legs), pruritus, pericardial friction rub (uraemic pericarditis — indicates urgent dialysis), prolonged bleeding (platelet dysfunction), musculoskeletal pain (renal osteodystrophy).

## Diagnosis

**Establish the stage and cause:**

- eGFR (CKD-EPI equation) + urine ACR — the two key measurements. Both must be abnormal for at least three months to diagnose CKD. Single abnormal readings may reflect acute processes.
- Renal ultrasound — all patients with CKD. Assesses size (bilateral small kidneys = CKD; normal/large = DM, ADPKD, amyloid), cortical thickness, echogenicity, hydronephrosis, cysts.
- Urine microscopy — RBC/WBC casts suggest active glomerulonephritis; initiate urgent nephrology referral.
- Renal biopsy — if cause is unclear and histology would change management (suspected GN, vasculitis, myeloma).

**Monitoring bloods:** U&E (including bicarbonate), eGFR, calcium, phosphate, PTH, vitamin D, FBC, iron studies, LFTs, lipids, HbA1c, uric acid.

**Frequency of monitoring:** Determined by CKD stage and rate of progression. G3 with stable disease: every 6–12 months. G4: every 3–6 months. G5: every 1–3 months.

## Management

### Slowing Progression

**1. Blood pressure control**

Target <130/80 mmHg in CKD. ACEi or ARB are first-line in any patient with proteinuria regardless of ethnicity — they reduce intraglomerular pressure and independently slow CKD progression beyond their antihypertensive effect. A rise in creatinine of up to 30% within two weeks of starting is expected and acceptable — do not stop. Stop only if creatinine rises >30% or hyperkalaemia develops.

**2. SGLT2 inhibitors — a paradigm shift**

Dapagliflozin and empagliflozin are now recommended for CKD with eGFR ≥20 and ACR >22 mg/mmol, regardless of whether the patient has diabetes. The DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY trials demonstrated significant reductions in progression to ESRD and cardiovascular events. These are now first-line alongside ACEi/ARB in appropriate patients.

**3. Glycaemic control (in diabetic CKD)**

Target HbA1c ~53 mmol/mol, but individualise — tighter control increases hypoglycaemia risk as renal clearance of insulin and hypoglycaemic drugs falls. Stop metformin when eGFR <30 mL/min.

**4. Finerenone**

A non-steroidal MRA. The FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD trials showed reduced CKD progression and cardiovascular events in diabetic nephropathy. Now recommended as add-on therapy in diabetic CKD with residual albuminuria despite ACEi/ARB.

**5. Dietary modification**

Protein restriction to 0.8 g/kg/day in stages G3–G4 reduces uraemic load and may slow progression. Low potassium diet when eGFR <30. Low phosphate diet and phosphate binder education as GFR falls.

### Managing Complications

**Anaemia of CKD:**

The mechanism is reduced erythropoietin production. Before attributing anaemia to CKD, exclude iron deficiency, B12/folate deficiency, and haemolysis.

- IV iron is preferred over oral in CKD — better absorbed and tolerated (oral iron is poorly absorbed due to hepcidin upregulation and GI intolerance)
- Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs — darbepoetin alfa, epoetin) when Hb <100 g/dL after iron is repleted. Target Hb 100–120 g/dL. Avoid exceeding 130 g/dL — associated with increased cardiovascular events (TREAT trial).

**CKD-Mineral Bone Disorder (CKD-MBD):**

- Phosphate binders — sevelamer (non-calcium-containing, also reduces vascular calcification), calcium carbonate (use cautiously in patients with vascular calcification)
- Active vitamin D analogues — alfacalcidol or calcitriol (regular cholecalciferol is not activated without the kidney's 1-alpha-hydroxylase)
- Calcimimetics — cinacalcet for secondary hyperparathyroidism not controlled by above measures

**Metabolic acidosis:**

Sodium bicarbonate supplementation when serum bicarbonate <22 mmol/L. Correcting acidosis slows muscle wasting and may independently slow CKD progression.

**Hyperkalaemia:**

Dietary potassium restriction. Stop nephrotoxins. Patiromer or sodium zirconium cyclosilicate for persistent hyperkalaemia — novel potassium binders that allow continuation of ACEi/ARB even in patients prone to hyperkalaemia.

### Preparation for Renal Replacement Therapy

Refer to nephrology when eGFR <30 (NICE guidance). This allows time for education, counselling, modality choice, and vascular access creation (AV fistula ideally 3–6 months before anticipated dialysis start).

**RRT options:**

| Modality | Notes |
|---------|-------|
| Haemodialysis | 3× weekly, 4 hours. Requires AV fistula (gold standard) or tunnelled line. |
| Peritoneal dialysis | Daily, at home. CAPD or automated (APD). Preserves residual renal function longer. |
| Kidney transplantation | Best outcomes. Pre-emptive transplant (before starting dialysis) is ideal. Living donor > deceased donor. |

## Complications

- Cardiovascular disease — the leading cause of death in CKD; risk is disproportionate even at moderate CKD stages
- Anaemia — fatigue, reduced quality of life, LVH
- Renal osteodystrophy — fractures, bone pain, vascular calcification
- Peripheral neuropathy, encephalopathy, restless leg syndrome
- Uraemic pericarditis — indication for urgent dialysis
- Increased infection risk — impaired cellular and humoral immunity

## Clinical Insight

The trajectory of eGFR matters as much as the absolute value. An eGFR of 35 that has been stable for three years is very different from an eGFR of 35 that was 52 eighteen months ago. Plot the rate of decline on a graph. Extrapolating the slope predicts when ESRD will occur and when to refer for transplant assessment — anticipating the end point avoids the common disaster of a patient dialysing through a neck line because their fistula was created too late.

The leading cause of death in CKD is cardiovascular disease, not renal failure. An eGFR of 45 confers the same cardiovascular risk as established diabetes. These patients need aggressive cardiovascular risk factor management — statins, blood pressure control, smoking cessation — as urgently as they need nephroprotective treatment.

SGLT2 inhibitors protect the kidney through a mechanism largely independent of glucose lowering — reduced intraglomerular pressure from afferent arteriolar constriction, anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic effects. They work in non-diabetic CKD and should be used in all eligible patients regardless of glycaemic status.
