## Overview

Emergency airway management is the most critical and unforgiving skill in acute medicine. Unlike elective airway management in the operating theatre — where conditions are optimised, the patient is fasted, and the anaesthetist has time to plan — the emergency airway presents in the worst possible circumstances: a sick, vomiting, hypoxic patient with an unpredicted anatomy, no time for full preparation, and the certainty that failure to act rapidly will cause death. Rapid sequence induction (RSI) is the cornerstone technique, but the airway physician must simultaneously have a clear escalation plan for when RSI fails, because failed airway management remains a preventable cause of death. The principle is: predict the difficulty, prepare for failure, and never be surprised by the impossible airway.

## Pathophysiology

### Physiology of Airway Loss

**Hypoxaemia and apnoeic oxygenation:** After cessation of breathing (apnoea), oxygen saturation falls at a rate determined by the patient's physiological reserve:
- Healthy adult after 3 minutes of preoxygenation at 100% FiO₂: SpO₂ remains >90% for ~8 minutes of apnoea
- Obese patient (reduced FRC): SpO₂ falls to <90% within 2–3 minutes
- Critically ill or paediatric patient: <1–2 minutes
- **Apnoeic oxygenation** (high-flow nasal O₂ at 15 L/min during laryngoscopy): mass flow of O₂ into the airway as CO₂ is not excreted, maintaining some alveolar PO₂ and extending safe apnoea time by 2–3 minutes. Now standard practice in RSI.

**Functional residual capacity (FRC):** The lung volume at end-expiration that acts as an oxygen reservoir during apnoea. FRC is reduced by obesity (abdominal mass pushes diaphragm upward), pregnancy, ascites, and supine positioning. Head-up positioning (20–30° head tilt = **ramping position** in obese patients) improves FRC and improves laryngoscopic view.

### Why RSI Rather Than Awake Intubation in Emergencies

In elective difficult airway management, awake fibreoptic intubation is the technique of choice — the patient maintains airway reflexes, breathing, and oxygenation throughout. In the emergency setting, time pressure, patient non-cooperation, airway haemorrhage, vomiting, and altered consciousness make awake intubation frequently impossible. RSI accepts the risk of apnoea during intubation in exchange for speed, control, and aspiration protection (cricoid pressure + rapid intubating conditions from neuromuscular blockade). The trade-off is that if intubation fails during RSI, the paralysed apnoeic patient deteriorates rapidly.

## Clinical Presentation — Airway Assessment

### LEMON Mnemonic

Pre-RSI airway assessment using **LEMON**:

**L — Look externally:**
- Facial trauma, oropharyngeal blood/secretions
- Short/bull neck (limited extension)
- Facial or neck burns (early angioedema)
- Beard/moustache (mask seal difficulty)
- Micrognathia, retrognathia, protruding teeth

**E — Evaluate the 3-3-2 rule:**
- 3 fingers: inter-incisor gap ≥3 finger-widths (mouth opening)
- 3 fingers: hyoid-mentum distance ≥3 finger-widths (sublingual space for laryngoscopy)
- 2 fingers: hyoid-thyroid notch ≥2 finger-widths (supraglottic space)
- Any measurement <3/3/2 = potential difficult laryngoscopy

**M — Mallampati score (if cooperative):**
- Class I: soft palate, fauces, uvula, pillars visible — easy
- Class II: soft palate, fauces, uvula
- Class III: soft palate, base of uvula — difficult
- Class IV: only hard palate visible — very difficult

**O — Obstruction:**
- Supraglottic mass (epiglottitis, abscess, tumour)
- Foreign body
- Airway haemorrhage
- Any cause of distorted anatomy

**N — Neck mobility:**
- C-spine immobilisation (hard collar) reduces mouth opening and neck extension — significantly worsens laryngoscopic view
- Atlanto-occipital fusion, cervical spondylosis, ankylosing spondylitis

**Predicted difficult airway:** LEMON score ≥2 positive features = high index for difficulty; prepare for video laryngoscopy, surgical airway, and have experienced backup present.

### Can't Intubate, Can't Oxygenate (CICO) — Recognition

CICO is the most feared emergency in airway management: failure to intubate AND failure to oxygenate with a bag-mask or supraglottic airway. This is an immediately life-threatening situation requiring emergency surgical airway.

Features that predict CICO:
- No neck (short, bull neck, prior neck surgery/irradiation)
- Massive facial/laryngeal trauma
- Complete upper airway obstruction (angioedema, foreign body)
- Morbid obesity
- Burns/inhalation injury with oropharyngeal oedema

## Diagnosis — Confirming Intubation

**Waveform capnography: the gold standard for ETT confirmation.** A consistent waveform on at least 6 breath cycles confirms tracheal placement with >99.9% sensitivity. A waveform that disappears after the first 1–2 breaths = oesophageal intubation (CO₂ is washed out of the stomach).

**Do NOT rely on:**
- Chest rise (can be minimal with oesophageal intubation)
- "Misting" in the ETT tube (unreliable)
- Auscultation alone (epigastric gurgling on intubation attempts should prompt immediate reassessment, but bilateral breath sounds are not definitive)
- The sensation of "passing through the cords" (unreliable even for experienced operators)

**Ultrasound confirmation:** Tracheal intubation shows a single hyperechoic shadow (air in trachea with acoustic shadowing) in the midline; oesophageal intubation shows a "double tract sign" (tracheal shadow + adjacent oesophageal shadow). Useful when capnography trace is poor (very low cardiac output states).

## Management

### Rapid Sequence Induction (RSI)

RSI is the simultaneous administration of an induction agent and a neuromuscular blocking agent to achieve rapid loss of consciousness and complete muscle relaxation for intubation, minimising the time from loss of consciousness to intubation.

**RSI sequence — the 7 Ps:**

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **Preparation** | Equipment: ETT (6.0 cuffed at minimum, have 5.5 and 6.5 ready), video laryngoscope, backup SAD, surgical airway kit on trolley; drugs drawn up; IV access confirmed; suction at bedside; team briefing and CICO plan stated |
| **Preoxygenation** | 100% O₂ via tight-fitting NRB mask for minimum 3 minutes (or 8 vital capacity breaths if urgent); high-flow nasal O₂ at 15 L/min during apnoea phase (apnoeic oxygenation) |
| **Pre-treatment** | Fentanyl 3 mcg/kg IV (attenuates sympathetic response to laryngoscopy — reduces ICP spike and reduces hypertension); **not universal** |
| **Paralysis and induction** | Induction agent + NMB simultaneously (or NMB 30 sec after induction agent for defasciculation with suxamethonium) |
| **Position** | Sniffing position (head extended, neck flexed); **ramp position** for obese (head elevated 20–30°, external ear canal level with sternal notch) |
| **Placement** | Laryngoscopy + intubation; if video laryngoscope, use as first line for all emergency intubations |
| **Proof** | Waveform capnography confirms tracheal placement; inflate cuff; secure ETT; note depth (typically 21–23 cm at lips) |

**Cricoid pressure (Sellick's manoeuvre):** Sustained downward pressure on the cricoid ring (30 N force) to compress the oesophagus against the vertebral body, reducing passive regurgitation during the apnoea phase. **Controversial:** The CODA trial showed cricoid pressure does not reduce aspiration risk and may worsen laryngoscopic view. Current practice: apply cricoid pressure routinely but release if it worsens laryngoscopy.

### Induction Agents

| Agent | Dose | Key Properties | Use in |
|-------|------|----------------|--------|
| **Ketamine** | 1–2 mg/kg IV | Dissociative; maintains BP + airway reflexes; bronchodilator; analgesic; increases ICP (avoided in isolated TBI); emergence reactions | Haemodynamic compromise, bronchospasm, pain |
| **Propofol** | 1.5–2 mg/kg IV | Rapid onset; reduces ICP; causes hypotension (vasodilation + negative inotropy); reduces cerebral metabolic demand | Normovolaemic patients, TBI without haemodynamic compromise |
| **Etomidate** | 0.3 mg/kg IV | Most haemodynamically stable; minimal cardiovascular effects; suppresses adrenal cortisol synthesis (single dose clinically insignificant but avoid in sepsis/adrenal insufficiency) | Cardiac disease, haemodynamically compromised; controversial in sepsis |
| **Thiopental** | 3–5 mg/kg IV | Rapid; reduces ICP; severe hypotension; falling out of use | Historically for RSI; largely replaced by propofol/etomidate |
| **Midazolam** | 0.1 mg/kg IV | Slow onset (not ideal for RSI); amnesia; respiratory depression | Sedation; combination with ketamine |

### Neuromuscular Blocking Agents

**Suxamethonium (succinylcholine) — depolarising NMB:**
- Dose: **1.5 mg/kg IV** (1–2 mg/kg)
- Onset: 45–60 seconds; duration: 10–12 minutes (short — expires quickly, allowing return of spontaneous breathing if intubation fails)
- Mechanism: binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at the neuromuscular junction → transient depolarisation (fasciculations) → paralysis
- **Contraindications:** Hyperkalaemia or risk of dangerous hyperkalaemia: burns (>24h post-burn, increased receptor upregulation → K+ surge), denervation injuries/spinal cord injury (>5 days), rhabdomyolysis, prolonged immobilisation, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, malignant hyperthermia susceptibility
- Suxamethonium causes transient K+ rise of 0.5–1 mEq/L (dangerous K+ surge in denervated muscle — risk of VF)

**Rocuronium — non-depolarising NMB:**
- High-dose: **1.2 mg/kg IV** — achieves intubating conditions at 60 seconds (equivalent to suxamethonium)
- Duration: 60–90 minutes
- Reversible with **sugammadex 16 mg/kg IV** (chelates rocuronium; reversal within 3 minutes — the CICO escape route if intubation fails after high-dose rocuronium)
- Preferred when suxamethonium is contraindicated

### Supraglottic Airways (SADs)

When face-mask ventilation is inadequate and intubation has failed, a supraglottic airway buys critical time:

**i-gel:** Gel-cuffed supraglottic device; easy insertion; has a dedicated **gastric drainage channel** (can decompress the stomach and reduce regurgitation risk — advantage over classic LMA). The current default SAD in UK emergency medicine.

**LMA ProSeal / Supreme:** Second-generation SADs with gastric drainage; better seal than classic LMA.

**Limitations of SADs:** No definitive airway protection; aspiration risk remains; cannot be used in complete upper airway obstruction; maximum ventilation pressures ~25–30 cmH₂O.

### Failed Airway — CICO Algorithm

When the patient cannot be intubated AND cannot be oxygenated adequately with bag-mask or SAD — this is CICO. **Scalpel-finger-bougie emergency front-of-neck airway (eFONA):**

1. **Call for help** (senior anaesthetist, ENT surgeon)
2. **Neck extension** (remove cervical collar if one is in place — C-spine concerns are irrelevant in CICO; the patient will die without an airway)
3. **Identify the cricothyroid membrane (CTM):** Midline, inferior to the thyroid cartilage notch, superior to the cricoid cartilage. In obese patients: run fingers down the midline from the thyroid notch — the CTM is the soft gap between the thyroid and cricoid cartilage.
4. **Stabilise the larynx** with the non-dominant hand (laryngeal handshake: thumb and middle finger grip the thyroid cartilage wings)
5. **Make a horizontal stab incision** through the skin and CTM with a size 10 scalpel
6. **Insert a finger** through the incision to confirm entry into the airway
7. **Insert a bougie** through the incision alongside the finger; angle caudally
8. **Railroad a cuffed ETT (6.0)** over the bougie into the trachea
9. Inflate cuff, ventilate, confirm capnography

**Commercial kits** (Portex, Melker): needle-guided or surgical cricothyrotomy kits — practice with these before encountering CICO in a real patient.

**Tracheostomy:** Definitive surgical airway for longer-term ventilation. Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (Ciaglia technique) performed in ICU under bronchoscopic guidance: needle puncture at 2nd–3rd tracheal ring → guidewire → serial dilatation → tracheostomy tube. Surgical tracheostomy: open technique, used for emergency or when anatomy is distorted.

### Video Laryngoscopy

**Video laryngoscopy (VL) as first-line for emergency intubation:**
- Devices: C-MAC, McGrath, King Vision, GlideScope
- Hyperangulated blades (GlideScope, McGrath): dramatically improved view of the glottis in anteriorly placed or difficult larynxes; however, improved view does NOT always translate to easier tube passage (requires use of rigid stylet bent to match the blade angle)
- **C-MAC or equivalent as default for all emergency RSI intubations:** The view is better, the intubation failure rate is lower, and there is no disadvantage to using VL in straightforward airways. Multiple RCTs now support VL as first-line in emergency settings.
- Limitation: Blood, secretions, and fogging can obscure the camera lens — **suction and meticulous preparation** before any laryngoscopy attempt.

## Complications

- **Failed intubation:** Oesophageal intubation (if unrecognised = catastrophic hypoxia); multiple attempts cause airway trauma and swelling (worsens subsequent attempts)
- **Aspiration:** Despite RSI, aspiration of gastric contents occurs in ~1 in 800 RSIs in the ED (Neilipovitz); causes aspiration pneumonitis/pneumonia
- **Post-intubation hypotension:** Induction agents + positive pressure ventilation + pre-existing hypovolaemia → fall in cardiac output; prevent with pre-RSI fluid bolus (cautious), vasopressor infusion (noradrenaline) initiated at RSI
- **Accidental extubation:** Tube displacement during movement/procedures; always confirm tube depth and secure carefully
- **Subglottic/tracheal stenosis:** Complication of prolonged intubation or tracheostomy (high-pressure cuffs); presents weeks/months later with stridor
- **Tracheostomy complications:** Haemorrhage (immediate), tube dislodgement, tracheo-innominate fistula (catastrophic delayed haemorrhage 3–14 days post-insertion from pulsatile erosion of the innominate artery)

## Clinical Insight

The surgical airway hesitation is the most dangerous cognitive failure in airway management. In CICO, every second of delay in proceeding to cricothyrotomy is a second of progressive hypoxia. The barriers are psychological: performing a surgical procedure on a patient who was alive 2 minutes ago, using a technique that is rarely practised in real life. The solution is cognitive — recognise CICO early (after two failed intubation attempts + failed SAD), call it out explicitly ("This is a CICO situation"), and proceed to the front-of-neck airway immediately. Simulation training for CICO scenarios has been shown to reduce time to surgical airway in real incidents.

The ramping position for obese patients is dramatically underused. A BMI 42 patient lying flat has the worst possible laryngoscopic view — the tongue, submandibular tissue, and airway all override the line of sight. Ramping (right-angle flex of the bed so the external auditory canal is level with the sternal notch) aligns the oral, pharyngeal, and laryngeal axes and can transform a CL grade III view into a grade I. This takes 30 seconds to set up and costs nothing. It is standard practice in bariatric anaesthesia and should be standard in emergency airway management of obese patients.

The ketamine versus propofol debate in emergency RSI: ketamine preserves blood pressure through sympathomimetic catecholamine release — this is why it is preferred in haemodynamically unstable patients. But in patients with severe hypertension, intracranial hypertension (isolated TBI), or cardiac ischaemia, the catecholamine surge from ketamine may be harmful. Etomidate offers the most cardiovascular stability without sympathomimetic effects — it is the drug of choice for shocked patients in most UK and North American centres. The controversy about etomidate in sepsis (adrenal suppression) has not translated to a meaningful clinical outcome difference in single-dose administration.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** BMI 42, GCS 7, vomiting, maxillofacial trauma with mandibular fractures + intraoral haemorrhage. SpO₂ 88%, RR 28. Cervical collar in situ.

**Step 1 — Predict the difficulty (LEMON):**
- L: Massive facial trauma, intraoral haemorrhage, morbid obesity
- E: Mandibular fractures → likely reduced inter-incisor gap (<3 fingers); sublingual haematoma possible
- M: Cannot assess (GCS 7)
- O: Active oropharyngeal haemorrhage — blood will flood the view at laryngoscopy
- N: Hard collar → reduced neck extension

**Assessment:** This is a predicted VERY DIFFICULT airway. CICO is a realistic possibility. **Assemble the full difficult airway team now** (most experienced airway physician available + backup + ENT surgeon if available). Prepare: video laryngoscope (hyperangulated C-MAC), i-gel (backup SAD), cricothyrotomy kit at bedside — opened and ready.

**Step 2 — Preoxygenation:**
- SpO₂ 88% on 15L NRB = cannot wait; already hypoxic
- Apply a bag-mask with 15 L O₂ + assistance (2-person technique)
- Simultaneous high-flow nasal cannula at 15 L/min for apnoeic oxygenation
- Target: SpO₂ ≥94% before induction (may not be achievable — proceed when above 90% if rising)

**Step 3 — Clear the airway:**
- Suction the oropharynx aggressively (Yankauer suction) to remove blood/vomit before laryngoscopy — the single most important preparatory step in haemorrhage airway management

**Step 4 — RSI drug selection:**
- **Ketamine 1.5 mg/kg IV** (haemodynamically safe in trauma; maintains airway reflexes until NMB; bronchodilator)
- **Rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg IV** (preferred over suxamethonium — no fasciculations, contraindicated in major burns and denervation injuries; and sugammadex 16 mg/kg is available for immediate reversal if CICO)
- Remove cervical collar immediately before laryngoscopy (manual in-line stabilisation maintained by an assistant)

**Step 5 — Intubation:**
- Video laryngoscope (C-MAC, hyperangulated blade) — first attempt
- If view obscured by blood: suction, reposition (ramp), adjust blade
- If first attempt fails: i-gel insertion + optimise ventilation + reassess
- If i-gel fails or SpO₂ falling rapidly → **CICO declared → proceed immediately to scalpel-finger-bougie cricothyrotomy**

**Step 6 — Post-intubation:**
- Confirm waveform capnography
- Secure ETT (tie + bite block)
- CT trauma when haemodynamically stable to define full injury extent
- Continue haemodynamic resuscitation (damage control principles)

**Exam trigger:** *Obese patient + maxillofacial trauma + intraoral haemorrhage + GCS 7 + SpO₂ 88%* → predicted difficult airway requiring experienced team + video laryngoscope as first-line + surgical airway kit at bedside before induction. The distractor is proceeding with standard direct laryngoscopy without preparation for failure — in this patient, that pathway leads to CICO and death.
