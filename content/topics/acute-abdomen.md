## Overview

The acute abdomen encompasses any condition causing sudden-onset abdominal pain of sufficient severity to warrant urgent surgical evaluation. The clinical challenge is that the same presentation — abdominal pain, guarding, tenderness — can arise from life-threatening surgical emergencies (perforated viscus, mesenteric ischaemia), urgent but less immediately dangerous conditions (acute appendicitis, bowel obstruction), and medical conditions that mimic surgical disease (diabetic ketoacidosis, lower lobe pneumonia, Addisonian crisis). The surgeon's skill is pattern recognition: which combination of history, examination, and investigation indicates that an organ is about to perforate, that bowel is dying, or that waiting is safe. Getting this wrong — operating on a medical abdomen or deferring surgery on a surgical one — carries mortality on both sides.

## Pathophysiology

### Peritoneal Pain Mechanisms

**Visceral pain** (early): transmitted by autonomic C fibres from the visceral peritoneum. Poorly localised, crampy or colicky, often midline. The site corresponds to the embryological origin of the organ: foregut (stomach to mid-duodenum) = epigastric; midgut (mid-duodenum to 2/3 transverse colon) = periumbilical; hindgut (distal transverse colon to rectum) = hypogastric.

**Somatic/parietal pain** (late): once inflammation reaches the parietal peritoneum (which is innervated by somatic nerves), pain becomes sharp, localised, and accompanied by guarding and rebound. Migration of pain from umbilical to RIF in appendicitis reflects this transition — visceral pain (periumbilical, colicky) as the appendix becomes inflamed → somatic pain (localised RIF, constant) once parietal peritoneum is involved.

**Referred pain:** Diaphragmatic irritation (blood, pus, air under the diaphragm) → referred shoulder-tip pain (C3–C5 dermatome via phrenic nerve).

### Appendicitis

The appendix is a blind-ending pouch of the caecum. Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen (faecolith most common, lymphoid hyperplasia in children, rarely a tumour) → bacterial overgrowth within the closed lumen → mucosal ischaemia from increased intraluminal pressure → transmural inflammation → risk of perforation (within 24–72 hours if untreated). Once the appendix perforates, faecal material and bacteria contaminate the peritoneal cavity → peritonitis. If perforation is walled off by omentum → appendix mass/abscess.

## Clinical Presentation

### Appendicitis

**Classical presentation** (present in ~70% of cases):
1. Central/periumbilical pain, colicky initially → migrates to RIF over 6–24 hours (somatic localisation)
2. Anorexia (nearly universal — a patient who is genuinely hungry does not have appendicitis)
3. Nausea, vomiting (usually 1–2 episodes, not severe)
4. Low-grade fever (37.5–38.5°C) — high fever with rigors suggests perforation or another diagnosis

**Clinical signs:**
- **McBurney's point** tenderness: 1/3 along the line from the right ASIS to the umbilicus — the surface marking of the appendix
- **Rovsing's sign**: palpation of the LIF causes pain in the RIF — palpating LIF pushes gas and bowel contents right, stretching an inflamed appendix
- **Psoas sign**: pain on extension of the right hip with the knee extended — indicates a retrocaecal appendix lying on the psoas muscle
- **Obturator sign**: pain on internal rotation of the right hip (flexed to 90°) — indicates a pelvic appendix lying near the obturator internus
- **Dunphy's sign**: increased RIF pain with coughing — parietal peritoneal involvement

**Atypical presentations:**
- **Retrocaecal appendix** (65%): may not have classical RIF tenderness; psoas sign positive; pain may be more in the flank
- **Pelvic appendix**: pain may be suprapubic; urinary symptoms from bladder irritation; vaginal tenderness in women → easy confusion with PID
- **Elderly patients**: classical signs often absent; perforation rate higher (thin omentum less effective at walling off)
- **Pregnant women**: the appendix is displaced upward by the gravid uterus → RIF tenderness moves to the right flank

**Alvarado (MANTRELS) Score:**

| Feature | Points |
|---------|--------|
| Migration of pain to RIF | 1 |
| Anorexia | 1 |
| Nausea / Vomiting | 1 |
| RIF tenderness | 2 |
| Rebound tenderness | 1 |
| Elevated temperature | 1 |
| Leucocytosis | 2 |
| Total | /10 |

Score ≤4 = appendicitis unlikely; 5–6 = equivocal (observe + imaging); ≥7 = high probability, consider surgery.

### Bowel Obstruction

**Small bowel obstruction (SBO):** Causes — adhesions (60% in developed world, prior laparotomy), hernia (20%), tumour, Crohn's stricture, volvulus, intussusception. Presentation: central colicky pain, vomiting (early and prominent — bilious if mid-small bowel obstruction), abdominal distension (more marked in distal SBO), absolute constipation (late sign when distal bowel empties).

**Large bowel obstruction (LBO):** Causes — colorectal cancer (most common), sigmoid volvulus, diverticular stricture, faecal impaction. Presentation: progressive distension, late onset constipation, vomiting late (faeculent if prolonged), abdominal pain (crampy, less severe than SBO).

**Caecal distension:** If the ileocaecal valve is competent, a closed-loop LBO (no decompression retrograde into small bowel) distends the caecum — risk of caecal perforation when diameter exceeds 12 cm (empirical threshold).

### Mesenteric Ischaemia

The most dangerous cause of acute abdomen and the most difficult to diagnose early. SMA embolism (50% — often from AF), SMA thrombosis (25%), mesenteric vein thrombosis (10%), non-occlusive (low-flow states). Classical presentation: **sudden-onset severe periumbilical pain out of proportion to clinical signs** in a patient with AF (embolic cause). In the early phase (before gut infarction), the abdomen is soft — the disparity between the severity of the pain and the lack of peritonism is the diagnostic clue. As infarction progresses, the abdomen becomes peritonitic and the patient deteriorates rapidly. Mortality exceeds 50% once infarction has occurred.

## Diagnosis

**Bloods:** FBC (leucocytosis — WBC >10 × 10⁹/L with neutrophilia), CRP (rises 12–24h after onset), lactate (elevated in mesenteric ischaemia and peritonitis), amylase/lipase (pancreatitis), LFTs (hepatobiliary), group and save.

**Urine:** Dipstick + beta-hCG in all women of reproductive age (exclude ectopic pregnancy — the most dangerous differential in young women with lower abdominal pain).

**Erect CXR:** Free gas under the right hemidiaphragm = hollow viscus perforation (present in ~80% of perforated DU, less common with colonic perforation). Also: lower lobe pneumonia masquerading as acute abdomen.

**AXR:** Small bowel gas (>3 cm diameter, central position, valvulae conniventes) vs large bowel gas (haustral markings, peripheral position). Multiple air-fluid levels = obstruction. Sigmoid volvulus: **coffee bean/omega loop sign** (massively dilated sigmoid loop). No peritoneal stripes or psoas shadow = ascites or blood.

**Ultrasound:** First-line for young women (exclude ovarian cyst, ectopic) and children (appendicitis in paediatrics). Non-compressible appendix >6 mm diameter with periappendiceal fat stranding = appendicitis.

**CT abdomen/pelvis with IV and oral contrast:** Gold standard for acute abdomen — sensitivity 95%, specificity 95% for appendicitis and bowel obstruction; identifies the transition point in SBO (transition from dilated to decompressed bowel = site of obstruction); free air; collections; diverticular disease. **CT angiography** for suspected mesenteric ischaemia — occlusion of SMA or SMV.

## Management

### Acute Appendicitis

**Non-perforated appendicitis:** Laparoscopic appendicectomy (preferred over open — faster recovery, fewer wound infections, lower incisional hernia rate, better visualisation in obese patients and women — allows exclusion of gynaecological pathology). Intraoperative antibiotics (co-amoxiclav or cefuroxime + metronidazole).

**Antibiotic-only approach (APPAC trial):** 73% of uncomplicated appendicitis (no perforation/abscess/free fluid on CT) resolved with IV antibiotics (ertapenem) alone; however, 27% required appendicectomy within 5 years. Offered as an alternative in select centres, but surgery remains the standard recommendation for reliable cure.

**Perforated appendicitis with localised abscess:** IV antibiotics (pip-tazo or co-amoxiclav + metronidazole) → interval appendicectomy at 6–8 weeks (allows inflammation to settle). Radiological drainage if abscess >4 cm.

**Generalised peritonitis from perforation:** Emergency laparoscopic or open appendicectomy + peritoneal washout.

### Bowel Obstruction

**Initial management (all cases):** IV access + fluid resuscitation (crystalloid), NG tube insertion + aspiration (decompresses stomach, reduces vomiting and aspiration risk), urinary catheter, nil by mouth, analgesia (does not mask signs when given appropriately — withholding analgesia in acute abdomen is not evidence-based).

**SBO — conservative management:** If no signs of strangulation (fever, continuous severe pain replacing colicky pain, peritonism, leucocytosis, lactate rise) → 24–72h trial of NG decompression + IV fluids; CT to identify transition point and cause.

**Indications for urgent surgery in SBO:** Signs of strangulation (ischaemic bowel), complete obstruction with closed loop, failure of conservative management after 48–72h, hernia as cause (risk of strangulation is high — reduce/repair urgently).

**Sigmoid volvulus:** Rigid sigmoidoscopy + flatus tube (90% success in decompression) — can be done in endoscopy suite. If fails or necrosis → sigmoid colectomy (Hartmann's procedure).

**Perforated viscus (peritonitis):** Resuscitate → IV antibiotics → laparotomy or laparoscopic repair. For perforated duodenal ulcer: Graham patch repair. For perforated sigmoid diverticulitis: Hartmann's procedure (sigmoid colectomy + end colostomy — safer than primary anastomosis in contaminated field in an unwell patient).

### Mesenteric Ischaemia

**Time is bowel.** Immediate heparinisation (prevents clot propagation). CT angiography to confirm and plan intervention. Emergency laparotomy with embolectomy (SMA embolism) or SMA bypass (thrombosis) + resection of infarcted bowel. **Second-look laparotomy at 24–48 hours** to reassess viability of bowel left at the margin of resection (bowel that looks dusky at first operation may be viable; bowel that clearly infarcts on second look is resected). Long-term anticoagulation for embolic cause.

## Complications

- **Perforation:** Generalised faecal peritonitis (sigmoid/appendix), pneumoperitoneum, septicaemia
- **Strangulation:** Bowel infarction, gangrene, perforation; highest risk with femoral hernias and closed-loop LBO
- **Mesenteric ischaemia:** Short bowel syndrome (extensive resection → malnutrition, TPN dependence), renal failure, multi-organ failure
- **Short bowel syndrome:** If >50% small bowel resected — malabsorption, TPN dependence, risk of D-lactic acidosis
- **Ogilvie's syndrome (acute colonic pseudo-obstruction):** Non-mechanical colonic dilation in hospitalised/post-surgical patients; caecal diameter >12 cm risks perforation; treat with neostigmine 2 mg IV (cholinergic — increases colonic motility), colonoscopic decompression if neostigmine fails

## Clinical Insight

Anorexia is the most reliable symptom of appendicitis — a patient who has eaten a full meal since the pain started almost certainly does not have acute appendicitis. Conversely, the migration of pain from periumbilical to RIF is not always present (30% of cases), and its absence should not be used to exclude the diagnosis. The CRP is equally important: normal CRP (<5 mg/L) in the first 12 hours of genuine appendicitis is possible — CRP has a 12–24 hour lag. A CRP of 80–100 mg/L with a leucocytosis and clinical signs = operate, do not defer for a repeat CRP.

Mesenteric ischaemia has one clinical hallmark that separates it from every other cause of acute abdomen: **pain out of proportion to physical signs**. The patient screams, writhes, looks desperately unwell — and you examine a soft, non-tender abdomen. This is because gut ischaemia causes visceral pain (C fibre, poorly localised, not producing peritonism) before the bowel infarcted and the parietal peritoneum becomes involved. The moment you examine a soft abdomen in a patient with excruciating pain and AF, think SMA embolism. CT angiography immediately. The window for successful revascularisation is narrow.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 22-year-old man, 18h of central abdominal pain migrated to RIF, anorexia, nausea, T 37.9°C, guarding + rebound at McBurney's point, Rovsing's positive, WBC 14.2 × 10⁹/L.

**Alvarado score:**
- Migration to RIF: 1 + Anorexia: 1 + Nausea: 1 + RIF tenderness: 2 + Rebound: 1 + Elevated temperature: 1 + Leucocytosis: 2 = **9/10** — high probability of appendicitis.

**Step 1 — Do not delay for CT in a classic presentation:** An Alvarado score of ≥7 with classical clinical features does not require CT before proceeding to surgery. CT is used when the diagnosis is uncertain (women of reproductive age where gynaecological pathology must be excluded, atypical presentations, very high or very low Alvarado score).

**Step 2 — Immediate management:**
- IV access, fluid resuscitation, analgesia (do not withhold — worsens patient experience without aiding diagnosis)
- NBM, IV antibiotics (co-amoxiclav or cefuroxime + metronidazole) perioperatively
- Group and save
- Consent and mark for laparoscopic appendicectomy

**Step 3 — Laparoscopic appendicectomy:**
- Trocar positions (umbilical 10mm, suprapubic 5mm, RIF 5mm)
- Identify appendix, dissect mesoappendix, ligate appendicular vessels, apply endoloop/staple at base
- Inspect rest of bowel and pelvis (exclude other pathology)
- Washout if local soiling

**Step 4 — Post-operative management:**
- Discharge day 1 post-op (laparoscopic: normal diet, mobilising)
- Histology of specimen (exclude carcinoid, mucocele, Crohn's — approximately 2% have an unexpected finding)
- Antibiotics: 24h perioperative course only for uncomplicated appendicitis; 5–7 days if perforation/abscess

**Exam trigger:** *Young man + central pain migrating to RIF + anorexia + low-grade fever + guarding at McBurney's point + leucocytosis* → acute appendicitis. Alvarado ≥7 = proceed to theatre. The distractor is sending for CT to "confirm" a classic clinical diagnosis — this delays surgery unnecessarily and the pre-test probability is already high enough to operate. CT is reserved for uncertain cases (women, atypical features, very young/elderly).
