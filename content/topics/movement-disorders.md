## Overview

Movement disorders are neurological conditions characterised by abnormal voluntary or involuntary movements. They are classified as hypokinetic (reduced movement — Parkinson's disease and Parkinsonism) or hyperkinetic (excessive, involuntary movement — tremor, chorea, dystonia, tics). Parkinson's disease is by far the most clinically significant, affecting 1–2% of people over 65 and representing the second most common neurodegenerative disease after Alzheimer's.

## Parkinson's Disease

### Pathophysiology

Parkinson's disease results from the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the **substantia nigra pars compacta**, which project via the nigrostriatal pathway to the striatum (putamen and caudate). Dopamine normally facilitates the **direct pathway** (promoting movement) and inhibits the **indirect pathway** (suppressing competing movements). Dopamine depletion tips this balance — the indirect pathway becomes overactive, increasing thalamic inhibition and reducing motor cortex output → bradykinesia and rigidity.

The pathological hallmark is **Lewy bodies** — intraneuronal inclusions of aggregated alpha-synuclein. Lewy body pathology follows a predictable caudal-to-rostral pattern (Braak staging), spreading from the lower brainstem and olfactory bulb to the substantia nigra and eventually the cortex. This explains why non-motor symptoms (anosmia, REM sleep behaviour disorder, constipation) often precede motor symptoms by years.

**Approximately 80% of dopaminergic neurons are lost before motor symptoms become apparent** — the brain compensates substantially before clinical threshold is crossed.

### Clinical Presentation

**The four cardinal motor features:**

1. **Tremor at rest** — pill-rolling, 4–6 Hz, typically unilateral at onset, improves with intentional movement (distinguishes from essential tremor which is action tremor), disappears during sleep. Often asymmetric throughout disease course.

2. **Rigidity** — increased resistance to passive limb movement throughout the range (lead-pipe rigidity), often with a superimposed ratchet quality (cogwheel rigidity = lead-pipe rigidity + tremor). Distinguished from spasticity, which is velocity-dependent.

3. **Bradykinesia** — slowness of movement, with progressive reduction in amplitude (decrement) on repetitive movements (finger taps, heel taps). Also affects facial expression (hypomimia — "mask face"), voice (hypophonia), blinking (reduced), and handwriting (micrographia).

4. **Postural instability** — develops later; impaired righting reflexes; positive pull test (patient takes >2 steps backward or falls when pulled by the examiner). Major cause of falls.

**Gait**: Shuffling, small steps, reduced arm swing, stooped posture, difficulty initiating (start hesitation), freezing of gait (sudden inability to step, particularly through doorways or at turns), festination (progressive acceleration of steps), en-bloc turning.

**Non-motor features** (often dominant and undertreated):
- Autonomic: constipation (often early), bladder urgency, orthostatic hypotension, erectile dysfunction, excessive sweating
- Neuropsychiatric: depression (very common), anxiety, apathy, cognitive impairment (dementia in ~30% over 10 years), psychosis (often drug-induced), impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality — associated with dopamine agonists)
- Sleep: REM sleep behaviour disorder (acting out dreams — may precede motor symptoms by a decade), excessive daytime somnolence, restless legs
- Sensory: anosmia (often early), pain
- Speech and swallowing: dysarthria, dysphagia

**Parkinson's disease diagnosis criteria (MDS, 2015):** Bradykinesia + at least one of: rest tremor, rigidity. Supported by typical unilateral onset, rest tremor, good and sustained response to levodopa. Exclusion of red flags suggesting Parkinsonism-plus.

### Distinguishing PD from Parkinsonism-Plus Syndromes

Secondary or atypical Parkinsonism should be suspected if:
- Symmetrical onset from the beginning
- No tremor
- Poor or no response to levodopa (hallmark of atypical Parkinsonism — this is the most important diagnostic clue)
- Early falls (within 1 year of onset)
- Early dementia
- Early autonomic failure
- Supranuclear gaze palsy

| Feature | PD | PSP | MSA | CBS |
|---------|----|----|-----|-----|
| Tremor | Yes (rest) | Rare | Occasional | Rare |
| Symmetry | Asymmetric | Symmetric | Symmetric | Asymmetric |
| Eye movements | Normal | Vertical gaze palsy | Normal | Abnormal |
| Early falls | Late | Early (backwards) | Moderate | Late |
| Autonomic | Moderate | Mild | Severe (early) | Mild |
| Levodopa response | Good (key feature) | Poor | Variable | Poor |
| Cognitive | Late | Frontal executive | Usually spared | Yes (alien limb) |

PSP = Progressive supranuclear palsy; MSA = Multiple system atrophy; CBS = Corticobasal syndrome.

**Drug-induced parkinsonism**: From dopamine receptor blockers — metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, haloperidol, risperidone. Usually symmetric, no tremor. Resolves when drug is stopped (may take months). These drugs must be avoided in PD patients.

### Diagnosis

PD is a **clinical diagnosis**. No blood test or imaging confirms it.

**MRI brain**: Usually normal in PD. Used to exclude structural causes (normal pressure hydrocephalus, vascular Parkinsonism, basal ganglia lesions).

**DAT scan (DaTscan/SPECT)**: Visualises dopamine transporter in the striatum. Reduced and asymmetric in PD and atypical Parkinsonism. Normal in essential tremor, drug-induced Parkinsonism, and psychogenic tremor. Does not distinguish PD from atypical Parkinsonism.

**Levodopa trial**: Significant and sustained improvement in motor function strongly supports PD over atypical Parkinsonism.

### Management

**Levodopa (co-beneldopa / co-careldopa — levodopa + peripheral DOPA decarboxylase inhibitor)**: The most effective symptomatic treatment. The carbidopa/benserazide component prevents peripheral conversion to dopamine, reducing nausea and increasing CNS availability. Start at lowest effective dose.

**Long-term levodopa complications:**
- **Motor fluctuations** ("wearing off"): return of symptoms before next dose; manage with dose frequency increase, extended-release preparations, MAO-B inhibitors, COMT inhibitors
- **Dyskinesias**: involuntary choreiform movements at peak levodopa dose; from long-term levodopa use and progressive receptor sensitisation; difficult to manage

**Other pharmacological agents:**

| Drug Class | Examples | Use |
|-----------|---------|-----|
| Dopamine agonists | Pramipexole, ropinirole, rotigotine (patch) | Early PD or as adjunct; avoid in elderly (hallucinations, impulse control disorders) |
| MAO-B inhibitors | Rasagiline, selegiline | Mild early disease; adjunct to levodopa |
| COMT inhibitors | Entacapone | Adjunct to levodopa for wearing-off |
| Anticholinergics | Trihexyphenidyl | Tremor in young patients; avoid in elderly (cognitive side effects) |
| Amantadine | — | Dyskinesia management |

**Non-pharmacological:**
- Physiotherapy — gait, balance, falls prevention
- Occupational therapy — adaptations for ADLs
- Speech and language therapy — dysarthria, dysphagia
- Dietitian — weight maintenance, dysphagia management
- Psychological support — depression and anxiety are undertreated

**Advanced therapies (late-stage PD with motor complications):**
- Deep brain stimulation (DBS) — bilateral subthalamic nucleus stimulation; most effective for motor fluctuations and dyskinesias in carefully selected patients; does not help non-motor features
- Duodopa (levodopa-carbidopa intestinal gel) — continuous infusion via PEG-J tube; smooths motor fluctuations
- Subcutaneous apomorphine infusion or pump

## Essential Tremor

The most common movement disorder overall. Action tremor (present with sustained posture or movement, absent at rest), typically bilateral, affecting the hands most commonly, also the head (yes-yes or no-no nodding) and voice. Worsened by caffeine, anxiety, and fatigue. Characteristically improved by alcohol (diagnostically useful but not a therapeutic approach). Positive family history in ~50%.

Distinguish from PD: action vs rest tremor; no rigidity or bradykinesia; symmetrical; improved by alcohol; normal DAT scan.

**Treatment**: Propranolol (first-line) or primidone. Topiramate as second-line. DBS for disabling essential tremor refractory to medication.

## Huntington's Disease

Autosomal dominant, caused by a CAG trinucleotide repeat expansion (>36 repeats) in the HTT gene on chromosome 4 — encoding a polyglutamine expansion in huntingtin. **Anticipation** — repeats expand across generations, causing earlier onset in subsequent generations. Mean onset 30–50 years.

**Clinical triad**: Chorea (involuntary, flowing, dance-like movements), psychiatric features (depression, irritability, psychosis — often preceding motor symptoms), and progressive cognitive decline.

Chorea begins focally (finger piano-playing movements, facial grimacing), becomes generalised, and eventually transitions to hypokinesia and rigidity in late stages. Dysphagia, dysarthria, and aspiration are major causes of morbidity and death.

Diagnosis: Genetic testing (CAG repeat number). MRI shows caudate atrophy (gives ventricles a characteristic "butterfly" appearance).

No disease-modifying treatment exists. Symptomatic: tetrabenazine (monoamine-depleter) for chorea; antipsychotics for psychosis; antidepressants for depression. Genetic counselling is essential — offspring have 50% inheritance risk.
## Complications

- PD: falls and hip fractures; aspiration pneumonia (leading cause of death); dementia; orthostatic hypotension; impulse control disorders from dopamine agonists
- Essential tremor: functional disability; social embarrassment
- Huntington's: aspiration pneumonia; psychiatric crisis; suicide (pre-symptomatic genetic testing reveals at-risk status)

## Clinical Insight

The most important single feature distinguishing Parkinson's disease from atypical Parkinsonism is the **response to levodopa**. A dramatic and sustained improvement in motor function after levodopa strongly supports PD. Minimal or absent response after an adequate trial (at least 1 g/day of levodopa for several months) should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis and investigation for PSP or MSA.

Impulse control disorders — pathological gambling, hypersexuality, binge eating, compulsive shopping — affect up to 15% of PD patients on dopamine agonists and are severely underreported by patients. Ask specifically and proactively at every medication review. If identified, reduce or stop the dopamine agonist, even if motor control worsens temporarily.

The neuropsychiatric features of PD are often more disabling than the motor features and are consistently undertreated. Depression affects more than 40% of patients, yet many clinicians focus exclusively on tremor and rigidity. A comprehensive review of non-motor symptoms at every clinic visit — using validated tools like the NMS Quest — is as important as the motor examination.
