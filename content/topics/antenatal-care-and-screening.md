## Overview

Antenatal care is the systematic, evidence-based programme of medical and educational support provided to pregnant women from conception to delivery. Its primary goals are to identify women and pregnancies at increased risk, offer appropriate screening and diagnostic testing, manage complications as they arise, and promote health behaviours (smoking cessation, folic acid, vaccination) that reduce adverse perinatal outcomes. The United Kingdom NICE antenatal care guidelines (CG62, updated 2021) and equivalent ACOG guidelines in the United States define a minimum set of contacts and investigations for low-risk pregnancies, with additional monitoring for those at elevated risk.

## Pathophysiology

### Placental Development and the Origins of Obstetric Complications

The placenta begins as trophoblast cells invading the decidua. From 8–18 weeks, extravillous trophoblast cells invade the uterine spiral arteries, transforming them from high-resistance, narrow vessels into wide, low-resistance channels capable of delivering the dramatically increased blood flow required by the second and third trimester. In pre-eclampsia and fetal growth restriction, this trophoblast invasion is impaired → shallow invasion → persistently high-resistance spiral arteries → placental ischaemia → IUGR and pre-eclampsia. This defective invasion is established by the end of the first trimester, explaining why first-trimester biomarkers (uterine artery Doppler, PAPP-A, PlGF) can identify these pregnancies early enough for aspirin prophylaxis.

### Chromosomal Non-Disjunction and Trisomy 21

Down syndrome (trisomy 21) results from failure of chromosome 21 to separate (non-disjunction) during meiosis, usually in the maternal egg (maternal meiosis I non-disjunction accounts for 95% of cases). The resulting egg has 24 chromosomes; after fertilisation, the zygote has 47 chromosomes (three copies of chromosome 21). Risk rises sharply with maternal age: age 20 → risk 1 in 1500; age 35 → 1 in 385; age 40 → 1 in 106; age 45 → 1 in 30.

The combined first-trimester screening test is a probabilistic risk assessment — not a diagnosis. It combines multiple variables (maternal age, NT measurement, beta-hCG, PAPP-A, ± nasal bone) to generate an individualised risk estimate. A risk of 1 in 85 means there is approximately a 1.2% probability that this particular pregnancy is affected — not certainty.

## Clinical Presentation — Schedule of Antenatal Care (NICE)

**Booking appointment (8–10 weeks ideally, up to 12 weeks):**
- **Full history:** Medical/surgical/psychiatric history, family history (genetic conditions), obstetric history, medications, allergies, social history (smoking, alcohol, drugs, domestic violence, occupation)
- **Examination:** BMI, BP, urinalysis (protein/glucose/infection)
- **Bloods:** FBC (anaemia, thrombocytopenia), blood group and Rhesus status (anti-D prophylaxis planning), antibody screen (irregular antibodies), rubella immunity, haemoglobin electrophoresis (sickle cell/thalassaemia screen — offered to all in UK), HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B surface antigen
- **Offer:** Folic acid 400 mcg/day (started pre-conception, continued to 12 weeks; 5 mg/day for high-risk — prior NTD, epilepsy, diabetes, BMI >30); vitamin D 10 mcg (400 IU)/day

**Screening schedule:**

| Gestation | Investigation | Purpose |
|-----------|--------------|---------|
| 10–13+6 weeks | First-trimester combined test (nuchal translucency + beta-hCG + PAPP-A ± nasal bone) | Trisomy 21, 18, 13 screening |
| 10–12 weeks | Cell-free fetal DNA (cfDNA/NIPT) if high-risk combined screen | High-sensitivity screening for chromosomal aneuploidies |
| 18–20+6 weeks | Anomaly scan (fetal morphology) | Structural abnormalities (cardiac, CNS, abdominal wall) |
| 24–28 weeks | Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) if risk factors | Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) screening |
| 28 weeks | FBC, group and antibody screen, Anti-D (if Rh-negative) | Anaemia, Rh alloimmunisation prevention |
| 28–32 weeks | Growth scan + umbilical artery Doppler (if indicated) | SGA/FGR surveillance |
| 36 weeks | GBS swab (US — vaginal/rectal); UK: risk-based IAP | Group B Streptococcus screening |

## Diagnosis

### First-Trimester Screening

**Combined first-trimester screening test:**
- **Nuchal translucency (NT):** Ultrasound measurement of the fluid-filled space at the back of the fetal neck. Normal NT <2.5–3 mm at 11–13+6 weeks. Increased NT (≥3.5 mm): associated with trisomies + cardiac defects + structural anomalies even if chromosomes are normal.
- **Serum biomarkers:** Free beta-hCG (elevated in trisomy 21, reduced in trisomy 18/13) + PAPP-A (pregnancy-associated plasma protein A — reduced in trisomy 21; also marker of placental dysfunction/FGR/stillbirth risk)
- **Combined test sensitivity:** ~90% for Down syndrome with 5% false positive rate

**Cell-free fetal DNA screening (NIPT/cfDNA):**
- From 10 weeks: fetal DNA fragments in maternal circulation (placental origin — principally trophoblast apoptosis)
- Sensitivity for trisomy 21: >99%; trisomy 18/13: >98%; PPV depends on background risk (a positive NIPT in a high-risk woman has far higher PPV than in a low-risk woman)
- **NIPT is NOT diagnostic** — a positive NIPT requires confirmation with invasive diagnostic testing (CVS or amniocentesis) before any irreversible decision
- **Contingent screening model (UK NHS Fetal Anomaly Screening Programme):** Combined test first → if 1 in 10 to 1 in 150 risk (high probability screen positive) → offer NIPT or direct CVS → if <1 in 150 to 1 in 1000 → offer NIPT only → if <1 in 1000 → routine care

**Diagnostic testing:**
- **Chorionic villus sampling (CVS):** 11–14 weeks; transcervical or transabdominal; placental biopsy for rapid fetal karyotype (2–3 days for QF-PCR; 10–14 days for full karyotype); miscarriage risk ~1%
- **Amniocentesis:** 15–22 weeks; transabdominal aspiration of amniotic fluid (contains fetal cells); full karyotype + chromosomal microarray (detects deletions/duplications); miscarriage risk ~0.5%

### Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM)

**Definition:** Any degree of glucose intolerance first recognised in pregnancy.

**Risk factors for GDM screening:** BMI >30, previous GDM, previous macrosomic baby (>9.9 lb/4.5 kg), first-degree relative with type 2 diabetes, family origin with high T2DM prevalence (South Asian, Middle Eastern, Black Caribbean/African).

**Diagnostic OGTT criteria (WHO 2013 / NICE 2015):**
- Fasting glucose **≥92 mg/dL** (5.1 mmol/L); OR
- 1-hour glucose **≥180 mg/dL** (10.0 mmol/L); OR
- 2-hour glucose **≥153 mg/dL** (8.5 mmol/L)
- Any one criterion = GDM diagnosis

**Complications of GDM (untreated):**
- Macrosomia → birth injury (shoulder dystocia, Erb's palsy), need for CS
- Neonatal hypoglycaemia (neonatal hyperinsulinaemia from maternal hyperglycaemia)
- Pre-eclampsia risk doubled
- IUFD (intrauterine fetal death) risk in poorly controlled GDM
- Long-term: 50% of women with GDM develop type 2 diabetes within 5 years

**Management:** Dietary modification + exercise first (4–6 weeks trial); metformin (first-line pharmacological); insulin (if metformin fails or contraindicated). Blood glucose targets: fasting <95 mg/dL, 1h post-meal <140 mg/dL, 2h post-meal <120 mg/dL. Monitor fetal growth with serial growth scans from 28 weeks. Delivery at 40+6 weeks (or earlier if macrosomia/glycaemic control poor).

### Fetal Growth Restriction (FGR)

**Definition:** Estimated fetal weight (EFW) <10th centile for gestation on ultrasound with evidence of placental insufficiency (abnormal umbilical artery Doppler ± brain-sparing ± uterine artery Doppler).

**Early FGR (<32 weeks):** Often severe placental failure; high stillbirth risk; manage in tertiary centre; serial Doppler surveillance; delivery when risks of prematurity outweighed by risk of continued intrauterine hypoxia.

**Late FGR (≥32 weeks):** More common; less severe; may be missed without growth surveillance. Risk factors: hypertension, autoimmune disease, thrombophilia, smoking, low PAPP-A.

**FIGO 2021 classification:** Stage I–IV based on Doppler findings (absent/reversed end-diastolic flow in umbilical artery = Stage II/III/IV) with corresponding management intensity.

### Pre-eclampsia Risk Assessment and Aspirin Prophylaxis

**ASPRE trial (2017):** Aspirin 150 mg at night from 11–13+6 weeks reduced pre-eclampsia <34 weeks by 62% in high-risk women (identified by combined first-trimester screening using uterine artery Doppler + MAP + PAPP-A + PlGF).

**NICE criteria for aspirin prophylaxis:** Offer aspirin 75–150 mg from 12 weeks if:
- 1+ high-risk factor: previous hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, renal or autoimmune disease (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome), diabetes (type 1 or 2), chronic hypertension
- 2+ moderate risk factors: nulliparity, BMI ≥35, age ≥40, family history of pre-eclampsia, multiple pregnancy, inter-pregnancy interval >10 years

## Management

### Rhesus (Rh) Sensitisation Prevention

**Anti-D prophylaxis:** Rh-negative women must receive anti-D immunoglobulin to prevent sensitisation (alloimmunisation) when fetal Rh-positive red blood cells enter the maternal circulation during sensitising events. Without anti-D: maternal IgG anti-D antibodies develop → in a subsequent Rh-positive pregnancy, antibodies cross the placenta → haemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (HDFN) → hydrops fetalis, stillbirth.

**Routine antenatal anti-D prophylaxis (RAADP):** 500 IU anti-D at 28 weeks (single dose) — covers the risk of feto-maternal haemorrhage in late pregnancy without a specific sensitising event.

**Sensitising events requiring additional anti-D (any gestation):** Miscarriage (>12 weeks), termination of pregnancy, ectopic pregnancy, amniocentesis, CVS, abdominal trauma, external cephalic version (ECV), antepartum haemorrhage.

**Kleihauer-Betke test / flow cytometry:** Quantifies feto-maternal haemorrhage to calculate dose of anti-D needed after birth (if baby is Rh-positive) or after a sensitising event in the third trimester.

### Smoking Cessation in Pregnancy

Smoking in pregnancy: miscarriage, preterm birth, FGR, low birth weight, placental abruption, stillbirth, sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke: binds haemoglobin (fetal COHb) → reduces oxygen delivery to fetus → chronic intrauterine hypoxia → FGR.

**Offer at booking:**
- Carbon monoxide (CO) breath testing (identifies current smokers and secondhand smoke exposure)
- Brief intervention + referral to NHS Stop Smoking Services
- Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) is safe in pregnancy and effective — patches + gum combined; no teratogenicity. Varenicline: limited evidence in pregnancy; generally avoided. Bupropion: avoided.

## Complications

- **First-trimester screening:** False positives (5% at 90% sensitivity for T21) → maternal anxiety, unnecessary invasive testing; false negatives → missed affected pregnancies
- **CVS complications:** Miscarriage (~1%), bleeding, infection; rare limb reduction defects if done <10 weeks
- **GDM:** Macrosomia, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycaemia, pre-eclampsia, maternal progression to T2DM
- **Anti-D failure:** If anti-D dose inadequate for the volume of feto-maternal haemorrhage → sensitisation → HDFN in subsequent pregnancy
- **Smoking/substance use:** FGR, preterm birth, SIDS, behavioural and cognitive effects in the child

## Clinical Insight

The first-trimester combined test risk is a probability, not a diagnosis — and explaining this distinction to a patient who has just received a "positive" result requires skill and time. A risk of 1 in 85 means that for every 85 women with this result, approximately 84 will have an unaffected baby. The result changes the prior probability (which was driven mainly by maternal age of 34 = approximately 1 in 250) upward, but it does not tell us whether this particular baby is affected. The next step is offering cfDNA/NIPT (highly sensitive but non-diagnostic) or invasive testing (CVS or amniocentesis — diagnostic, ~0.5–1% procedural miscarriage risk). The patient must understand that invasive testing gives a definitive answer at a small risk of losing a pregnancy that may have been unaffected.

The BMI 31 and smoking history in this patient have specific, actionable antenatal implications: BMI >30 triggers the GDM screening OGTT at 24–28 weeks, aspirin prophylaxis assessment, additional growth scans in the third trimester, and an anaesthetic review at 28–32 weeks (difficult IV access, increased spinal anaesthesia failure rate). Smoking triggers CO breath testing at every visit, brief intervention, NRT prescription, and serial growth scans from 28 weeks (FGR risk). These are systematic responses to identifiable risk factors — the booking visit is where these risk pathways are initiated.

PAPP-A below the 5th centile at 11–13 weeks is a marker of deficient trophoblast invasion and placental insufficiency, independent of the Down syndrome risk calculation. A low PAPP-A (with or without increased NT or abnormal Down risk) predicts pre-eclampsia and FGR: these women need aspirin 150 mg from 12 weeks, additional uterine artery Doppler at 20 weeks, and serial growth scans from 28 weeks. The Down syndrome risk number captures only part of the clinical information in the first-trimester screen.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 34-year-old primigravida, 12+2 weeks, smoker, BMI 31. Combined test = T21 risk 1 in 85 (high-probability screen positive).

**Step 1 — Explain the result:**
- "The test tells us this pregnancy has a 1 in 85 chance of Down syndrome — about 1.2%. This is higher than average for your age, which is why the test is positive. It does not mean your baby has Down syndrome — in fact, 84 out of 85 women with this result have a baby without Down syndrome."
- "We need to offer you more testing to find out for certain."

**Step 2 — Discuss options:**
- **NIPT (cell-free fetal DNA):** >99% sensitivity; takes 7–10 days for results; NOT diagnostic — if positive, requires amniocentesis/CVS to confirm; no miscarriage risk
- **CVS (chorionic villus sampling):** Can be done now (11–13+6 weeks); diagnostic; result in 2–3 days; ~1% miscarriage risk
- **Amniocentesis:** From 15 weeks; diagnostic; ~0.5% miscarriage risk; longer wait for result
- Patient chooses NIPT first (contingent screening pathway) — if positive, proceed to invasive testing

**Step 3 — Manage concurrent risk factors:**
- **Smoking:** CO breath test + brief intervention + NRT prescription + referral to NHS stop smoking services; document
- **BMI 31:** GDM screening OGTT at 24–28 weeks; aspirin 75–150 mg from 12 weeks (BMI ≥35 is a moderate risk factor — BMI 31 alone does not trigger; but she is also nulliparous + age 34 + two moderate risk factors = consider); review at 28–32 weeks anaesthetic clinic; additional growth scans from 28 weeks
- **Folic acid 5 mg/day:** BMI >30 → higher dose folic acid (5 mg instead of 400 mcg)
- **Vitamin D 400 IU/day**

**Step 4 — Routine booking investigations:**
- Blood group + Rh (start anti-D planning if Rh-negative)
- Haemoglobin (anaemia screening — correct with iron if Hb <11 g/dL)
- Antibody screen, rubella, syphilis, HIV, hepatitis B, haemoglobin electrophoresis

**Exam trigger:** *First-trimester combined test risk 1 in 85 + 34-year-old primigravida* → screen positive. Offer NIPT or invasive testing (CVS/amniocentesis); explain the distinction between screening and diagnosis. The distractor is treating the 1 in 85 result as definitive — it is a risk estimate, not a diagnosis; invasive testing is required before any irreversible decision.
