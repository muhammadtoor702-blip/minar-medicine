## Overview

Colorectal cancer is the third most common cancer worldwide and the second most common cause of cancer death in the United Kingdom and United States. The majority arise from adenomatous polyps through the adenoma-carcinoma sequence — a process taking 10–15 years that is the rationale for colonoscopic surveillance and population screening programmes. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment; the type of resection depends on the tumour location, stage, and the patient's fitness. Diverticular disease, the most common condition affecting the large bowel in Western populations, ranges from asymptomatic to life-threatening (perforation, peritonitis) and shares many clinical features with colorectal cancer — a distinction that must always be made histologically.

## Pathophysiology

### Adenoma-Carcinoma Sequence (Fearon-Vogelstein Model)

The progression from normal colorectal epithelium to invasive carcinoma involves sequential accumulation of mutations:
1. **APC mutation** (chromosome 5q — tumour suppressor): earliest event; causes dysregulation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling → uncontrolled cell proliferation → adenoma formation
2. **KRAS mutation** (proto-oncogene): drives adenoma growth and transition from early to intermediate adenoma
3. **SMAD2/4 mutation** (18q — TGF-β pathway): loss of growth inhibition
4. **TP53 mutation** (chromosome 17p): loss of apoptosis → transition to invasive carcinoma

The process takes approximately 10–15 years from normal mucosa to invasive cancer. This time window is the justification for colonoscopic surveillance and polypectomy.

**Hereditary syndromes:**
- **Lynch syndrome (HNPCC):** Most common hereditary CRC syndrome (3–5% of all CRC); autosomal dominant mutations in DNA mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) → microsatellite instability (MSI-H); right-sided colon cancer predominance; extracolonic cancers (endometrial, ovarian, urinary tract); Amsterdam II criteria; 70–80% lifetime CRC risk; colonoscopic surveillance from age 25 every 1–2 years
- **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP):** APC gene mutation → >100 colonic polyps by teenage years; 100% risk of CRC by age 40 without prophylactic colectomy (total colectomy + ileal pouch-anal anastomosis); upper GI surveillance (duodenal polyps/periampullary cancer risk)
- **MUTYH-associated polyposis (MAP):** Autosomal recessive; MUTYH base excision repair gene mutation; 10–100 polyps; slower progression than FAP

### Colorectal Cancer Distribution

Right-sided (caecum + ascending + transverse): 30%; left-sided (descending + sigmoid): 40%; rectal: 30%.

**Why the different presentations by location:**
- **Right colon:** Large lumen, liquid stool → tumours grow to large size before symptoms; presents with iron-deficiency anaemia, weight loss, right-sided mass; obstruction relatively late
- **Left colon/sigmoid:** Narrow lumen, formed stool → earlier obstruction; change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, "pencil-thin" stools
- **Rectum:** Tenesmus (sensation of incomplete evacuation), bright red rectal bleeding, rectal mass on DRE; spread to lateral pelvic wall lymph nodes (mesorectal envelope)

### Diverticular Disease

Colonic diverticula are mucosal herniations through weaknesses in the circular muscle layer where blood vessels penetrate (pulsion diverticula). They form as a result of high intraluminal pressure from low-fibre diet and altered colonic motility. Predominantly left-sided (sigmoid) in Western populations; right-sided predominance in Asian populations.

**Diverticulitis:** Microperforation of a diverticulum → pericolonic infection → localised abscess or purulent peritonitis or faecal peritonitis. Hinchey classification: I = pericolic abscess; II = pelvic abscess; III = purulent peritonitis; IV = faecal peritonitis. **Diverticular bleeding:** Erosion of arterial supply at the neck of the diverticulum → painless, often massive rectal haemorrhage; right-sided diverticula responsible for the majority of significant bleeding despite being less prevalent.

## Clinical Presentation

### Colorectal Cancer

**Right-sided CRC (caecum/ascending colon):**
- Insidious — iron-deficiency anaemia, fatigue, weight loss
- Palpable right-sided abdominal mass
- Often advanced at diagnosis (large size before obstruction)
- Rarely presents with acute obstruction (wide lumen)

**Left-sided CRC (descending/sigmoid):**
- Change in bowel habit: constipation alternating with diarrhoea; "pencil-thin" stools (narrowed lumen)
- Rectal bleeding mixed with stool (distinguish from haemorrhoidal bleeding which is fresh blood coating stool)
- Lower abdominal cramping
- Obstruction: sudden onset in an elderly patient with a left-sided mass = CRC causing acute large bowel obstruction until proven otherwise

**Rectal cancer:**
- Rectal bleeding (bright red), tenesmus, sensation of incomplete emptying
- Rectal mass on DRE (50% of rectal cancers are within reach of the examining finger)
- Anal canal involvement: faecal incontinence

**Staging (TNM/Duke's):**

| Dukes' | TNM Equivalent | Definition | 5-year Survival |
|--------|---------------|------------|----------------|
| A | T1–2 N0 M0 | Limited to bowel wall | >90% |
| B | T3–4 N0 M0 | Through bowel wall, no nodes | 70–80% |
| C | Any T N1–2 M0 | Lymph node involvement | 40–60% |
| D | Any T Any N M1 | Distant metastases | <10% |

**CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen):** Not for diagnosis (low sensitivity/specificity); used for monitoring after resection — rising CEA indicates recurrence.

### Diverticular Disease

**Diverticulosis (uncomplicated):** Asymptomatic; found incidentally on colonoscopy or CT; no treatment required; high-fibre diet.

**Diverticulitis:** Left iliac fossa pain, fever, nausea. LIF tenderness + guarding (localised peritonism). Leucocytosis. CT abdomen/pelvis: pericolic fat stranding, bowel wall thickening, diverticula, ± abscess or free air. The "left-sided appendicitis."

**Complicated diverticulitis (Hinchey III–IV):** Generalised peritonitis, septic shock, free air under diaphragm (pneumoperitoneum) → emergency surgery.

## Diagnosis

**Investigations for suspected CRC:**

**Colonoscopy:** Gold standard — visualises the entire colon, allows biopsies, detects synchronous lesions (3–5% of CRC are synchronous). If the left-sided tumour prevents the scope from passing, right colon should be imaged by CT colonography post-resection.

**CT colonography (virtual colonoscopy):** For patients unable to tolerate colonoscopy (elderly, comorbid); sensitivity ~95% for polyps >10 mm and cancers.

**Bowel cancer screening (UK NHS programme):** Faecal immunochemical test (FIT) every 2 years for age 50–74 (previously faecal occult blood FOBT) → FIT positive → colonoscopy. Scotland: 50–74 years (from age 50 as of 2021). US: USPSTF recommends FIT annually or colonoscopy every 10 years from age 45–75.

**CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast:** Staging investigation for confirmed CRC — hepatic and pulmonary metastases (sites of haematogenous spread via portal vein → liver, then lung); locoregional lymph node assessment.

**MRI rectum:** Essential for rectal cancer staging — defines the circumferential resection margin (CRM, distance from tumour to mesorectal fascia); CRM <1 mm = positive margin → local recurrence risk >20% → consider long-course neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) to downstage before surgery.

**Carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA):** Pre-operative baseline; used post-operatively to monitor for recurrence — rises 6–12 months before clinical evidence of recurrence.

## Management

### Colorectal Cancer Surgery

**Right-sided CRC:** **Right hemicolectomy** — removal of the terminal ileum, caecum, ascending colon, and hepatic flexure; ileocolic anastomosis. Laparoscopic approach (preferred over open — reduced blood loss, faster recovery, fewer wound complications; equivalent oncological outcomes — COLOR, COST trials).

**Left-sided/sigmoid CRC:** **Left hemicolectomy or sigmoid colectomy** with colorectal anastomosis. High ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) — removes the central lymph node chain; mesorectal dissection.

**Rectal cancer:** **Total mesorectal excision (TME)** — the complete removal of the mesorectal envelope (the fascial compartment containing the rectum and its lymphatic drainage) en bloc; reduces local recurrence from 40% to <10%. Procedures:
- **Anterior resection (AR):** Resection of the upper/mid rectum with colorectal anastomosis; defunctioning loop ileostomy to protect the anastomosis ± reversal at 3 months
- **Abdominoperineal resection (APR):** Low rectal or anal canal tumours not amenable to sphincter-preserving surgery — removal of the rectum AND the anus + sphincter complex → permanent end colostomy; requires specialist pelvic reconstruction

**Neoadjuvant treatment for rectal cancer:**
- **Short-course radiotherapy (25 Gy in 5 fractions):** For operable rectal cancer without threatened CRM; reduces local recurrence
- **Long-course chemoradiotherapy (45–50 Gy + capecitabine):** For locally advanced disease with threatened CRM; downsizes tumour and may sterilise nodes; restage with MRI at 8–12 weeks

**Adjuvant chemotherapy (stage III — positive nodes):** FOLFOX (fluorouracil + leucovorin + oxaliplatin) or CAPOX (capecitabine + oxaliplatin) for 6 months; reduces recurrence by ~25%.

**Liver metastases:** Colorectal liver metastases are potentially resectable — hepatic resection of isolated metastases achieves 5-year survival of 25–40% (FOLFOX ± bevacizumab as perioperative chemotherapy).

### Stoma Types

| Stoma | Contents | Loop or End | Location | Indications |
|-------|----------|-------------|----------|-------------|
| Loop ileostomy | Liquid | Loop | RIF | Defunctioning anastomosis |
| End ileostomy | Liquid | End | RIF | After proctocolectomy |
| Loop colostomy | Semi-formed | Loop | LIF | Defunctioning (emergency) |
| End colostomy (Hartmann's) | Formed | End | LIF | After sigmoid resection with no anastomosis |

### Diverticulitis Management

**Uncomplicated diverticulitis (Hinchey I with small abscess <4 cm):**
- Oral or IV antibiotics (ciprofloxacin + metronidazole; or co-amoxiclav + metronidazole)
- ACTUALLY — multiple RCTs (AVOD, DIABOLO) show antibiotics may not reduce the duration or complication rate of uncomplicated diverticulitis vs. supportive care; UK guidance increasingly recommends reserving antibiotics for the immunocompromised, systemically unwell, or Hinchey II+
- Clear fluids, analgesia
- CT-guided drainage for abscess >4 cm (Hinchey II)
- Colonoscopy 6–8 weeks after resolution (to exclude CRC as the cause)

**Complicated diverticulitis (Hinchey III/IV — peritonitis):**
- **Resuscitation → emergency laparotomy**
- **Hartmann's procedure:** Sigmoid colectomy + end colostomy + closure of rectal stump — the safe operation in a contaminated field; avoids anastomosis in contaminated/haemodynamically unstable patient
- Stoma reversal at 3–6 months (Hartmann's reversal) — if patient fit enough

## Complications

- **CRC surgery:** Anastomotic leak (3–5% colorectal, higher for low anterior resection), post-operative ileus, wound infection, DVT/PE, urinary retention (nerve injury at TME), sexual dysfunction (autonomic nerve injury), stoma complications (parastomal hernia, prolapse, retraction)
- **Rectal cancer:** Nerve injury at TME causing erectile dysfunction, ejaculatory dysfunction, bladder dysfunction (injury to pelvic autonomic plexus); local recurrence (TME has reduced this from 40% to <5% in specialist hands)
- **Local recurrence of CRC:** Most within 3 years of resection; rising CEA is the earliest marker; managed with re-resection (if technically feasible) ± chemotherapy ± ablation
- **Diverticulitis:** Recurrence rate ~20% after first episode; elective sigmoid colectomy recommended after 2nd attack or complicated first attack (abscess, fistula)
- **Diverticular fistula:** Colovesical fistula (most common — air in bladder = pneumaturia, faeces in urine = faecaluria); colovaginal; colocutaneous; requires surgical resection of the fistula and sigmoid segment

## Clinical Insight

The "left-sided appendicitis" heuristic for diverticulitis is useful but dangerous if taken too far. Every patient admitted with diverticulitis must have a colonoscopy 6–8 weeks after resolution to exclude an underlying carcinoma. CT can show thickening and inflammation but cannot always distinguish diverticulitis from a perforated colon cancer. The colonoscopy is not optional — it is the quality-of-care standard.

TME transformed rectal cancer surgery. Before TME was described by Bill Heald in 1982, local recurrence rates after rectal cancer resection were 30–40%. TME — the meticulous dissection of the mesorectal fascia as a complete anatomical unit — reduced this to <5–8% in specialist centres. The anatomical principle is surgical embryology: the mesorectum and its fascial envelope are a distinct compartment that can be removed intact along embryological tissue planes, removing all lymphatics without violating them. The same principle — sharp dissection along embryological planes — applies throughout pelvic surgery.

The CRM on MRI is the single most important pre-operative staging parameter for rectal cancer. A tumour threatening the CRM (≤1 mm from mesorectal fascia) has a >20% local recurrence rate after surgery alone — these patients must receive neoadjuvant long-course chemoradiotherapy before surgery to reduce the tumour and convert a threatened margin to a clear one. Never operate on a threatened CRM without neoadjuvant treatment.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 67-year-old man, 3 months of loose stools/bleeding/weight loss 8 kg. LIF mass. Blood on DRE. Hb 9.8 g/dL, MCV 68 fL (iron-deficiency anaemia), CEA 18.4 ng/mL (raised).

**Step 1 — Urgent 2-week-wait referral (already in secondary care):**
- Classic presentation of left-sided CRC: change in bowel habit + rectal bleeding mixed with stool + iron-deficiency anaemia + raised CEA + palpable mass

**Step 2 — Colonoscopy + biopsy:**
- Confirms tumour in sigmoid/descending colon
- Biopsy for histological confirmation and molecular profiling (MSI/MMR, RAS/RAF, BRAF status — important for systemic therapy decisions)
- Map any synchronous lesions

**Step 3 — Staging CT chest/abdomen/pelvis:**
- Any liver metastases? (portal venous drainage → liver first)
- Lymph node involvement?
- MRI rectum if tumour extends to or near the rectum

**Step 4 — MDT discussion:**
- Stage IIB-III (T4N0 or T3N1) sigmoid adenocarcinoma → proceed to surgery
- No neoadjuvant chemotherapy for colon cancer (unlike rectal cancer)
- Optimise anaemia pre-operatively: IV iron infusion (Hb 9.8 g/dL → aim >10 g/dL before surgery)

**Step 5 — Laparoscopic left hemicolectomy or sigmoid colectomy:**
- High ligation of IMA
- Complete mesocolic excision (CME) — equivalent of TME for colon cancers
- Colorectal anastomosis (± defunctioning loop ileostomy if anastomosis under tension or technically difficult)
- Enhanced recovery pathway post-operatively

**Step 6 — Adjuvant chemotherapy:**
- If lymph nodes positive (stage III): CAPOX 6 months
- Baseline CEA post-operatively; 3-monthly follow-up CEA + CT for 3–5 years

**Exam trigger:** *Elderly man + change in bowel habit + rectal bleeding mixed with stool + iron-deficiency anaemia + left iliac fossa mass* → sigmoid/left-sided CRC until proven otherwise. Urgent colonoscopy + biopsy + CT staging + MDT discussion. The distractor is attributing the rectal bleeding to haemorrhoids — fresh coating blood is haemorrhoids; blood mixed with stool in an elderly patient with weight loss is carcinoma until excluded by colonoscopy.
