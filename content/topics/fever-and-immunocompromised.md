## Overview

Infection in the immunocompromised host is one of the most challenging areas of clinical medicine. The normal inflammatory response is blunted, classical signs of infection (fever, localising inflammation) may be absent or misleading, and the spectrum of potential pathogens is vastly expanded compared with immunocompetent individuals. The two most clinically important contexts are **neutropenic sepsis** (in haematological malignancy and chemotherapy) and **opportunistic infections** in profoundly immunosuppressed patients (HIV/AIDS, solid organ transplant, long-term corticosteroids). The unifying principle: a low threshold to treat, a low threshold to broaden coverage, and a systematic approach to identifying the organism.

## Neutropenic Sepsis

### Pathophysiology

Neutropenia is defined as a neutrophil count <0.5 × 10⁹/L (or <1.0 × 10⁹/L and falling). Febrile neutropenia = temperature ≥38°C in a neutropenic patient. The risk of life-threatening infection is inversely proportional to the neutrophil count and directly proportional to the duration of neutropenia.

Chemotherapy ablates the neutrophil-producing bone marrow, removing the primary defence against bacterial and fungal infections. The mucosal barrier is also disrupted — GI mucositis allows enteric organisms to translocate into the bloodstream. Central venous devices provide a direct portal for skin organisms.

**Pathogens in neutropenic sepsis have shifted over decades:**
- Previously: Gram-negative bacteria (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*) dominated
- Now: Gram-positive organisms (*Staphylococcus epidermidis*, *S. aureus*, *Streptococcus* spp.) more common — partly due to central lines and mucositis
- Gram-negative bacteraemia remains the highest mortality
- **Fungi** (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*) become increasingly important in prolonged neutropenia (>7–10 days) and after prior antibiotic exposure

### Diagnosis

**Blood cultures**: Take peripheral blood cultures AND cultures from each lumen of the central venous catheter before antibiotics. Total 2–3 sets. Do not delay antibiotics by more than 45–60 minutes for cultures.

**Additional investigations**: FBC and differential, U&E, LFTs, CRP, procalcitonin, CXR (baseline). Urine MC&S. Stool MC&S and *C. difficile* if diarrhoea. CT chest if persistent fever after 72 hours of antibiotics (to detect fungal infection — early ground-glass halos around pulmonary nodules suggest *Aspergillus*).

**MASCC score** — risk stratification for neutropenic fever: scores ≥21 = low risk (may be suitable for oral antibiotics and discharge); <21 = high risk (requires IV therapy and inpatient management).

### Management

**Start within 60 minutes** of triage. This is a time-critical emergency.

**Empirical IV antibiotics (NICE NG143):**
- **First-line**: IV piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5g TDS (covers Gram-negatives including *Pseudomonas*, and Gram-positives)
- Add **vancomycin** (or teicoplanin) if: suspected line infection, skin/soft tissue infection, haemodynamic instability, MRSA risk, or mucositis with streptococcal risk
- Add **gentamicin** for suspected Gram-negative septicaemia with haemodynamic compromise (synergy with pip-tazo)

**Antifungal escalation:**
- If persistent fever after 72–96 hours of broad-spectrum antibiotics with no bacterial source identified: **add IV antifungal therapy** (caspofungin first-line, or liposomal amphotericin B for suspected mould)
- CT chest at 72–96 hours to identify fungal pneumonia (*Aspergillus*)
- Galactomannan assay — serum biomarker for *Aspergillus* — send twice weekly in prolonged neutropenia

**De-escalation**: Review at 48–72 hours. If cultures are negative and patient is improving, consider de-escalation or oral switch. Duration typically 5–7 days minimum or until neutrophil recovery.

**G-CSF** (granulocyte colony-stimulating factor — filgrastim): Accelerates neutrophil recovery. NICE recommends consideration in high-risk neutropenic fever.

**Central line removal**: If line infection is suspected and organisms are cultured from line blood cultures with differential time to positivity >2 hours compared with peripheral culture, remove the line. Always remove for *S. aureus*, fungaemia, or if the patient is not improving.

## Fever of Unknown Origin (FUO)

### Definition

Classic FUO (Petersdorf and Beeson, revised):
- Temperature >38.3°C on ≥3 occasions
- Duration >3 weeks (distinguishes from acute self-limiting illnesses)
- No diagnosis established after 3 days of inpatient investigation (or 3 outpatient visits)

This definition has been supplemented by specific FUO categories:

| Category | Setting | Key Pathogens/Causes |
|---------|---------|---------------------|
| Classic FUO | Community patient | Infection (~30%), malignancy (~20%), inflammatory/autoimmune (~30%), miscellaneous (~20%) |
| Healthcare-associated FUO | Hospitalised ≥3 days | Drug fever, VTE, sinusitis (NG tube), *C. difficile*, wound infection, device infection |
| Immunocompromised FUO | Neutropenic, HIV, transplant | Bacterial, fungal, viral — expanded differential |
| HIV-associated FUO | HIV-positive | MAC, CMV, PCP, TB, lymphoma |

### Causes of Classic FUO

**Infections (~30%):**
- TB (most common infectious cause globally) — miliary or extrapulmonary
- Bacterial endocarditis — low-grade bacteraemia; blood cultures may require prolonged incubation
- Intra-abdominal abscess — subphrenic, pelvic, hepatic
- Brucellosis — unpasteurised dairy or animal exposure
- Visceral leishmaniasis (kala-azar) — travel to endemic regions; splenomegaly; pancytopaenia
- Viral: EBV, CMV (infectious mononucleosis-like syndromes)
- Parasitic: toxoplasmosis, malaria (especially *P. vivax* with delayed presentation)

**Malignancies (~20%):**
- Hodgkin's and non-Hodgkin's lymphoma — most common malignant cause. Pel-Ebstein fever (cyclical high fever) is classic but rare. Night sweats, weight loss.
- Leukaemia, solid tumours with hepatic metastases, renal cell carcinoma, atrial myxoma

**Inflammatory/autoimmune (~30%):**
- Adult-onset Still's disease — quotidian fever, salmon-pink rash (evanescent, appears with fever), arthritis, high ferritin (often >5000 µg/L)
- Systemic vasculitis (GCA, PAN, GPA)
- SLE — multi-system, ANA, anti-dsDNA
- Sarcoidosis — hilar lymphadenopathy, elevated ACE
- Inflammatory bowel disease
- Crystal arthropathy (gout, pseudogout)

**Drug fever (~10%):**
Beta-lactam antibiotics, sulphonamides, heparin, phenytoin, allopurinol, and many others. Fever with relative bradycardia and eosinophilia are clues. Resolves within 48–72 hours of stopping the offending drug.

### Diagnosis of FUO

**A systematic, stepwise approach:**

**Tier 1 — broad screen (most patients):**
FBC + differential, ESR, CRP, LFTs, U&E, LDH, ferritin (markedly elevated in adult Still's and HLH), protein electrophoresis (myeloma, lymphoma), TFTs, blood cultures × 3 (prolonged incubation), urine MC&S, CXR, USS abdomen (lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, abscess), HIV serology, EBV and CMV serology, ANA, ANCA, anti-dsDNA, complement, TB IGRA.

**Tier 2 — guided by tier 1 findings:**
CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (lymphoma staging, abscess, splenomegaly), echocardiography (endocarditis, atrial myxoma), bone marrow biopsy (lymphoma, TB, leishmaniasis), PET-CT (identifies occult infection/malignancy foci with excellent sensitivity), lumbar puncture (CNS infection/malignancy), specific serologies based on exposure history.

**PET-CT**: Increasingly used early in FUO workup — identifies areas of high metabolic activity (infection, malignancy, vasculitis) that direct further investigation. Reduces time to diagnosis significantly.

### Management of FUO

**Do not start empirical antibiotics or steroids in stable patients with FUO** — this obscures the diagnosis and delays appropriate treatment. Antibiotics suppress bacteraemia (reducing culture sensitivity); steroids suppress the inflammatory response that may be the only diagnostic clue. Wait for a diagnosis before treating, unless the patient is deteriorating.

## Infection in Solid Organ Transplant Recipients

Transplant recipients require lifelong immunosuppression to prevent rejection — calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, ciclosporin), mycophenolate, corticosteroids — which profoundly impair T-cell function and make patients susceptible to a predictable timeline of infections.

**Timeline of infections post-transplant:**

| Period | Risk | Infections |
|--------|------|-----------|
| 0–1 month | Surgical, nosocomial | Bacterial (wound, anastomotic, pneumonia), donor-derived infections, *C. difficile* |
| 1–6 months | Peak immunosuppression | CMV (most important — fever, leucopaenia, hepatitis, pneumonitis), EBV (post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder), PCP, *Pneumocystis*, *Listeria*, *Nocardia*, TB reactivation |
| >6 months | Chronic maintenance | Opportunistic infections (if heavy immunosuppression), community-acquired infections, late CMV |

**CMV in transplant**: Screen all donors and recipients before transplant. D+/R- (donor CMV-positive, recipient negative) = highest risk. Prophylaxis with valganciclovir or pre-emptive monitoring with CMV PCR.

**PCP prophylaxis**: Co-trimoxazole for first 6–12 months post-transplant.
## Complications

- **Untreated febrile neutropenia**: bacteraemia progressing to septic shock within hours; mortality rises steeply with each hour of delayed antibiotics
- **Invasive fungal infection**: Aspergillus — pulmonary angioinvasion, haemoptysis, haematogenous dissemination; Candida — endophthalmitis, endocarditis; mortality >40% in invasive disease
- **PCP**: respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, pneumothorax, long-term oxygen dependence
- **CMV disease**: retinitis (can progress to blindness), colitis, oesophagitis, pneumonitis, encephalitis
- **Graft-versus-host disease** (post-SCT): acute GVHD — hepatic failure, severe colitis; chronic GVHD — bronchiolitis obliterans, sicca syndrome
- **Antimicrobial overuse**: AKI (aminoglycosides, amphotericin), hepatotoxicity (azoles), drug-resistant organisms

## Clinical Insight

In febrile neutropenia, the source of infection is never found in approximately 40–60% of patients. This does not mean there is no infection — it means the organisms are not identifiable by current methods, or the infection has been suppressed before localising. Empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics are started because the consequences of undertreating are catastrophic. A temperature of 38°C in a neutropenic patient is a medical emergency equivalent to septic shock in an immunocompetent patient.

Markedly elevated ferritin (>5000 µg/L, often >10,000 µg/L) in a patient with FUO narrows the differential significantly: adult-onset Still's disease, haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), viral infections (EBV, CMV), and haematological malignancies. HLH — extreme macrophage and NK-cell activation with phagocytosis of blood cells — is life-threatening and requires immediate recognition: fever + splenomegaly + bicytopaenia/pancytopaenia + hyperferritinaemia + triglyceridaemia. Treat with etoposide and dexamethasone (HLH-94 protocol).

Never start steroids for presumed inflammatory FUO without excluding TB, occult lymphoma, and disseminated fungal infection. Steroids given to a patient with undiagnosed TB or systemic fungal disease cause a clinical catastrophe — converting a controlled infection into overwhelming dissemination. Invest in the diagnosis before the treatment.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 58-year-old man with AML on chemotherapy. Day 12 post-cycle. Fever 38.6°C, rigors. Central venous line in situ. HR 118, BP 96/62. Neutrophil count 0.2 × 10⁹/L. No clear focus of infection clinically. Blood cultures taken.

**Step 1 — Recognise this as neutropenic sepsis — a time-critical emergency:** Neutrophil count 0.2 × 10⁹/L (<0.5 × 10⁹/L = neutropenic) + temperature 38.6°C = febrile neutropenia. The haemodynamic picture — HR 118, BP 96/62 — indicates septic shock. This is not "pyrexia to monitor"; this is a patient who will die without immediate antibiotics. The target is **antibiotics within 60 minutes** of triage. Every additional hour of delay increases mortality.

**Step 2 — Blood cultures before antibiotics, but do not delay antibiotics beyond 45–60 minutes:** Take peripheral blood cultures AND cultures from each lumen of the central venous catheter (differential time to positivity >2 hours between line and peripheral cultures = line infection). Total 2–3 sets. Also send: FBC, U&E, LFTs, CRP, procalcitonin, CXR (baseline), urine MC&S. The source of neutropenic sepsis is never found in ~40–60% of cases — do not wait for a source before starting antibiotics.

**Step 3 — Start empirical broad-spectrum IV antibiotics immediately:** **First-line: IV piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5g TDS** — covers Gram-negative rods including *Pseudomonas* (the highest-mortality pathogen in febrile neutropenia), and Gram-positives. This patient has a central venous line and is haemodynamically unstable — **add IV vancomycin** (covers MRSA line infection and coagulase-negative staphylococci). The line is a portal of entry for skin organisms. Fluid resuscitation simultaneously — 500 mL crystalloid boluses, reassess after each.

**Step 4 — If no improvement at 72–96 hours, add antifungal therapy:** The absence of a positive bacterial culture and persistent fever after 72 hours of pip-tazo + vancomycin in a patient who has been neutropenic for 12 days (day 12 post-cycle) raises the possibility of **invasive fungal infection** — particularly *Aspergillus* or *Candida*. Order CT chest — early *Aspergillus* pulmonary angioinvasion presents as nodules with a "halo sign" (ground-glass surrounding a solid nodule — represents haemorrhagic infarction). Add **caspofungin IV** (first-line empirical antifungal in neutropenic fever). Send **galactomannan** twice weekly in prolonged neutropenia.

**Step 5 — Assess for central line removal:** Line infection is highly probable given the line in situ. If cultures from the line lumen turn positive (particularly with *S. aureus*, fungaemia, or coagulase-negative staphylococci that are not responding to antibiotics), **remove the line**. A line that is the source of infection will not be sterilised by antibiotics alone for high-risk organisms. The temporary loss of IV access is manageable; untreated line infection in a neutropenic patient is not.

**Step 6 — Neutrophil recovery is the definitive treatment:** Once the marrow recovers (neutrophil count rises above 0.5 × 10⁹/L and continuing to rise), the patient's primary defence is restored. Consider **G-CSF** (filgrastim) to accelerate recovery in high-risk patients. Review for IV-to-oral switch and de-escalation once cultures are known, the patient is clinically improving, and the neutrophil count is rising.

**Exam trigger:** *Neutropenic patient (chemotherapy day 12) + fever + haemodynamic instability* → **neutropenic sepsis — antibiotics within 60 minutes**. First-line: **IV piperacillin-tazobactam**. Add **vancomycin** if line infection suspected, haemodynamic instability, or MRSA risk. No bacterial source found after 72 hours + persistent fever → add **caspofungin** + CT chest for fungal infection. The distractor is waiting to find the source before starting antibiotics — there is no time, and a source is not found in ~half of cases. The other distractor is giving oral antibiotics in a haemodynamically unstable neutropenic patient.
