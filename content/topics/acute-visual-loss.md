## Overview

Acute visual loss is an ophthalmological emergency — the therapeutic window for many causes is measured in minutes to hours. Monocular visual loss indicates pathology at or anterior to the optic chiasm; binocular loss suggests posterior (chiasm, optic tracts, cortex, or brainstem). The single most important distinction in acute visual loss is whether it is **painful** or **painless**, as this dichotomy largely separates anterior segment disease from posterior segment and vascular causes. The prior history of transient visual loss (amaurosis fugax) in this scenario is a critical clue — it indicates retinal ischaemia from a carotid or cardiac embolic source and correctly frames the current acute loss as likely vascular.

## Pathophysiology

### Vascular Causes of Acute Visual Loss

**Central Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO):**
- Occlusion of the central retinal artery (branch of ophthalmic artery, from ICA) → sudden, profound, painless monocular visual loss
- Retinal ischaemia: retinal neural tissue tolerates ischaemia for approximately 90–100 minutes before irreversible damage; arterial occlusion → inner retinal ischaemia → whitening (oedema) of entire retina except fovea (supplied by choroidal circulation → appears as **"cherry red spot"** against the white ischaemic retina)
- Aetiology: embolism (cholesterol/Hollenhorst plaque from carotid, cardiac thrombus, calcified valve emboli), thrombosis (atherosclerosis, hypercoagulable state), vasculitis (GCA — always consider in elderly patients)
- **Prior amaurosis fugax = TIA of the retina** — emboli briefly occluding retinal circulation; same pathophysiology as cerebral TIA

**Central Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO):**
- Occlusion of the central retinal vein at the lamina cribrosa → venous hypertension throughout retina → haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants ("blood and thunder" fundus), disc oedema, tortuous veins, cotton wool spots, macular oedema
- Two types:
  - **Ischaemic CRVO** (~20%): Marked visual loss (VA often <6/60); >10 disc areas of non-perfusion on FFA; high risk of rubeosis iridis (iris neovascularisation) → neovascular glaucoma within 3 months ("90-day glaucoma")
  - **Non-ischaemic CRVO** (~80%): Milder visual loss; better prognosis; may recover spontaneously
- Risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, hyperviscosity (polycythaemia, myeloma, Waldenström's), glaucoma

**Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (AION):**
- Ischaemia of the anterior portion of the optic nerve (supplied by posterior ciliary arteries from the ophthalmic artery)
- **Arteritic AION (AAION) — caused by Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA):** Most common cause of blindness from GCA; characterised by dramatic visual loss; chalky pale disc oedema; urgent ESR/CRP/temporal artery biopsy + immediate high-dose steroids (see below); **bilateral involvement in 25–50% if untreated**
- **Non-arteritic AION (NAION):** Small cup:disc ratio (disc at risk); associated with cardiovascular risk factors, OSA, nocturnal hypotension; no proven treatment; fellow eye involved in 15% at 5 years

### Retinal Detachment

Separation of the neurosensory retina from the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Three types:
- **Rhegmatogenous (most common):** Retinal break/tear → liquid vitreous enters subretinal space → detachment progressively extends. Break commonly caused by posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) — age-related vitreous liquefaction and collapse → vitreous base tears retina at weakest points.
- **Tractional:** Fibrovascular proliferative membrane (from diabetic retinopathy, sickle cell) pulls retina off — no break.
- **Exudative:** Subretinal fluid from choroidal disease (tumour, inflammation, uveitis) — no break, retina shifts with position.

**Symptoms of RRD (rhegmatogenous):** PVD symptoms precede — **flashes of light** (phosphenes from vitreous traction on retina), **floaters** (vitreous debris/blood from torn vessel). Then: peripheral shadow (detachment extending) → curtain or veil (macula threatened/involved) → central visual loss (macular detachment = surgical emergency within 24h).

### Vitreous Haemorrhage

Blood in the vitreous cavity — blocks the fundal view. Sudden onset of **floaters, haze, or profound visual loss** depending on density. Causes: posterior vitreous detachment (with retinal tear — beware concurrent retinal detachment), proliferative diabetic retinopathy (neovascular frond bleeding), CRVO with rubeosis, trauma, subarachnoid haemorrhage (Terson syndrome — blood breaks into vitreous from raised ICP).

## Clinical Presentation

### Painless Monocular Visual Loss — Differential

| Feature | CRAO | CRVO | AION | Retinal Detachment | Vitreous Haemorrhage |
|---------|------|------|------|--------------------|---------------------|
| **Onset** | Sudden, seconds | Rapid (hours) | Sudden | Gradual (curtain) | Sudden floaters/haze |
| **Vision** | Profound loss (CF/HM/PL) | Moderate–severe | Altitudinal field defect | Peripheral → central | Variable |
| **Pain** | None | None | None | None | None |
| **Fundus** | Cherry red spot + pale retina | "Blood and thunder" | Pale disc oedema | Retina elevated, grey | No fundal view |
| **Emergency** | Yes — 90 min window | Yes | Yes (GCA) | Yes | Yes |

**Amaurosis fugax (TIA of the retina):**
- Brief (<5 min), complete monocular visual loss, "curtain descending" or "like a shutter closing"
- Painless, complete recovery
- Caused by transient embolus to central retinal artery
- Carries same stroke risk as hemispheric TIA → same-day stroke service/TIA clinic + ABCD2 score + urgent carotid duplex + antiplatelet therapy + risk factor management
- **GCA must be excluded** in elderly patients: scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, headache, ESR/CRP

### Giant Cell Arteritis — Recognition

GCA must be considered in ANY patient >50 with acute visual loss, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, temporal headache, or systemic symptoms (malaise, weight loss, polymyalgia). Once one eye is affected, the fellow eye is at risk if treatment is delayed.

**Investigations:**
- ESR: typically >50 mm/h (often >100); CRP: elevated
- **Temporal artery biopsy:** Definitive but can be negative ("skip lesions"); biopsy within 2 weeks of starting steroids (steroids do not negate biopsy for up to 2 weeks)
- Ultrasound of temporal arteries: "Halo sign" (oedematous vessel wall) on Doppler

**Treatment:**
- **START HIGH-DOSE STEROIDS IMMEDIATELY — do not wait for biopsy**
- AION: IV methylprednisolone 500 mg–1 g daily × 3 days, then prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day orally
- Non-ocular GCA without visual loss: prednisolone 40–60 mg/day

### Retinal Detachment — Urgency Stratification

- **Macula ON (macula attached):** Surgical emergency within 24h — macula detachment worsens prognosis; every hour matters
- **Macula OFF (macula detached):** Surgery within 7–10 days acceptable; prognosis already reduced (central vision loss likely permanent even after anatomically successful repair)

## Diagnosis

### Emergency Assessment

**Visual acuity (Snellen, pinhole, counting fingers, hand motion, light perception):** Quantifies severity.

**Pupil reactions:**
- **Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD):** Swinging flashlight test — affected eye appears to dilate when light swings to it (reduced afferent signal → reduced consensual reflex → net pupil dilation relative to fellow eye). Indicates optic nerve or severe retinal disease. Present in CRAO, AION, retinal detachment, optic neuritis. Absent in early macular disease, CRVO (bilateral input reduction).

**Fundoscopy (dilated):** Gold standard — dilate with tropicamide 1% for best view. In vitreous haemorrhage, fundal view obscured.

**Ultrasound B-scan:** When fundal view obscured by VH → identifies retinal detachment (high reflective membrane with restricted movement), vitreous haemorrhage (low-medium reflective opacities), choroidal detachment.

**OCT (optical coherence tomography):** High-resolution cross-sectional imaging of retina and optic nerve — identifies macular oedema (CRVO, DME), subretinal fluid, disc oedema (AION), macular hole.

**Fluorescein angiography (FFA):** Maps retinal vascular perfusion — identifies areas of non-perfusion (CRVO ischaemia), neovascularisation, leakage.

**Carotid Doppler + echocardiogram:** For CRAO/amaurosis fugax — identify embolic source.

**Blood tests for GCA:** ESR, CRP, FBC (normocytic anaemia, elevated platelets), LFTs (ALP raised), glucose.

## Management

### Central Retinal Artery Occlusion — Emergency

CRAO has a **therapeutic window of ~90–100 minutes** before irreversible retinal damage. There are no proven therapies with robust RCT evidence, but immediate action is standard:

**Ocular massage:** Apply firm pressure to the eye for 5–10 seconds, then release suddenly — may dislodge embolus distally.

**Anterior chamber paracentesis:** Ophthalmologist removes a small amount of aqueous fluid → reduces IOP → increases perfusion pressure gradient → may improve retinal blood flow. Performed in theatre or procedure room.

**Carbogen inhalation (5% CO₂ + 95% O₂):** Vasodilatory → increases retinal blood flow; limited evidence.

**IV thrombolysis (tissue plasminogen activator — tPA):** For embolic CRAO within 4.5 hours → intraarterial tPA via ophthalmological radiology is under investigation; IV tPA has been used in selected centres analogous to ischaemic stroke. NOT standard care but evolving evidence.

**Systemic workup (simultaneously):** ECG (AF), carotid duplex, echocardiogram, blood glucose, FBC, ESR/CRP (GCA exclusion) — identify and treat underlying cause.

### Retinal Detachment — Surgery

**Pneumatic retinopexy:** Office procedure; gas bubble injected into vitreous → tamponade retinal break; laser/cryo to seal break; suitable for selected superior RRDs.

**Scleral buckle:** Silicone band sutured around scleral circumference → indents scleral wall toward retinal break; reduces traction; effective for peripheral breaks; no vitrectomy required; LA or GA.

**Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV):** Most versatile; remove vitreous, drain subretinal fluid, endolaser around break, gas or silicone oil tamponade. Standard for posterior breaks, large RRDs, PVR, and vitreous haemorrhage with RRD.

**Post-operative:** Gas-filled eye → posturing (face down for oil/gas to tamponade break) × 1–2 weeks; no flying (gas expansion at altitude → acute IOP rise).

### CRVO

**Macular oedema (most common vision-threatening complication):**
- **Intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept):** First-line for macular oedema from CRVO — monthly injections initially
- **Intravitreal dexamethasone implant (Ozurdex):** Sustained-release steroid — effective; risk of IOP rise and cataract

**Rubeosis iridis prevention (ischaemic CRVO):** Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) if neovascularisation develops — ablates ischaemic retina, reduces VEGF production.

### Vitreous Haemorrhage

- Bed rest + head elevation for 1–2 days (allows VH to settle inferiorly, improves central vision)
- B-scan USS to exclude RRD
- **PPV (pars plana vitrectomy):** If VH fails to clear or if underlying RRD requires repair; also allows endolaser treatment of PDR fronds to prevent recurrent haemorrhage

## Complications

- **CRAO:** Visual acuity <6/60 in ~75% despite treatment; cherry red spot disappears after weeks as retina atrophies; optic atrophy; high stroke risk (same pathophysiology as cerebral TIA/stroke)
- **CRVO:** Permanent macular oedema (vision loss); neovascular glaucoma (90-day glaucoma) — devastating, painful blind eye; requires PRP and IOP control
- **GCA:** Bilateral blindness if second eye involved; systemic vascular complications (aortic aneurysm, large vessel stenosis) in 20%
- **Retinal detachment:** Even after successful anatomical repair, visual acuity may be permanently reduced especially if macula was off; PVR (proliferative vitreoretinopathy) — recurrence of detachment from fibrous membrane formation; re-operation required in 5–10%

## Clinical Insight

Amaurosis fugax is the most important warning symptom in vascular eye disease. The temporary monocular visual loss (seconds to minutes) is the retinal equivalent of a TIA — an embolic event that resolves before infarction occurs. The proper analogy is hemispheric TIA: the absolute stroke risk in the days following amaurosis fugax is similar to hemispheric TIA, and the management is identical — same-day assessment, carotid duplex, cardiac monitoring, immediate antiplatelet therapy, and aggressive risk factor management. In the clinical scenario, the 2-week history of such episodes before the current acute loss is exactly the warning that preceded CRAO or vitreous haemorrhage from underlying retinal vascular disease.

Giant cell arteritis deserves special emphasis because it is both common (prevalence 1 in 500 over age 60) and because the treatment for one of its most devastating complications — visual loss — must be started before the diagnosis is confirmed. The teaching principle: in a patient over 50 with acute visual loss, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, or elevated ESR/CRP, start high-dose steroids immediately. Do not wait for the temporal artery biopsy result. Biopsying a vessel does not require suspending treatment. The biopsy will remain positive for at least 2 weeks after starting steroids. The cost of delayed treatment is bilateral blindness; the cost of a 2-week unnecessary steroid course is modest.

The "curtain coming down" description in retinal detachment is so specific that it should immediately trigger the question of macular involvement. Peripheral RRD causes a peripheral shadow or field loss — often ignored by patients. When the curtain is perceived to originate from above (superior detachment extending inferiorly toward the macula) and is moving toward central vision, the macula is about to detach. This is a same-day surgical emergency: every hour of macular detachment reduces final visual acuity outcomes, and the distinction between macula-on and macula-off is the single most important prognostic variable in retinal detachment surgery.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 72F, woke with sudden painless right visual loss ("curtain from top"), 2-week history of brief right monocular visual loss (amaurosis fugax), HT + hypercholesterolaemia + T2DM, fundoscopy: vitreous haemorrhage obscuring view.

**Step 1 — Interpret the history:**
- Amaurosis fugax × 2 weeks → retinal TIAs from embolic source (carotid/cardiac)
- Now sudden visual loss with VH → likely **CRAO with vitreous haemorrhage**, OR **retinal detachment** (PVD tearing a vessel + retina), OR proliferative diabetic retinopathy bleed (though no mention of PDR history)

**Step 2 — Immediate assessment:**
- VA right eye: CF or HM level (document)
- RAPD right eye? (likely present if CRAO or RD)
- IOP both eyes
- Left eye normal: good sign (CRAO usually unilateral)

**Step 3 — B-scan ultrasound (same day):**
- Fundal view obscured by VH → **B-scan USS urgently** → exclude retinal detachment
- If retinal detachment found: emergency PPV within 24h (macular status unknown until VH clears, but if macula at risk from VH + RD → treat as macula-on emergency)

**Step 4 — Vascular workup (CRAO aetiology):**
- ECG: AF?
- Carotid duplex: significant stenosis/plaque?
- ESR/CRP: GCA (age 72 — always check)
- Fasting glucose, HbA1c, lipids
- Cardiology review for echocardiogram if cardiac source suspected

**Step 5 — CRAO treatment (if confirmed, within 90-min window):**
- Ocular massage + anterior chamber paracentesis (if within window)
- Systemic workup to prevent fellow eye and stroke

**Step 6 — Secondary prevention:**
- Aspirin 300 mg stat + 75 mg maintenance
- Statin optimisation, BP control, glycaemic control
- Same-day TIA/stroke pathway assessment — ABCD2 score

**Exam trigger:** *Amaurosis fugax preceding acute monocular visual loss* → vascular aetiology (CRAO, embolic); treat as retinal TIA; same-day carotid duplex, aspirin, statin. Always check GCA in elderly with monocular visual loss.
