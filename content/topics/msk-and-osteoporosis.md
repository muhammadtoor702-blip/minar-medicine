## Overview

Musculoskeletal (MSK) disorders encompass degenerative joint disease, soft tissue conditions, and metabolic bone disease. Osteoarthritis is the most prevalent joint disease globally. Osteoporosis — reduced bone mineral density leading to fragility fractures — is the most common metabolic bone disease and a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the elderly. Understanding these conditions requires distinguishing inflammatory from mechanical joint disease and identifying patients at risk of fracture before it occurs.

## Osteoarthritis

### Pathophysiology

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a disease of the entire joint — cartilage, subchondral bone, synovium, ligaments, and periarticular muscle. It is not simply "wear and tear" — it is an active, dynamic process of failed cartilage repair.

Articular cartilage is avascular and alnervous — it depends on diffusion from synovial fluid for nutrition. Chondrocytes maintain the extracellular matrix (collagen II and proteoglycans). In OA, an imbalance between matrix synthesis and degradation (from MMPs and ADAMTS) leads to cartilage loss. Subchondral bone sclerosis (from increased mechanical load), osteophyte formation (bony outgrowths at joint margins), and low-grade synovial inflammation all contribute to symptoms.

**Risk factors**: Age (most important — cartilage repair capacity declines), female sex, obesity (increased mechanical load + metabolic — adipokines from visceral fat are directly inflammatory to cartilage), previous joint injury, occupation (repetitive joint loading), genetic predisposition.

### Clinical Presentation

**Distribution**: OA affects specific joints — DIPs (Heberden's nodes — bony swellings), PIPs (Bouchard's nodes), first CMC (thumb base — "squaring" of the thumb), hips, knees, and the first MTP. The spine (facet joints and intervertebral discs) is commonly affected.

**Distinguishing OA from inflammatory arthritis:**

| Feature | Osteoarthritis | Inflammatory Arthritis |
|---------|--------------|----------------------|
| Age | Usually >50 | Any age |
| Onset | Gradual | Insidious or acute |
| Morning stiffness | <30 minutes | >60 minutes |
| Activity effect | Worse with use, better with rest | Better with use, worse with rest |
| Joint heat | Absent or mild | Present |
| Synovitis | Absent or mild | Prominent |
| Distribution | DIPs, thumb base, hips, knees, spine | MCPs, PIPs, wrists (RA); DIP (PsA) |
| CRP/ESR | Normal | Elevated |

**Hip OA**: Groin pain (true hip pain is groin pain — not buttock or lateral thigh pain), radiating to the knee, worse with weight-bearing and stairs. Reduced internal rotation is the earliest and most sensitive examination finding.

**Knee OA**: Medial compartment most commonly — varus deformity ("bow-legs"). Crepitus, effusion (cool — unlike inflammatory), joint line tenderness. Difficulty with stairs, squatting, and rising from chairs.

### Diagnosis

X-ray findings (**LOSS mnemonic**):
- **L**oss of joint space (asymmetric — medial compartment in knee)
- **O**steophytes (at joint margins)
- **S**ubchondral sclerosis (increased density of bone beneath cartilage)
- **S**ubchondral cysts (geodes)

MRI detects early cartilage changes and soft tissue pathology (meniscal tears, ligament damage). Bloods are normal in OA — raised CRP suggests concomitant inflammatory disease.

### Management

**Non-pharmacological (always first-line):**
- Weight loss — the most effective single intervention for knee OA. Each kg of weight loss reduces knee joint load by ~4 kg. Weight loss of 10% reduces pain by ~50%.
- Physiotherapy — strengthening periarticular muscles reduces joint load and improves function. Quadriceps strengthening for knee OA; hip abductor and external rotator strengthening for hip OA.
- Walking aids (stick in the contralateral hand), orthotics, bracing.
- Local heat, TENS, hydrotherapy.

**Pharmacological:**
- Topical NSAIDs (diclofenac gel) — first-line for knee and hand OA; effective, minimal systemic absorption
- Paracetamol — limited evidence as monotherapy; reasonable as add-on
- Oral NSAIDs — for moderate-severe symptoms; lowest effective dose, shortest duration; add PPI
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injection — for acute flare with effusion; short-term relief; maximum 3–4 per joint per year
- Intra-articular hyaluronic acid — limited evidence; some guidelines no longer recommend
- Duloxetine — for OA with central sensitisation component (chronic pain with disproportionate distress)

**Surgical**:
- Total knee or hip replacement — most effective intervention for end-stage OA with failed conservative treatment. Dramatically reduces pain and improves function. Revision rate ~10% at 15 years.
- Arthroscopy — no role for degenerative meniscal tears or knee OA without mechanical symptoms (meniscal locking) — multiple RCTs show no benefit over physiotherapy.

## Osteoporosis

### Pathophysiology

Bone is a dynamic tissue — continuously remodelled by osteoclasts (resorption) and osteoblasts (formation). Peak bone mass is achieved in the third decade. Osteoporosis results from an imbalance favouring resorption, leading to reduced bone mineral density (BMD) and microarchitectural deterioration → increased fracture risk.

**Causes of bone loss:**
- **Oestrogen deficiency** — the primary driver in postmenopausal women. Oestrogen normally inhibits osteoclast activity; its withdrawal removes this brake.
- **Glucocorticoids** — the most common cause of secondary osteoporosis. Suppress osteoblast activity (reduced bone formation), increase osteoclast lifespan, reduce intestinal calcium absorption, increase renal calcium excretion.
- Other: Hypogonadism (men — testosterone inhibits bone resorption), hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, CKD, malabsorption, immobility, alcohol, smoking.

**WHO diagnostic criteria (DEXA T-score):**
- Normal: T-score ≥ -1.0
- Osteopaenia: T-score -1.0 to -2.5
- Osteoporosis: T-score ≤ -2.5
- Severe osteoporosis: T-score ≤ -2.5 + fragility fracture

**Fracture risk assessment (FRAX)**: Combines BMD with clinical risk factors to estimate 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture (hip, spine, wrist, humerus). Used to guide treatment decisions. Clinical risk factors include: age, sex, prior fragility fracture, parental hip fracture, smoking, alcohol, BMI, glucocorticoid use, rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis.

**Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (GIOP)**: Bone loss occurs rapidly — 5–10% in the first year of prednisolone use. Fracture risk is elevated even with T-scores in the normal range (glucocorticoids impair bone quality beyond BMD). Start bone protection with any dose of prednisolone for >3 months.

### Clinical Presentation

Osteoporosis is asymptomatic until fracture. **Fragility fracture** — fracture from a fall from standing height or less, or no trauma. Classic sites: vertebrae (most common — often silent, discovered incidentally on imaging), hip (highest morbidity and mortality), distal radius/wrist (Colles' fracture), proximal humerus.

**Vertebral fractures**: May present as acute back pain (severe, sharp, localised — from compression fracture) or progressive height loss and kyphosis (from multiple silent fractures — loss of >4 cm from maximum adult height is significant). Vertebral fracture is the strongest predictor of future fracture risk.

**Hip fracture**: ~30% 1-year mortality. Most survivors do not return to pre-fracture function. Pathological fractures (through tumour deposits or metastases) must be distinguished — location (sub-trochanteric is more suspicious for pathological fracture), pain history, bone scan/MRI.

### Diagnosis

**DEXA scan** (dual energy X-ray absorptiometry): Measures BMD at lumbar spine (L1-L4) and femoral neck. Provides T-score (compared with young normal) and Z-score (compared with age-matched population — Z-score <-2.0 suggests secondary cause).

**FRAX calculator**: Input age, sex, BMI, risk factors and BMD → 10-year fracture probability. Guides treatment decision (NICE thresholds for treatment intervention).

**Bloods**: Calcium (exclude hyperparathyroidism and malignancy), phosphate, ALP, 25-OH vitamin D, PTH, TFTs, testosterone/oestradiol, U&E (CKD), FBC, SPEP (myeloma). Urine for Bence-Jones protein if myeloma suspected.

**Lateral spine X-ray or VFA on DEXA**: To identify prevalent vertebral fractures — each vertebral fracture multiplies future fracture risk ~5-fold.

### Management

**Universal measures:**
- **Calcium 1000–1200 mg/day** and **vitamin D 800–1000 IU/day** — essential foundation for all pharmacological treatment (inadequate calcium/vitamin D undermines antiresorptive efficacy)
- Weight-bearing exercise (walking, resistance training) — maintains and modestly improves BMD
- Fall prevention — home safety assessment, balance training, review sedating medications, vision correction, hip protectors
- Smoking cessation, reduce alcohol

**Antiresorptive therapy:**

**Bisphosphonates** (first-line): Alendronate 70 mg weekly (oral) or risedronate 35 mg weekly. Bind hydroxyapatite and inhibit osteoclast activity. Reduce vertebral fracture by ~50%, hip fracture by ~40%.
- Take on an empty stomach with a full glass of water; remain upright for 30 minutes (reduces oesophageal irritation)
- Monitor: renal function (contraindicated if eGFR <30); osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare — avoid dental extractions in patients on long-term bisphosphonates; dental assessment before starting); atypical subtrochanteric femoral fractures (after >5 years — consider "drug holiday")
- IV zoledronate 5 mg annually — for patients intolerant of oral bisphosphonates or with malabsorption

**Denosumab** (anti-RANKL antibody): Subcutaneous 60 mg every 6 months. Inhibits osteoclast differentiation and function. Highly effective — superior to bisphosphonates for glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis. **Rebound resorption on discontinuation** — must transition to bisphosphonate when stopping.

**Anabolic therapy (severe/very high-risk osteoporosis):**
- **Teriparatide** (PTH 1-34): Subcutaneous daily injection for 24 months. Stimulates bone formation — the only approved anabolic agent until recently. For severe osteoporosis (T-score <-3.5, or multiple fractures despite antiresorptive therapy), or GIOP
- **Romosozumab** (anti-sclerostin): Subcutaneous monthly for 12 months — dual action (anabolic + antiresorptive). Rapid BMD gains. Cardiovascular safety signal — avoid in patients with recent MI or stroke.
- **HRT**: For postmenopausal women — most effective for preventing bone loss in early menopause. See reproductive endocrinology chapter.

**Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (NICE/BSR guidance):**
- Oral prednisolone ≥5 mg/day for ≥3 months anticipated → start bone protection
- In patients >70, or prior fragility fracture: start bisphosphonate without waiting for DEXA
- DEXA for younger patients and guide accordingly

## Common Soft Tissue Conditions

### Rotator Cuff Disorders

The rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, subscapularis, teres minor) stabilises the glenohumeral joint and initiates shoulder abduction. **Supraspinatus tendinopathy/partial tear** is the most common — presents with pain in the C5 dermatome (lateral arm), painful arc (60–120° of abduction — the tendon impinges under the acromion), and weakness of abduction. Full-thickness tears produce a drop-arm sign.

Management: Physiotherapy (rotator cuff strengthening, scapular stabilisation), subacromial corticosteroid injection, ultrasound-guided needling. Surgical repair for full-thickness tears in younger patients.

### Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

Compression of the median nerve in the carpal tunnel. Produces paraesthesia and numbness in the lateral 3.5 fingers (thumb, index, middle, lateral half of ring finger), worse at night and with wrist flexion. Associated with RA, hypothyroidism, pregnancy, diabetes, acromegaly.

Tinel's sign (tapping over the carpal tunnel → paraesthesia), Phalen's sign (wrist flexion for 60 seconds → paraesthesia). Confirmed by nerve conduction studies. Management: wrist splinting, corticosteroid injection, surgical decompression (carpal tunnel release) for persistent symptoms.

### Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis)

Tendinopathy at the origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis from the lateral epicondyle. Lateral elbow pain on resisted wrist extension and grip. Treatment: physiotherapy (eccentric exercises), activity modification, corticosteroid injection (short-term relief only), shockwave therapy.

### Plantar Fasciitis

Inflammation at the origin of the plantar fascia at the calcaneus. Severe heel pain on first steps in the morning or after rest ("post-static dyskinesia"). Associated with obesity, flat feet, Achilles tightness, and SpA. Management: stretching programme, orthotic insoles, night splints, corticosteroid injection, shockwave therapy.

### Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)

**First Aid / OHCM high-yield** — an inflammatory condition of middle and elderly age (almost never before 50, peak incidence 70s) characterised by aching and profound stiffness in the shoulder and pelvic girdles.

**Pathophysiology**: Synovitis and bursitis (subacromial and hip bursae) — the pain is periarticular rather than articular. Aetiology is unclear; IL-6 plays a central role. **Strongly associated with giant cell arteritis (GCA) — occurs concurrently in 40–50% of GCA; 15–20% of PMR patients have or develop GCA.** Screen all PMR patients for GCA symptoms (headache, jaw claudication, visual symptoms).

**Clinical features**:
- Bilateral shoulder and pelvic girdle aching — raising arms above head is painful; difficulty rising from a chair
- **Profound morning stiffness lasting ≥45 minutes** — often the dominant complaint
- Constitutional features: fatigue, weight loss, low-grade fever
- **Strikingly elevated ESR** (often >50 mm/hr, frequently >100) and CRP
- Normal CK (distinguishes from inflammatory myopathy — in polymyositis, CK is elevated)
- **No muscle weakness on formal testing** (pain may limit effort but power is preserved — the key distinction from dermatomyositis/polymyositis)

**Diagnosis**: Clinical — no diagnostic test. EULAR/ACR 2012 classification criteria: age ≥50, bilateral shoulder aching, abnormal CRP and/or ESR, plus morning stiffness >45 min, new hip pain/stiffness, negative RF and anti-CCP, and absence of other joint involvement.

**Management**: **Prednisolone 15 mg/day** — dramatic response within 24–48 hours is virtually diagnostic. Failure to respond promptly should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis (consider inflammatory myopathy, myeloma, occult malignancy). Slow taper over 12–24 months; relapse is common. Methotrexate as steroid-sparing agent. Bone protection mandatory.

**FA buzzword**: Elderly woman + bilateral shoulder/hip girdle stiffness + ESR >100 + dramatic response to 15 mg prednisolone = PMR. If she also has headache and jaw claudication → GCA.

### Fibromyalgia

A central sensitisation syndrome — amplified pain processing without peripheral tissue inflammation. The most common cause of widespread musculoskeletal pain in clinical practice. Affects ~2% of the population; female predominance (7:1). Often co-exists with IBS, chronic fatigue syndrome, migraine, and mood disorders.

**Pathophysiology**: Altered central pain processing — functional neuroimaging shows enhanced activation of pain-processing brain regions in response to stimuli that are not painful in healthy controls. Substance P levels in CSF are elevated; descending inhibitory pathways are impaired. This is not psychosomatic — it is a genuine disorder of pain modulation.

**Clinical features** (ACR 2010 diagnostic criteria):
- **Widespread pain index (WPI) ≥7** — pain in ≥7 of 19 specified body areas — and symptom severity score ≥5, OR WPI 3–6 plus symptom severity score ≥9
- Symptoms present for ≥3 months
- No other disorder that would explain the pain
- Key associated symptoms: fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive dysfunction ("fibrofog"), headache, IBS, bladder urgency

**Diagnosis**: Clinical — normal inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP), normal FBC, normal TFTs (exclude hypothyroidism — an important mimic). The absence of objective joint swelling, synovitis, or inflammation distinguishes fibromyalgia from inflammatory arthritis.

**Management**:
- **Patient education**: Explain central sensitisation — legitimises the diagnosis and empowers self-management
- **Aerobic exercise**: The single most evidence-based intervention — graded, progressive; improves pain, fatigue, and function
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**: Addresses maladaptive pain beliefs and coping strategies
- **Pharmacological** (modest benefit): Duloxetine (SNRI), pregabalin/gabapentin (reduce central sensitisation), amitriptyline low-dose at night (sleep + pain). **Opioids and NSAIDs are not effective** and should be avoided.
- Sleep hygiene intervention — non-restorative sleep perpetuates central sensitisation
## Complications

- **Osteoporotic fractures**: vertebral — chronic pain, kyphosis, restrictive lung disease; hip fracture — 30-day mortality 5-10%, 1-year mortality up to 30%, loss of independence
- **Secondary osteoporosis**: untreated underlying disease (coeliac, hypogonadism, hyperparathyroidism) accelerates bone loss despite bisphosphonate therapy
- **Bisphosphonate complications**: osteonecrosis of the jaw (rare, more common with IV zoledronic acid in oncology doses); atypical subtrochanteric femoral fractures (rare, after >5 years)
- **Chronic back pain**: radiculopathy, spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication, cauda equina syndrome (surgical emergency)
- **Inflammatory arthritis untreated**: joint erosion and destruction, functional disability, extra-articular manifestations

## Clinical Insight

Every patient starting long-term steroids (prednisolone ≥5 mg for >3 months) needs bone protection started simultaneously — not at the 3-month review, not when they fracture. Glucocorticoids reduce bone quality faster than BMD measurements reflect. A patient on prednisolone with a T-score of -1.5 has a higher absolute fracture risk than an untreated postmenopausal woman with the same T-score — because glucocorticoids impair bone quality beyond what DEXA captures. FRAX adjusts for this, but the clinical bottom line is: start bisphosphonate or another bone protection agent with the steroid prescription, not after a fracture proves the need.

Knee arthroscopy for osteoarthritis or degenerative meniscal tears is one of the most convincingly overperformed procedures in surgical practice. Multiple high-quality RCTs — FINISH, FIDELITY, and the Kirkley trial — show no benefit over sham surgery or physiotherapy for degenerative meniscal disease in the absence of true mechanical locking. The patient with knee pain and an MRI showing a meniscal tear likely has OA with incidental meniscal degeneration. Arthroscopy will not help and may accelerate OA progression. Physiotherapy is the correct management.

The first step in assessing any joint complaint is to decide: is this inflammatory or mechanical? The answer shapes every subsequent decision — investigations, urgency of referral, and treatment. Morning stiffness lasting >60 minutes, joint warmth, elevated CRP, and improvement with activity all point to inflammation. Morning stiffness lasting <30 minutes, worsening with use, and normal inflammatory markers point to mechanical disease. Getting this right at the first consultation prevents months of inappropriate treatment.
