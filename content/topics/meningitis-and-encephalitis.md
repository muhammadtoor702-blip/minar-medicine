## Overview

Bacterial meningitis is a life-threatening emergency — inflammation of the meninges (pia, arachnoid, dura) caused by bacterial invasion of the subarachnoid space. In children beyond the neonatal period, Neisseria meningitidis (meningococcus) and Streptococcus pneumoniae (pneumococcus) account for the vast majority of cases. Neisseria meningitidis type B is the most common cause in the UK after vaccine introduction against types A, C, W, Y and infant MenB vaccination (introduced 2015). The mortality of untreated bacterial meningitis approaches 100%. Even with treatment, mortality is 5–10% for meningococcal disease and up to 20–30% for pneumococcal meningitis, with significant neurological sequelae in survivors (deafness, cognitive impairment, limb amputation from septicaemia).

Encephalitis — inflammation of the brain parenchyma itself — causes altered consciousness, seizures, and focal deficits, and is most commonly viral. Herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE) is the most common sporadic fatal encephalitis and a treatable emergency. Autoimmune encephalitides (most notably anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis) are increasingly recognised and are often more common than viral causes in adolescents and young adults.

## Pathophysiology

### Bacterial Meningitis

**Route of infection:** Haematogenous spread (most common — colonisation of nasopharynx → bacteraemia → seeding of meninges) or direct extension (sinusitis, mastoiditis, skull fracture).

**Meningeal inflammation cascade:**
1. Bacteria enter subarachnoid space → bacterial cell wall components (lipopolysaccharide in Gram-negatives, teichoic acid in Gram-positives) activate TLRs on microglia and meningeal macrophages
2. Massive cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) → recruitment of neutrophils → purulent CSF
3. **Cerebral oedema** (vasogenic + cytotoxic) → raised ICP → herniation risk
4. **Cerebrovascular inflammation** → arteritis → cortical venous thrombosis → ischaemic injury
5. **Blood-brain barrier breakdown** → electrolyte disturbance, seizures

**Meningococcal septicaemia:** N. meningitidis releases endotoxin → overwhelming complement activation and coagulation cascade → disseminated intravascular coagulation → purpuric/petechial rash (non-blanching — cutaneous haemorrhage from microvascular thrombosis), adrenal haemorrhage (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome), multi-organ failure. A non-blanching rash in a sick child is meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise.

### Age-Specific Causative Organisms

| Age | Common Organisms |
|-----|-----------------|
| Neonates (<28 days) | Group B Streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria monocytogenes |
| 1–3 months | GBS + N. meningitidis + S. pneumoniae |
| 3 months – 18 years | **N. meningitidis**, **S. pneumoniae**, H. influenzae (rare post-Hib vaccine) |
| Immunocompromised/elderly | Listeria monocytogenes — add ampicillin |
| Viral | Enteroviruses (most common overall), HSV-2 (neonates), EBV, mumps |

### Viral Encephalitis — HSV

Herpes simplex virus type 1 (HSV-1) causes most cases of sporadic encephalitis beyond the neonatal period. The virus reactivates from trigeminal ganglia and spreads to temporal lobes via olfactory tracts → **temporal lobe necrosis** → haemorrhagic encephalitis. EEG shows periodic lateralising epileptiform discharges (PLEDs) over temporal lobes. Without aciclovir, HSE mortality is 70%; with treatment, mortality falls to ~20%.

### Anti-NMDA Receptor Encephalitis

The most common autoimmune encephalitis. IgG antibodies against GluN1 subunit of NMDA receptor → receptor internalisation → reduced NMDA signalling in interneurons → disinhibition. Presents in young women/adolescents with: psychiatric prodrome (psychosis, behavioural change) → movement disorders (orofacial dyskinesias, stereotypies) → decreased consciousness → autonomic instability → central hypoventilation. Associated with ovarian teratoma in 50% of adult women (less common in children). CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis + NMDA-receptor antibodies. Treatment: immunotherapy (steroids, IVIG, plasma exchange) ± teratoma removal; second-line rituximab/cyclophosphamide.

## Clinical Presentation

### Classic Triad of Meningitis

**Fever + headache + neck stiffness** — present in only ~45% of adult cases; even less reliable in children and infants.

### Age-Dependent Presentation

**Infants (<18 months):**
- Bulging fontanelle (raised ICP) — highly specific but late sign
- High-pitched or inconsolable cry
- Refusal to feed, vomiting
- Paradoxical irritability (crying when handled — meningeal irritation worsened by movement)
- Drowsiness, poor responsiveness
- NO neck stiffness — unreliable in infants; Kernig's/Brudzinski's often absent

**Children >18 months:**
- Classic triad more likely
- Photophobia, phonophobia
- Kernig's sign: extension of knee with hip flexed causes pain/resistance (meningeal stretch)
- Brudzinski's sign: passive neck flexion causes involuntary knee flexion

### The Non-Blanching Rash — Critical Red Flag

- **Blanching (macular/urticarial):** Viral illness, drug reaction — press a glass → blanches
- **Non-blanching petechiae/purpura:** Cutaneous haemorrhage — does NOT blanch with glass test
- Non-blanching rash + unwell child = **meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise — antibiotics BEFORE LP, BEFORE waiting for results**
- Petechiae may evolve to purpura (coalescing haemorrhage) to ecchymosis within hours

### Signs of Raised ICP (Contraindications to Immediate LP)
- GCS <13 or falling GCS
- Focal neurological deficit
- Papilloedema on fundoscopy
- Abnormal posturing (decerebrate/decorticate)
- Unequal pupils
- Bradycardia + hypertension (Cushing's triad — late, pre-terminal)

### NICE Traffic Light System for Fever

For recognition: any child with fever + non-blanching rash (NOT purpura fulminans), stiff neck, bulging fontanelle, reduced consciousness, or seizure → HIGH RISK → immediate assessment.

## Diagnosis

### CSF Analysis — Lumbar Puncture

LP is the gold standard for diagnosis — but must not delay antibiotics. If any LP contraindications are present, give antibiotics first, obtain CT head, then LP.

| Parameter | Normal | Bacterial | Viral | TB/Fungal |
|-----------|--------|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Appearance | Crystal clear | Turbid/purulent | Clear/slightly turbid | Turbid/xanthochromic |
| WBC | <5/mm³ | 100–10,000+ (neutrophils) | 10–1,000 (lymphocytes) | 100–500 (lymphocytes) |
| Protein | 15–45 mg/dL | **High (>150 mg/dL)** | Normal–mildly high | High |
| Glucose (CSF:serum ratio) | >0.6 | **Low (<0.4)** | Normal (>0.6) | Very low |
| Gram stain | Negative | Positive in 60–80% | Negative | AFB (Ziehl-Neelsen) |
| Opening pressure | 5–20 cmH₂O | High | Normal–mildly high | High |

**CSF glucose interpretation:** Always compare to serum glucose taken simultaneously. CSF glucose <40 mg/dL or CSF:serum ratio <0.4 strongly suggests bacterial aetiology.

### Blood Tests

- **Blood cultures × 2:** Before antibiotics if possible (do NOT delay antibiotics to obtain cultures)
- **Meningococcal PCR (blood + CSF):** Positive in >80% even after antibiotics — essential when Gram stain negative
- **FBC:** Neutrophilia; leukopenia (severe meningococcal septicaemia — poor prognosis)
- **CRP/PCT:** Elevated; PCT >0.5 ng/mL suggests bacterial over viral
- **Coagulation (PT, APTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer):** Assess DIC in meningococcal septicaemia
- **U&E:** SIADH (hyponatraemia common in bacterial meningitis)
- **Blood glucose:** For CSF:serum ratio comparison

### Imaging

**CT head before LP:** Only required if LP contraindications present (GCS <13, focal deficit, papilloedema, seizure, immunocompromised, known CNS lesion). Do NOT routinely delay LP for CT.

**MRI brain:** Better than CT for encephalitis — shows **temporal lobe signal change (T2/FLAIR hyperintensity)** in HSV encephalitis; basal enhancement in TB meningitis.

**EEG in suspected encephalitis:** PLEDs in HSV encephalitis; diffuse slowing in encephalopathy.

## Management

### Immediate Management — The 1-Hour Rule

**Do NOT delay antibiotics for LP.** If LP contraindications exist → give antibiotics → CT head → LP. Studies show antibiotics given 1–2 hours before LP do NOT significantly affect culture sensitivity if PCR is used, but delay kills patients.

### Empirical Antibiotics

**Children 3 months to 50 years — First-line:**
- **Ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg IV (max 4 g)** — covers N. meningitidis and S. pneumoniae; once daily dosing acceptable in meningitis but BD dosing preferred for severity
- Alternative in penicillin allergy: chloramphenicol

**Neonates (<3 months):**
- Cefotaxime + ampicillin — adds Listeria and GBS coverage
- Gentamicin sometimes added for synergy

**Adults >50 years or immunocompromised:** Add **ampicillin** to cover Listeria

**Suspected HSV encephalitis:** **Aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV TID × 14–21 days** — start empirically whenever encephalitis (altered consciousness + fever) is in the differential; do not wait for CSF PCR result

### Dexamethasone

**Indications:** Community-acquired bacterial meningitis, age >3 months
- **Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV QID × 4 days — give with or just before first dose of antibiotics**
- Reduces cytokine-mediated inflammation → reduces cerebral oedema, vasculitis, hearing loss
- **Evidence:** Reduces hearing loss from 26% to 10% in pneumococcal meningitis (van de Beek trial); reduces mortality in adults with S. pneumoniae
- **Do NOT give for meningococcal disease after antibiotics already given** — limited benefit if steroids delayed
- **Do NOT give in neonates** — insufficient evidence, potential harm

### Raised ICP Management

- **Nurse at 30° head elevation** (improves venous drainage without reducing CPP)
- **Avoid hypotension** — MAP must be maintained to ensure CPP
- **Intubation + hyperventilation** (target PaCO₂ 32–35 mmHg) — temporary bridge if herniation imminent
- **Mannitol 0.25–0.5 g/kg IV** or **3% hypertonic saline** — osmotic therapy for acute decompensation
- Neurosurgical input for external ventricular drain if hydrocephalus develops

### Meningococcal Septicaemia — Shock Management

- **Volume resuscitation:** 10–20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl IV boluses titrated to HR, BP, CRT, urine output
- NICE 2014 advises caution with large-volume fluid boluses (FEAST trial) — reassess after each bolus
- **Vasopressors (noradrenaline ± adrenaline)** for refractory shock after adequate fluid resuscitation
- **Ceftriaxone** (as above)
- **FFP/cryoprecipitate/platelets** for DIC coagulopathy
- Paediatric intensive care unit admission

### Chemoprophylaxis for Contacts

**Rifampicin** (or single-dose ciprofloxacin in adults) for close household contacts of confirmed meningococcal disease — reduces nasopharyngeal carriage. Public Health notification mandatory within 24 hours.

## Complications

- **Sensorineural hearing loss:** Most common complication of bacterial meningitis (10–30%); pneumococcal > meningococcal; audiological assessment in ALL survivors
- **Subdural empyema:** Purulent collection between dura and arachnoid — fever failing to resolve + focal deficit → urgent neurosurgical drainage
- **Hydrocephalus:** Meningeal fibrosis → CSF outflow obstruction → VP shunt
- **Cerebral infarction:** Vasculitis of cortical vessels → focal neurological deficit
- **Cognitive impairment/epilepsy:** Post-inflammatory neuronal injury
- **Limb ischaemia/amputation:** DIC + purpura fulminans → peripheral gangrene in meningococcal septicaemia
- **Adrenal insufficiency (Waterhouse-Friderichsen):** Bilateral adrenal haemorrhage in meningococcaemia → acute adrenal crisis — may require hydrocortisone
- **SIADH:** Hyponatraemia → restrict free water; correct sodium cautiously

## Clinical Insight

The glass test is one of the most important pieces of public health education in paediatric emergency medicine — yet it is often misapplied. A blanching rash (viral exanthem, urticaria) disappears under glass pressure because the redness comes from dilated capillaries emptying of blood. A non-blanching rash (petechiae, purpura) does NOT disappear because the colour comes from red cells that have already escaped into the dermis. The critical clinical teaching is that a child with non-blanching rash and fever needs immediate parenteral antibiotics before any other test is performed. Time to antibiotics is the single most modifiable determinant of outcome in meningococcal disease.

The timing of LP relative to antibiotics generates frequent confusion. The correct principle is: **give antibiotics immediately, then decide about LP**. Antibiotics do not significantly reduce CSF culture positivity within the first 1–2 hours, and PCR (meningococcal, pneumococcal, enteroviral, HSV) remains positive even after antibiotics. The only reason to delay LP is the presence of raised ICP signs — in which case CT head first is necessary to rule out a mass lesion before LP.

Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis has transformed the diagnostic approach to encephalitis. Before its discovery (2007), young women presenting with acute psychosis + movement disorders + autonomic instability were frequently labelled with a functional or primary psychiatric disorder. The critical teaching is that **autoimmune encephalitis must be considered in any encephalitis without a clear viral cause**, particularly in adolescent females with psychiatric features. The CSF may be normal in 30% of cases — serum and CSF NMDA-receptor antibodies are required, and treatment with immunotherapy can lead to full recovery.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 4-year-old, 6-hour history of fever/headache/vomiting, non-blanching petechial rash on legs over past hour, increasingly difficult to rouse, GCS 12, HR 148, BP 82/50, CRT 4 seconds.

**Diagnosis: Meningococcal septicaemia + meningitis — medical emergency requiring immediate treatment**

**Step 1 — Immediate: antibiotics before anything else:**
- Non-blanching rash + septic shock → meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise
- **Ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg IV immediately** — do NOT wait for LP, blood cultures, or CT
- If IV access delayed → **IM benzylpenicillin 600 mg** (if child weighs >10 kg) as temporising measure
- Blood cultures should be drawn simultaneously with cannulation but must not delay antibiotics

**Step 2 — Resuscitation:**
- HR 148, BP 82/50, CRT 4s → septic shock
- IV 0.9% NaCl 10 mL/kg bolus × reassess → repeat if needed
- High-flow O₂
- Alert PICU immediately — this child needs intensive care

**Step 3 — LP assessment:**
- GCS 12, shocked → LP CONTRAINDICATED at this time
- Do not perform LP while haemodynamically unstable or with reduced GCS
- LP deferred until stabilised on PICU (if at all — meningococcal PCR on blood will likely give diagnosis)

**Step 4 — Dexamethasone:**
- Age >3 months, bacterial meningitis likely → **dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV with first antibiotic dose**

**Step 5 — Investigations (simultaneously with treatment):**
- Blood cultures, meningococcal PCR, FBC, CRP, U&E (SIADH), LFTs, coagulation (DIC screen), glucose
- Meningococcal PCR will remain positive even after antibiotics

**Step 6 — Public Health:**
- Immediate notification to Health Protection Unit — household contacts require rifampicin chemoprophylaxis

**Exam trigger:** *Non-blanching rash + fever + shocked child* → immediate ceftriaxone IV/IM (do NOT delay for any investigation). The distractor is waiting for LP results or blood cultures before giving antibiotics — this delay is potentially fatal.
