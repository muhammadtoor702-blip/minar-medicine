## Overview

Safeguarding is the process of protecting children from abuse, neglect, exploitation, and harm. Child maltreatment is far more common than most clinicians recognise — approximately 1 in 5 children in high-income countries experience some form of maltreatment. In England, over 50,000 children are on child protection plans at any one time. Non-accidental injury (NAI), also termed physical abuse, is a pattern of inflicted injury that occurs when a caregiver deliberately harms a child. It is a leading cause of preventable child death. The challenge for clinicians is that presentations are frequently disguised as accidents, and the history provided by caregivers may be inconsistent, minimised, or fabricated. Every healthcare professional — not just paediatricians — has a legal and professional duty to act on safeguarding concerns. The Children Act 1989 and 2004, and Working Together to Safeguard Children (2023 revision), provide the statutory framework in England.

## Pathophysiology

### Types of Child Maltreatment

**Physical abuse (NAI):** Deliberate infliction of physical injury. Includes hitting, shaking, burning, biting, poisoning, suffocation, drowning, and fabricated or induced illness.

**Emotional abuse:** Persistent emotional ill-treatment — threats, humiliation, rejection, unrealistic expectations, witnessing domestic violence. Most pervasive form; difficult to identify; often coexists with other abuse.

**Sexual abuse:** Involves a child in sexual activity they do not understand, cannot consent to, and that the law prohibits. Includes contact (assault, rape) and non-contact (exposure to pornography, grooming, online exploitation).

**Neglect:** Persistent failure to meet a child's basic physical and/or psychological needs — failure to provide adequate food, shelter, clothing, supervision, medical care, or emotional nurturing. Most common form of maltreatment; often missed; causes significant long-term developmental harm.

**Fabricated or induced illness (FII):** Caregiver (usually mother) fabricates symptoms/history or deliberately induces illness in a child to obtain medical care. Previously termed Munchausen syndrome by proxy. Presents with unexplained symptoms, multiple investigations, and symptoms only reported by carer (never observed by healthcare staff).

### Mechanisms of Injury in NAI

**Abusive head trauma (AHT) / Shaken Baby Syndrome:**
The infant brain is particularly vulnerable — large head:body ratio, weak neck muscles, unmyelinated brain, bridging veins between cerebral cortex and dural sinuses are susceptible to tearing. Violent acceleration-deceleration (shaking ± impact) → bridging vein rupture → subdural haemorrhage + retinal haemorrhages + diffuse axonal injury. The classic triad (subdural haemorrhage + retinal haemorrhages + encephalopathy) is strongly associated with AHT, though no single finding is pathognomonic.

**Metaphyseal fractures:** The metaphysis (growth plate region) is a zone of rapid bone turnover with relatively weak architecture. Violent traction, twisting, or shaking forces cause avulsion of the metaphyseal corner → "corner fracture" or "bucket-handle fracture." These are highly specific for NAI in infants (<1 year) and are NOT caused by normal handling.

**Rib fractures:** Posterior rib fractures (particularly posterior) in infants are highly specific for NAI — caused by forceful squeezing of the chest (front-to-back compression during shaking or direct impact). Accidental rib fractures in children are rare (unlike adults); require significant trauma (RTC) to occur non-accidentally.

## Clinical Presentation

### Features That Should Raise NAI Concern

**History red flags:**
- History inconsistent with developmental stage (e.g., "rolled off the sofa" in a 4-month-old who cannot roll)
- History changes between accounts or between caregivers
- Delay in seeking medical attention (esp. for significant injury)
- Vague or no explanation offered for significant injury
- Child alleged to have self-inflicted injury inconsistently with their abilities
- Previous attendances with injuries

**Injury patterns specific to NAI:**

| Injury | Feature Suggesting NAI |
|--------|------------------------|
| **Bruising** | Bruises in non-mobile infants (pre-walking children rarely bruise accidentally); bruising on ears, neck, trunk, buttocks, face (accidental bruising = shins, forehead of mobile toddlers); multiple bruises in different stages of healing |
| **Torn frenulum** | Force-feeding or blow to mouth; highly suspicious in infants |
| **Patterned bruising** | Bruises shaped like implement (belt buckle, hand, cord) |
| **Burns** | Immersion scalds (clear waterline, glove/stocking distribution — accidental splash burns are irregular/asymmetric); cigarette burns (circular, punched-out, uniform size) |
| **Spiral femoral fracture** | High-energy rotational force; in non-ambulatory child → NAI until proven otherwise |
| **Metaphyseal fractures** | Highly specific for NAI in infants |
| **Posterior rib fractures** | Highly specific for NAI in infants |
| **Multiple fractures in different stages of healing** | Repeated trauma over time |

**Retinal haemorrhages:** Present in 80–85% of AHT; bilateral, extensive, extending to periphery. Rare causes in children: severe accidental head injury (RTC), birth trauma (resolves by 4–6 weeks), coagulopathy, retinal vasculitis — but in an infant with encephalopathy, bilateral extensive retinal haemorrhages are AHT until disproven.

**Withdrawn/frozen child:** A child who does not cry when examined, avoids eye contact, shows no stranger anxiety, or is excessively compliant may be displaying hyper-vigilant behaviour from chronic fear. Normal developmental stranger anxiety develops by 9 months — its absence in a toddler can be significant.

### Differential Diagnosis — Medical Mimics

Always consider and exclude organic conditions that can mimic NAI:
- **Mongolian blue spots:** Grey-blue pigmentation over sacrum/buttocks in dark-skinned infants — present at birth, flat, do not represent bruising (photograph and document)
- **Osteogenesis imperfecta:** Multiple fractures from minimal trauma — but blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta, family history, bone density on imaging; genetic testing
- **Bleeding disorders** (ITP, haemophilia, Von Willebrand disease): Unusual bruising/haemorrhage → coagulation screen mandatory before concluding bruising is NAI
- **Ehlers-Danlos syndrome:** Skin fragility, easy bruising, joint hypermobility
- **Scurvy:** Periosteal haemorrhage, subperiosteal bruising (rare)
- **Haemolytic uraemic syndrome, Henoch-Schönlein purpura:** Petechiae/purpura from non-traumatic cause

## Diagnosis

### Investigations in Suspected NAI

**Skeletal survey:** Series of plain radiographs of entire skeleton — looking for occult fractures in different stages of healing. **Mandatory in all children <2 years with suspected physical abuse** and in all children <2 years presenting with AHT. Repeat survey at 2 weeks may detect new callus at previously invisible fractures.

**CT head:** Urgent if AHT suspected — identifies subdural haemorrhage, intracerebral injury, cerebral oedema. More sensitive for acute haemorrhage than MRI.

**MRI brain:** After initial CT — superior for timing haemorrhage, identifying diffuse axonal injury, posterior fossa injury. Dating of subdural haematoma is imprecise (important medico-legally).

**Ophthalmology review:** Dilated fundoscopy by paediatric ophthalmologist for retinal haemorrhage assessment — mandatory in all suspected AHT.

**Coagulation screen:** FBC, PT, APTT, fibrinogen, von Willebrand factor, Factor VIII/IX — to exclude bleeding disorder before attributing bruising/haemorrhage to abuse.

**Bone profile, calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, vitamin D:** Exclude metabolic bone disease (rickets) — osteomalacia increases fracture risk.

**Photography:** Document all surface injuries with consent; standard medical photography by medical illustration department or trained clinician.

### Timing of Injuries

Bruise dating by colour is **unreliable** — do not attempt to date bruises by appearance in medico-legal reports. Yellow indicates bruise >18h old (haemoglobin breakdown products), but the converse is not true. Fracture dating by callus formation is more reliable (callus visible 7–10 days after injury).

## Management

### Immediate Safeguarding Response

**The Three Rs in A&E:**
1. **Recognise** — identify the concern
2. **Record** — detailed contemporaneous documentation
3. **Refer** — to paediatric safeguarding team and Children's Social Care

**Do NOT:**
- Confront the alleged perpetrator
- Accuse the carer of abuse
- Promise confidentiality to a child disclosing abuse
- Send the child home with the suspected perpetrator if the child's safety cannot be assured

**Do:**
- Admit the child to a paediatric ward (child is safe; allows further assessment)
- Discuss with the paediatric consultant on call
- Document verbatim what child/carer says (use quotation marks; do not paraphrase)
- Contact the Named Nurse or Named Doctor for Safeguarding
- Inform the GP

### Statutory Process — Child Protection

**Section 47 enquiry (Children Act 1989):** If there is reasonable cause to suspect a child is suffering or likely to suffer significant harm → Local Authority Children's Social Care (CSC) has a duty to make enquiries. Usually a joint investigation with police.

**Initial Child Protection Conference:** Convened within 15 working days of a Section 47 enquiry — professionals from health, education, social work, police present; decides whether to place child on a Child Protection Plan (CPP) under a category: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, neglect.

**Police involvement:** Parallel criminal investigation for suspected NAI — Joint Protocol between NHS, Police, and Social Services. All cases of suspected AHT or serious NAI should be referred to police.

**Safeguarding thresholds:**
- **Section 17 support (Child in Need):** Child unlikely to achieve reasonable standard of health/development without Local Authority support
- **Section 47 investigation:** Reasonable suspicion of significant harm
- **Emergency Protection Order (EPO, Section 44):** Court order allowing immediate removal of child from risk for up to 8 days (extendable)
- **Police Protection Power (Section 46):** Police can remove child to safety for up to 72h without court order

### Documentation

All safeguarding concerns must be documented to the highest standard:
- Date, time, who was present
- Verbatim history from carers (not paraphrase) in quotation marks
- Description of injuries using body map diagram
- Photographs (with consent)
- Assessment findings and differential diagnosis
- Actions taken and who contacted
- Must be legible and signed

## Complications

- **Long-term neurodevelopmental sequelae of AHT:** Cerebral palsy, epilepsy, cognitive impairment, visual impairment — even with survival; up to 50% of AHT survivors have significant neurodevelopmental disability
- **Death from AHT:** Mortality ~25% for severe AHT; leading cause of traumatic death in infants
- **Psychological trauma:** PTSD, attachment disorders, conduct disorder, substance misuse, self-harm in later life
- **Re-abuse risk:** Without intervention, risk of further abuse remains high — protective factors (non-abusing parent, acknowledgement of harm, engagement with services) are key prognostic factors

## Clinical Insight

The most common reason that child abuse is missed is that it is not considered. Studies consistently show that when NAI is on the differential diagnosis, it is investigated appropriately. The failure occurs when the history is plausible, the caregiver appears concerned, and the injury seems minor. The correct mindset is systematic: does this injury pattern fit this history, at this developmental stage, in this child? A spiral femoral fracture in a non-ambulatory toddler does not fit any accidental mechanism — not a fall from a sofa, not rough play, not being dropped. The history must be challenged, internally.

The "sentinel injury" concept is one of the most important advances in child protection medicine. A sentinel injury is a small, seemingly minor injury in a pre-mobile infant (usually bruising, torn frenulum, mouth injury) that precedes serious abuse. Retrospective studies of children who died from AHT show that 25–30% had a sentinel injury identified by a healthcare provider in the preceding weeks — and the opportunity to protect was missed. A single unexplained bruise in a pre-walking infant is a safeguarding concern and warrants a full assessment, not reassurance and discharge.

The duty to safeguard overrides patient confidentiality. This is a legal principle under both the Children Act and GMC guidance — the welfare of the child is paramount. If a clinician believes a child is at risk of significant harm, they must share that information with Children's Social Care regardless of parental or carer objection. The phrase "I can't discuss this without the carer's consent" is both legally incorrect and professionally indefensible in a child protection context.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 2-year-old girl, "fall from sofa," spiral femoral fracture + multiple bruises different stages + torn frenulum + retinal haemorrhages + withdrawn. Mother absent; presented by mother's partner.

**Step 1 — Recognise:**
- Spiral femoral fracture in a pre-ambulatory/early-ambulant toddler + multiple injuries in different stages + torn frenulum + retinal haemorrhages = **high-probability NAI including AHT**
- History (fall from sofa) does not explain the constellation of findings
- Retinal haemorrhages → suspect AHT/abusive head trauma component

**Step 2 — Immediate safety:**
- **Admit to paediatric ward** — child must NOT go home with the alleged perpetrator
- Explain to carer that the injuries require hospital admission for further assessment
- Do NOT accuse; do NOT discharge

**Step 3 — Medical management:**
- Analgesia for femoral fracture
- **CT head urgently** — retinal haemorrhages → exclude subdural haemorrhage/intracranial injury
- **Ophthalmology review** — formal assessment of retinal haemorrhages
- **Skeletal survey** — whole body X-rays; look for occult fractures in different stages of healing
- **Coagulation screen** — exclude bleeding disorder
- **Bone profile + vitamin D** — exclude metabolic bone disease

**Step 4 — Safeguarding:**
- Contact **Named Nurse/Doctor for Safeguarding** immediately
- Contact mother — why is she not present?
- Contact **Children's Social Care — Section 47 enquiry** (significant harm)
- Contact **Police** — joint investigation
- Document verbatim what the partner says in quotation marks; draw body map; arrange photography

**Step 5 — Child Protection Conference:**
- Within 15 working days of Section 47 referral
- Likely to result in Child Protection Plan — physical abuse category
- Consider safety of any siblings

**Exam trigger:** *Pre-ambulatory infant with fracture + multiple bruises + retinal haemorrhages* → NAI/AHT — immediate admission, skeletal survey, CT head, ophthalmology, safeguarding referral. The distractor is accepting the fall history and discharging — this is potentially fatal.
