## Overview

Fluid and electrolyte disorders are among the most common and clinically significant problems in hospital medicine. Sodium dysregulation governs water distribution and cell volume across the entire body — its correction must follow a precise rate to avoid catastrophic neurological injury. Potassium abnormalities carry direct, life-threatening cardiac consequences. A systematic, physiological approach — always asking why the kidney is retaining or losing solute — avoids dangerous empirical treatment. In this patient, thiazide-induced SIADH-like physiology has produced life-threatening hyponatraemia requiring careful correction over 48–72 hours.

---

## Pathophysiology

**Sodium and water regulation:** Serum sodium reflects the ratio of total body sodium to total body water. Hyponatraemia almost always reflects excess water relative to sodium (not sodium deficiency). ADH (antidiuretic hormone/vasopressin) is the primary regulator — it increases water reabsorption in the collecting duct. Any condition that causes ADH secretion despite dilute plasma will cause hyponatraemia.

**Potassium homeostasis:** 98% of body potassium is intracellular. Serum potassium reflects a tiny fraction of total body stores, but it determines the resting membrane potential of excitable cells. The ratio of intracellular to extracellular K+ governs myocardial conduction — small changes in serum K+ produce large changes in cardiac electrophysiology.

**The kidney's role:** In healthy kidneys, excess solute and water are excreted with precision. Electrolyte disorders persist only when the kidney is prevented from correcting them — by volume depletion (preserving sodium over electrolyte balance), drugs (thiazides, SSRIs), hormone excess (SIADH, Conn's), or intrinsic renal failure.

## Sodium Disorders

### Hyponatraemia (Na+ <135 mEq/L)

**Stepwise assessment — ask three questions in sequence:**

**Step 1 — Is the hyponatraemia truly hypotonic?** Measure serum osmolality.
- Isotonic osmolality (280–295): pseudohyponatraemia — severe hyperlipidaemia or hyperproteinaemia diluting the aqueous phase of plasma; sodium itself is normal
- Hypertonic osmolality (>295): hyperglycaemia or mannitol drawing water into plasma and diluting measured sodium; not a water excess problem
- Hypotonic osmolality (<280): true hyponatraemia — proceed to Step 2

**Step 2 — What is the volume status?**

| Volume Status | Urine Na+ | Diagnostic Interpretation |
|--------------|---------|--------------------------|
| Hypovolaemic + urine Na+ <20 mEq/L | Salt lost via gut or skin | Vomiting, diarrhoea, burns, third spacing |
| Hypovolaemic + urine Na+ >20 mEq/L | Salt lost via kidney | Diuretics, Addison's disease, cerebral salt wasting |
| Euvolaemic + urine Na+ >20 mEq/L | Water excess, ADH active | **SIADH** (most common); hypothyroidism; glucocorticoid deficiency |
| Hypervolaemic + urine Na+ <20 mEq/L | Na and water retained; more water than Na | Heart failure, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome |
| Hypervolaemic + urine Na+ >20 mEq/L | Kidney failing to excrete water | CKD, AKI |

**SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH Secretion):**

The most common cause of euvolaemic hypotonic hyponatraemia. Diagnostic criteria: hyponatraemia + low serum osmolality + urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg (inappropriately concentrated — the hallmark) + urine Na+ >20 mEq/L + euvolaemia + no adrenal or thyroid insufficiency.

Causes of SIADH:
- **CNS disease**: meningitis, encephalitis, stroke, SAH, head injury
- **Pulmonary disease**: pneumonia, TB, lung abscess, empyema
- **Malignancy**: small cell lung cancer (paraneoplastic ADH secretion) — check CXR in any unexplained SIADH
- **Drugs** (most common cause in practice): **thiazide diuretics, SSRIs** (the combination is synergistic and common in elderly women), carbamazepine, opiates, PPIs, cyclophosphamide
- **Post-operative state**: pain and nausea both stimulate ADH; hypotonic IV fluids post-operatively in a patient with elevated ADH → dilutional hyponatraemia

**Symptoms of hyponatraemia** correlate with acuity and severity:
- 130–135 mEq/L: usually asymptomatic
- 125–130 mEq/L: nausea, headache, malaise
- 120–125 mEq/L: confusion, lethargy
- <120 mEq/L OR rapid fall: seizures, respiratory arrest, brain herniation, death

**Acute (<48 hours)** versus **chronic (>48 hours)** hyponatraemia is the critical clinical distinction — not because the symptoms differ, but because safe correction rates differ dramatically.

**Treatment — the critical principle: correction rate must never exceed 10 mEq/L per 24 hours**

**Osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** — formerly called central pontine myelinolysis — occurs when sodium is corrected too rapidly in chronic hyponatraemia. In chronic hyponatraemia (>48 hours), brain cells adapt to low sodium by exporting organic osmolytes (myoinositol, taurine, glutamine). Rapid sodium correction reverses this gradient faster than cells can re-import osmolytes — brain cells shrink, causing demyelination of pontine and extrapontine fibres. Clinical features appear 2–5 days after correction: dysarthria, dysphagia, quadriplegia, locked-in syndrome. These are often irreversible. Paradoxically, if overcorrection is detected within 24 hours, re-lowering the sodium with desmopressin + 5% dextrose can prevent ODS.

**Safe correction targets:**
- Maximum 10 mEq/L per 24 hours (8 mEq/L in high-risk: alcohol dependence, malnutrition, severe hypokalaemia, liver disease)
- Not more than 18 mEq/L per 48 hours

**Exception — acute symptomatic hyponatraemia (onset <48 hours, seizures, obtundation):** Rapid correction is safe and necessary. Give 100–150 mL of 3% hypertonic saline IV over 20 minutes, repeat once or twice if still seizing. Target: raise Na+ by 5 mEq/L in the first hour to stop seizures, then slow to safe correction rate.

**Treatment by underlying cause:**
- **SIADH**: fluid restriction to 750–1000 mL/day (first-line); demeclocycline (ADH antagonist at collecting duct — rarely used); tolvaptan (vasopressin V2 receptor antagonist) for severe or refractory SIADH — avoid in liver disease
- **Hypovolaemic hyponatraemia**: 0.9% NaCl (restoring volume suppresses ADH, allowing water excretion; monitor rate carefully)
- **Hypervolaemic hyponatraemia (HF, cirrhosis)**: treat underlying cause; fluid restriction; loop diuretics

### Hypernatraemia (Na+ >145 mEq/L)

Hypernatraemia indicates free water deficit. It is almost always due to inadequate water intake (impaired thirst, inability to access water) or excessive water losses.

**Causes:**
- **Inadequate intake**: ICU patients (inability to drink), dementia, immobility, unconsciousness
- **Extrarenal water loss**: diarrhoea, vomiting, high fever, burns, excessive sweating
- **Renal water loss**: diabetes insipidus (DI)
- **Hypertonic sodium gain**: hypertonic saline, excessive NaHCO₃ administration

**Diabetes insipidus:**
- **Central DI**: ADH deficiency from posterior pituitary or hypothalamic disease (trauma, surgery, tumour, infiltration — sarcoidosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis). Polyuria >3 L/day of dilute urine. Treat with intranasal desmopressin (DDAVP).
- **Nephrogenic DI**: Renal resistance to ADH. Causes: lithium (most common drug cause — damages collecting duct), hypercalcaemia, hypokalaemia, chronic renal disease. Treat with thiazide diuretics (paradoxically — reduce extracellular volume, increasing proximal tubular reabsorption and reducing distal delivery to the ADH-resistant collecting duct).
- **Diagnosis**: Water deprivation test — urine fails to concentrate. Desmopressin challenge distinguishes central (responds) from nephrogenic (does not respond).

**Treatment of hypernatraemia:** Free water replacement. Oral water if the patient can tolerate it. IV: 5% dextrose (free water) or 0.45% saline. Correct at no more than 10–12 mEq/L per day — rapid correction causes cerebral oedema (brain cells that adapted to hypertonicity by accumulating organic osmolytes swell rapidly when sodium falls).

## Potassium Disorders

### Hypokalaemia (K+ <3.5 mEq/L)

Hypokalaemia hyperpolarises the cardiac membrane, increasing the excitation threshold. Mild hypokalaemia causes fatigue and weakness; severe hypokalaemia causes life-threatening arrhythmias and rhabdomyolysis.

**ECG changes (in order of severity):** Flattening/inversion of T waves → prominent U waves (positive deflection after T wave, best seen in V2–V3) → prolonged QU interval → ST depression → widened QRS → VF.

**Causes:**

| Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|---------|
| Transcellular shift (K+ into cells) | Insulin, catecholamines (salbutamol, adrenaline), alkalosis, hypokalaemic periodic paralysis |
| GI loss | Vomiting (HCl loss → alkalosis → K+ shift into cells; also volume contraction stimulating aldosterone), diarrhoea, fistulae, laxative abuse |
| Renal loss | **Loop and thiazide diuretics** (most common), Conn's syndrome (primary hyperaldosteronism), Cushing's, Bartter syndrome, Gitelman syndrome, hypomagnesaemia, RTA type 1/2 |
| Inadequate intake | Eating disorders, prolonged fasting (rare in isolation) |

**Hypomagnesaemia perpetuates hypokalaemia** by increasing renal K+ wasting through inhibition of ROMK channels in the distal tubule. Any hypokalaemia that fails to correct with K+ replacement should prompt serum magnesium measurement and replacement.

**Treatment:**
- Mild (3.0–3.5 mEq/L): oral potassium chloride
- Moderate (2.5–3.0 mEq/L): oral or IV, depending on symptoms
- Severe (<2.5 mEq/L or ECG changes): IV KCl. **Maximum safe IV rate: 20 mEq/hour via central line** (10 mEq/hour peripherally). **Never give as a bolus** — concentrated KCl as a bolus causes immediate cardiac arrest. Always dilute adequately and give with cardiac monitoring.
- Replace magnesium concurrently if deficient

### Hyperkalaemia (K+ >5.5 mEq/L)

The most immediately dangerous electrolyte disorder — the cardiac membrane becomes unstable, predisposing to fatal arrhythmias.

**ECG changes (in order of severity):** Peaked (tented) T waves → PR prolongation → flattened P waves → widened QRS → sine wave pattern → VF or asystole.

**Causes:**

| Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|---------|
| Increased release | Rhabdomyolysis, haemolysis, tumour lysis syndrome, blood transfusion |
| Transcellular shift (K+ out of cells) | Acidosis, insulin deficiency (DKA), beta-blockers, digoxin toxicity, succinylcholine, hyperkalaemic periodic paralysis |
| Reduced renal excretion | AKI/CKD, ACEi/ARB, spironolactone, NSAIDs, Addison's disease, type 4 RTA (hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism — diabetic nephropathy) |
| Pseudohyperkalaemia | Haemolysis of sample, prolonged tourniquet, extreme thrombocytosis/leucocytosis — always repeat if unexpected |

**Emergency treatment protocol (K+ ≥6.5 mEq/L OR ECG changes):**

| Step | Drug | Dose | Mechanism | Onset |
|------|------|------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | **Calcium chloride 10%** (preferred) OR calcium gluconate 10% | 10 mL IV (CaCl₂) OR 20–30 mL IV (gluconate) | Membrane stabilisation — does NOT lower K+ | Seconds |
| 2 | Insulin + glucose | 10 U insulin + 125 mL 20% glucose IV | Shifts K+ into cells via Na+/K+-ATPase | 15–30 min |
| 3 | Nebulised salbutamol | 10–20 mg | Additional cellular K+ shift via beta-2 receptor | 15–30 min |
| 4 | **Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC/Lokelma) 10 g TDS** OR patiromer | Oral | Gut K+ binder — removes K+ from body | SZC: ~1h; patiromer: 4–7h |
| 5 | Haemodialysis | — | Definitive K+ removal | Immediate |

Calcium chloride 10% contains 272 mg elemental calcium per 10 mL; calcium gluconate 10% contains 93 mg per 10 mL. Calcium chloride is more caustic (central line or wide-bore IV required) but provides faster, more reliable membrane stabilisation — use it when the ECG shows broad complex changes and time matters. SZC is preferred over patiromer in the acute setting due to faster onset.

**Sodium bicarbonate** is no longer routinely recommended for isolated hyperkalaemia — it has modest, unpredictable benefit in non-acidotic patients. Reserve for severe combined metabolic acidosis and hyperkalaemia.

## Calcium Disorders

### Hypercalcaemia (Ca²+ >10.5 mg/dL)

**Causes:** Primary hyperparathyroidism (most common in outpatients — usually asymptomatic, discovered incidentally); malignancy (most common in inpatients — bone metastases, PTHrP secretion from squamous cell lung/head and neck cancers, myeloma); sarcoidosis (extrarenal 1-alpha-hydroxylase in granulomas); vitamin D toxicity; thiazide diuretics; immobility; milk-alkali syndrome.

**Symptoms — "bones, stones, groans, and psychic moans":**
- Bones: osteitis fibrosa cystica (hyperparathyroidism — subperiosteal erosions of radial border of middle phalanges)
- Stones: nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis, polyuria/polydipsia (nephrogenic DI from hypercalcaemia)
- Groans: nausea, vomiting, constipation, anorexia, pancreatitis
- Psychic moans: confusion, depression, psychosis, coma in severe hypercalcaemia

**ECG:** Shortened QT interval (opposite of hypocalcaemia).

**Treatment of hypercalcaemic crisis (Ca²+ >14 mg/dL or symptomatic):**
1. IV 0.9% NaCl — aggressive rehydration (often 4–6 L/day); volume expansion increases urinary calcium excretion
2. IV furosemide — only after adequate rehydration (saline diuresis enhances calcium excretion; furosemide without volume replacement worsens hypercalcaemia)
3. IV bisphosphonates — zoledronate 4 mg IV over 15 minutes (inhibits osteoclast bone resorption; onset 2–4 days; most effective for malignancy-associated hypercalcaemia)
4. Calcitonin — rapid onset (hours) but tachyphylaxis develops within 48 hours; useful as bridge while bisphosphonate takes effect
5. Hydrocortisone — for sarcoidosis or haematological malignancy

### Hypocalcaemia (Ca²+ <8.5 mg/dL)

**Causes:** Hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroid or parathyroid surgery — most common surgical cause), vitamin D deficiency, CKD (reduced 1-alpha-hydroxylation), hypomagnesaemia (impairs PTH secretion), acute pancreatitis (calcium chelated by lipolytic fat acids), rhabdomyolysis (Ca²+ deposited in damaged muscle), massive blood transfusion (citrate chelation).

**Symptoms:** Perioral tingling, paraesthesiae in hands/feet, muscle cramps. Severe: carpopedal spasm, laryngospasm, seizures.
- **Chvostek's sign**: facial twitching on tapping the facial nerve anterior to the ear
- **Trousseau's sign**: carpopedal spasm with BP cuff inflated above systolic for 3 minutes (more specific)

**ECG:** Prolonged QT interval.

**Treatment:** Mild/asymptomatic: oral calcium carbonate + vitamin D. Severe/symptomatic: IV 10% calcium gluconate 10 mL over 10 minutes, then infusion. Always check and correct magnesium — hypomagnesaemia prevents PTH secretion and causes refractory hypocalcaemia.

## Intravenous Fluid Therapy Principles

### Crystalloid Composition

**0.9% Normal saline:** Na+ 154 mEq/L, Cl− 154 mEq/L. No potassium, no buffer. Supraphysiological chloride causes hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis with large volumes. Preferred for hypovolaemia with hyponatraemia or hypochloraemic alkalosis.

**Hartmann's (compound sodium lactate):** Balanced crystalloid — Na+ 131, K+ 5, Ca2+ 2 mEq/L, HCO₃ equivalent 29 mEq/L, Cl− 111 mEq/L. Preferred for large-volume resuscitation (less acidosis, closer to plasma composition). Do not use in hyperkalaemia.

**5% Dextrose:** Effectively free water once glucose is metabolised. Used for hypernatraemia correction and maintenance fluids. Not a resuscitation fluid — distributes throughout total body water, not the intravascular space. 1 litre of 5% dextrose raises plasma Na+ by only ~1 mEq/L in a 70 kg adult.

**0.45% saline:** Half-normal saline — hypotonic; provides both free water and some sodium. Used in hypernatraemia and DI correction.

### Assessing Fluid Status Before Prescribing

- **Euvolaemic patient with adequate oral intake**: no IV fluids needed
- **Signs of hypovolaemia** (tachycardia, hypotension, oliguria, dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, postural drop): IV crystalloid bolus 500 mL over 15 minutes; reassess JVP and urine output
- **Signs of hypervolaemia** (raised JVP, pulmonary oedema, peripheral oedema, S3 gallop): restrict IV fluids; diuretics if indicated

## Complications

- **Rapid overcorrection of hyponatraemia**: osmotic demyelination syndrome — quadriplegia, pseudobulbar palsy; irreversible; correction must not exceed 10 mEq/L per 24 hours
- **Severe hyponatraemia (Na+ <120 mEq/L)**: cerebral oedema, seizures, coma, uncal herniation, death
- **Rapid overcorrection of hypernatraemia**: cerebral oedema — correct at ≤10–12 mEq/L per day
- **Hyperkalaemia (K+ ≥6.5 mEq/L or ECG changes)**: VF, asystole — immediately life-threatening
- **Severe hypokalaemia (<2.5 mEq/L)**: rhabdomyolysis, ascending paralysis, respiratory failure, malignant arrhythmias, digoxin toxicity potentiation
- **Hypercalcaemic crisis**: AKI, nephrocalcinosis, pancreatitis, cardiac arrest

## Clinical Insight

Thiazide diuretics and SSRIs are the two most common drug causes of SIADH — and they are frequently prescribed together in elderly women for hypertension and depression. The combination produces synergistic hyponatraemia: thiazides block NaCl reabsorption in the distal tubule while reducing urinary dilution capacity; SSRIs independently stimulate ADH release. When an elderly woman on both drugs presents confused, the sodium is the first number to check.

Osmotic demyelination is irreversible once established. The only treatment is prevention — correct sodium at ≤10 mEq/L per 24 hours without exception. If overcorrection occurs, act within 24 hours: desmopressin 2 mcg IV every 8 hours + 5% dextrose infusion to re-lower the sodium back toward the starting point can abort demyelination. After 24 hours, this window closes.

Never bolus potassium intravenously. Concentrated KCl delivered as a bolus causes immediate cardiac arrest by directly depolarising the cardiac membrane. Potassium must always be diluted and given as a slow infusion with continuous cardiac monitoring. This is one of the most consequential drug errors in hospital medicine — it appears on never-event lists internationally and still occurs.
