## Overview

Pericardial disease encompasses a spectrum of conditions affecting the two-layered fibroserous sac surrounding the heart. The pericardium normally contains 15–50 mL of serous fluid. The three major clinical entities — acute pericarditis, pericardial effusion with tamponade, and constrictive pericarditis — represent a continuum of inflammation, fluid accumulation, and fibrosis. Each has a distinct presentation, investigation, and management approach, though they frequently coexist or progress from one to another.

---

## Acute Pericarditis

### Pathophysiology

Acute pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium, often involving the superficial epicardial myocardium (myopericarditis). The fibrinous exudate produced by inflamed pericardial layers creates the characteristic friction rub as layers move against one another. If inflammation is prolonged or recurrent, fibrosis and calcification can develop, progressing to constrictive pericarditis.

**Causes:**

| Category | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| **Idiopathic** | ~80% of cases — presumed viral, often not investigated further |
| Viral | Coxsackie, echovirus, EBV, CMV, adenovirus, mumps, varicella, HIV |
| Bacterial | TB (most common bacterial cause worldwide); Lyme disease; Q fever; pneumonia; rheumatic fever; Staph, Strep, Mycoplasma, Legionella |
| Autoimmune | SLE, RA; vasculitides (Behçet's, Takayasu); IBD; sarcoidosis; amyloidosis |
| Post-cardiac injury | Dressler's syndrome (post-MI, post-cardiac surgery, post-pacemaker insertion) |
| Metabolic | Uraemia (dialysis treats this; NSAIDs ineffective); hypothyroidism; anorexia nervosa |
| Drugs | Procainamide, hydralazine, penicillin, isoniazid, chemotherapy |
| Others | Malignancy (breast, lung, lymphoma); radiotherapy; trauma; surgery; chronic heart failure |

### Clinical Presentation

**Characteristic features:**
- Central chest pain, sharp in quality, worse on **inspiration** and **lying flat**, relieved by **leaning forward** — this positional behaviour distinguishes pericarditis from other causes of chest pain
- **Pericardial friction rub** — three-component scratching sound (atrial systole, ventricular systole, early ventricular filling). Best heard at the lower left sternal edge with the patient sitting forward. Described classically as "leather rubbing on leather." It can be evanescent — may be present at one examination and gone at the next.
- Fever may occur
- Look for signs of a pericardial effusion or tamponade (see later)

### Diagnosis

**Diagnosis requires at least two of four criteria:**
1. Pericarditic chest pain (sharp, positional)
2. Pericardial friction rub
3. Characteristic ECG changes
4. Pericardial effusion on echocardiogram

**ECG changes — classic but present in only ~90%** (10% may be normal or non-specific):
- **Diffuse concave-up (saddle-shaped) ST elevation** in most leads — classically spares aVR and V1
- **PR depression** in the same leads (PR elevation in aVR — reciprocal)
- No reciprocal ST depression (distinguishes from STEMI)

**Distinguishing pericarditis from STEMI:**

| Feature | Pericarditis | STEMI |
|---------|-------------|-------|
| ST morphology | Concave up (saddle-shaped) | Convex up (dome-shaped) |
| Distribution | Diffuse (all territories) | Focal (one vascular territory) |
| PR depression | Present (in most leads) | Absent |
| Reciprocal ST depression | Absent | Present (opposite leads) |
| Progression | ST resolves → T-wave inversion globally | Progressive Q waves in infarct territory |

**Blood tests:** FBC, ESR, CRP (elevated in pericarditis — useful for monitoring treatment), troponin (may be raised if myopericarditis), U&E, and tests directed at likely aetiology.

**Echo** if a pericardial effusion is suspected on CXR (cardiomegaly) or clinically.

**When to investigate further for cause:** ESC guidelines recommend hospital admission and aetiology search for any predictor of poor prognosis (see below). Otherwise, in young patients with a typical viral prodrome, pericarditis can be treated empirically.

### Management

**First-line: NSAIDs + Colchicine**
- **Ibuprofen 600mg three times daily** (preferred over indomethacin because of superior safety profile) for 1–2 weeks, with a PPI for gastric protection. Aspirin 750–1000mg TDS is an alternative, especially preferred post-MI because of its antiplatelet properties.
- **Colchicine 0.5mg twice daily for 3 months** — mandatory alongside NSAIDs. The COPE trial (2005) and ICAP trial (2013) demonstrated that adding colchicine halves the recurrence rate and reduces the risk of persistent symptoms. This is not optional.
- **Restrict strenuous physical activity** until completely asymptomatic and CRP has normalised — risk of myopericarditis and arrhythmia during exertion.

**Special circumstances:**
- **Post-MI pericarditis (early, within 1 week):** aspirin (preferred as already indicated for ACS) + colchicine. Avoid NSAIDs — they impair myocardial scar formation and increase the risk of ventricular aneurysm and rupture.
- **Uraemic pericarditis:** dialysis is the treatment. NSAIDs are ineffective.
- **Bacterial pericarditis (purulent):** IV antibiotics + urgent surgical drainage. High mortality if not recognised.
- **Steroids** — only if NSAIDs are contraindicated (significant CKD, NSAID allergy), or the aetiology is confirmed autoimmune. Corticosteroids increase the risk of recurrence and should not be used routinely in viral/idiopathic pericarditis.
- **Anakinra** (IL-1 receptor antagonist) — for corticosteroid-dependent recurrent pericarditis refractory to colchicine (supported by the AIRTRIP trial).

**Predictors of poor prognosis** — admit and investigate these patients:

| Major | Minor |
|-------|-------|
| Fever >38°C | Myopericarditis |
| Subacute onset (days to weeks of symptoms before diagnosis) | Immunosuppressed patients |
| Large pericardial effusion or tamponade | On anticoagulants |
| Lack of response after 1 week of NSAID therapy | History of trauma |

## Pericardial Effusion

### Pathophysiology

The pericardium can accommodate slowly accumulating fluid up to 1–2 litres without significant haemodynamic compromise, because gradual distension allows the pericardium to stretch. In contrast, even 150–200 mL accumulating rapidly (as in haemopericardium from trauma or aortic dissection) raises intrapericardial pressure above diastolic filling pressure, compressing the cardiac chambers and causing tamponade.

**Causes:** Pericarditis; myocardial rupture (haemopericardium from stab wound, post-MI free wall rupture); aortic dissection; malignancy; filling with pus (purulent pericarditis); iatrogenic (central line, pacemaker lead).

### Clinical Presentation

- Dyspnoea, chest pain, signs of local compression: hiccoughs (phrenic nerve), nausea (diaphragm compression), bronchial breathing at the left base — **Ewart's sign**, from compression of the left lower lobe
- Muffled heart sounds
- **CXR:** enlarged, globular "flask-shaped" heart if effusion >300mL
- **ECG:** low-voltage QRS complexes; **electrical alternans** (alternating QRS axis from the heart swinging in the fluid) — highly suggestive of significant effusion
- **Echo** is diagnostic — echo-free zone surrounding the heart; identifies diastolic collapse of RA/RV (tamponade)

### Cardiac Tamponade

**Beck's triad:**
1. Hypotension (falling BP)
2. Elevated JVP (rising JVP)
3. Muffled heart sounds

In practice, Beck's triad is present in only a minority of cases. Clinical suspicion is based on the combination of rising JVP, hypotension, tachycardia, and a plausible cause (recent procedure, malignancy, infection, post-MI day 5–7).

**Additional features:**
- Sinus tachycardia (compensatory)
- Pulsus paradoxus — exaggerated fall in systolic BP >10 mmHg during inspiration. Mechanism: inspiratory RV expansion displaces the interventricular septum toward the LV, further reducing LV stroke volume.
- Kussmaul's sign — paradoxical rise in JVP during inspiration (may also occur in tamponade, though more characteristic of constrictive pericarditis)
- **Echo is diagnostic:** echo-free zone >2cm around the heart (>1cm if acute); diastolic collapse of right atrium then right ventricle (in sequence, as pressure builds)

### Management of Tamponade

- IV fluids — tamponade is preload-dependent; the only way to maintain output while awaiting drainage. Avoid diuretics and nitrates.
- **Urgent pericardiocentesis** — for non-haemorrhagic tamponade. Insert a needle subxiphoid, aiming toward the left shoulder, under ECG or echo guidance. Even small-volume drainage causes dramatic haemodynamic improvement.
- **Surgical drainage** — for haemorrhagic tamponade (trauma, aortic dissection) or recurrent effusion requiring pericardial window.
- Send pericardial fluid for culture, Ziehl-Neelsen stain and TB culture, and cytology.

## Constrictive Pericarditis

### Pathophysiology

Fibrous scarring and calcification of the pericardium following chronic inflammation creates a rigid, non-compliant shell encasing the heart. Ventricular filling proceeds rapidly at first (the pericardium is slack in early diastole), then abruptly halts when the ventricles meet the stiff pericardium. This produces characteristic **"dip-plateau" or "square-root" haemodynamics** — rapid early filling then an abrupt plateau. All four chamber filling pressures equalise at an elevated level, predominantly manifesting as right heart failure.

**Causes:** Often unknown in the UK; elsewhere TB (most common cause worldwide), or following any pericarditis, post-cardiac surgery, or post-radiotherapy (can develop years later).

### Clinical Presentation

- **Progressive right heart failure** — elevated JVP with prominent x and y descents; hepatosplenomegaly; peripheral oedema; ascites
- **Kussmaul's sign** — paradoxical rise in JVP with inspiration. The rigid pericardium prevents the RV from expanding during inspiration, so increased venous return backs up into the jugular veins instead.
- **Pericardial knock** — early diastolic sound coinciding with abrupt cessation of rapid ventricular filling; high-pitched and earlier than S3
- Soft, diffuse apex beat; quiet heart sounds
- Possible S3

**Investigation:**
- **CXR** — small heart; pericardial calcification (suggests constrictive)
- **CT or MRI** — best for distinguishing constrictive pericarditis from restrictive cardiomyopathy (the critical differential)
- **Echo** — septal bounce; dilated IVC; thickened pericardium
- **Cardiac catheterisation** — confirms equalisation of filling pressures; dip-plateau pattern

### Management

**Pericardiectomy** is the definitive treatment — surgical excision of the pericardium. Medical management (diuretics for fluid overload, treat the underlying cause) provides symptomatic relief while awaiting surgery. Diuresis must be cautious — diastolic filling is preload-dependent.

**Distinguishing tamponade from constrictive pericarditis:**

| Feature | Tamponade | Constrictive Pericarditis |
|---------|-----------|--------------------------|
| Pulsus paradoxus | Present | Absent |
| Kussmaul's sign | Absent (usually) | Present |
| JVP y-descent | Blunted | Prominent |
| Pericardial knock | Absent | Present |
| Pericardial calcification | Absent | Often present |
| CXR heart size | Large | Small |
| Echo finding | Fluid around heart | Thickened pericardium, septal bounce |

## Dressler's Syndrome

Dressler's syndrome develops **2–10 weeks** after an MI, cardiac surgery, or even pacemaker insertion. It is thought to result from autoantibody formation against myocardial antigens exposed by the initial injury. The mechanism is similar to post-pericardiotomy syndrome after cardiac surgery.

**Presentation:** Recurrent fever and chest pain; pleural or pericardial friction rub from serositis. Cardiac tamponade may occur.

**Management:** Aspirin, NSAIDs, or steroids. **Avoid anticoagulants** if tamponade is suspected — haemopericardium risk.

## Complications

- **Cardiac tamponade** — obstructive shock, cardiac arrest; emergency pericardiocentesis is life-saving
- **Recurrent pericarditis** — affects 15–30% after a first episode; multiple recurrences significantly impair quality of life; refractory disease may require pericardiectomy
- **Constrictive pericarditis** — progressive right heart failure, hepatic congestion, ascites, protein-losing enteropathy; requires pericardiectomy
- **Myopericarditis** — ventricular arrhythmias, LV dysfunction, sudden death during exercise (hence sport restriction during acute pericarditis)
- **Purulent pericarditis** — rare but carries very high mortality; requires emergency surgical drainage

## Clinical Insight

Colchicine is not optional. Adding it to NSAIDs from the outset halves the recurrence rate. Every patient with pericarditis who can tolerate it should receive colchicine for three months regardless of whether symptoms resolve rapidly.

Beck's triad is present in only a minority of tamponade cases. The clinical diagnosis is made at the bedside by the combination of rising JVP, hypotension, tachycardia, and a plausible cause. Waiting for all three classic components before ordering an echo or calling for pericardiocentesis is dangerous.

The most important distinction in pericardial disease is between tamponade (fluid under pressure) and constrictive pericarditis (rigid shell). Both cause elevated JVP and right heart failure, but their haemodynamics are mirror images: tamponade has pulsus paradoxus and blunted y-descent; constrictive has Kussmaul's sign and prominent y-descent. Pericardiocentesis cures tamponade but has no role in constrictive pericarditis, where pericardiectomy is required.
