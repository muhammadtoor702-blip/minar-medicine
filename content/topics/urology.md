## Overview

Urology encompasses diseases of the urinary tract (kidneys, ureters, bladder, urethra) and the male reproductive system (prostate, testes, penis). The spectrum in acute surgical practice ranges from the immediately painful (renal colic — one of the most severe pains in medicine) to the slowly progressive and potentially fatal (prostate cancer, renal cell carcinoma). The ED physician must distinguish renal colic (self-limiting or requiring interventional urology) from the urological emergency of an infected obstructed ureter (septic, immediately life-threatening) — a distinction that requires both clinical assessment and appropriate imaging.

## Pathophysiology

### Urinary Calculi — Formation and Obstruction

**Stone composition:** Calcium oxalate (70–80% — most common, radio-opaque on plain X-ray); calcium phosphate (10%); struvite (magnesium ammonium phosphate — infection stones, staghorn calculi, radio-opaque, formed by urease-producing organisms: Proteus, Klebsiella); uric acid (5–10% — radiolucent on plain X-ray, seen on CT; associated with hyperuricaemia, gout, low urine pH); cystine (rare, autosomal recessive cystinuria).

**Risk factors:** Dehydration (concentrated urine), hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hyperuricosuria, low urinary citrate (inhibitor of crystal formation), anatomical abnormality (horseshoe kidney, UPJO), recurrent UTIs.

**Obstruction mechanism:** Stone lodges at a natural narrowing: (1) pelvic-ureteric junction (PUJ); (2) crossing of the ureter over the iliac vessels; (3) vesicoureteric junction (VUJ) — the tightest. Obstruction → increased intraluminal pressure → ureteral smooth muscle spasm (prostaglandin-mediated) → intense colicky pain. If obstruction is combined with urinary infection → infected obstructed ureter → **pyonephrosis** → septic shock (a urological emergency).

### Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

Progressive enlargement of the transitional zone of the prostate from age 50 onwards (90% of men have histological BPH by age 85). The enlarged prostate compresses the urethra → increased bladder outflow resistance → detrusor hypertrophy (to overcome resistance) → eventually detrusor failure → urinary retention. Bladder detrusor also develops overactivity (detrusor overactivity) → urgency, frequency, nocturia.

**Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms (LUTS) classification:**
- **Storage symptoms** (irritative — from detrusor overactivity): frequency, urgency, nocturia, urge incontinence
- **Voiding symptoms** (obstructive — from outflow obstruction): poor stream, hesitancy, incomplete emptying, terminal dribbling, straining

### Prostate Cancer Biology

**Androgen-dependent growth:** Prostate cancer cells express androgen receptors (AR) → driven by testosterone (converted to DHT by 5-alpha-reductase in prostate cells). This is the basis for androgen deprivation therapy (ADT). Over time, castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) develops — AR amplification, AR splice variants (AR-V7), bypass signalling → growth despite low testosterone.

## Clinical Presentation

### Renal Colic

**Presentation:** Sudden-onset severe loin-to-groin pain (follows the line of the ureter); colicky (waxes and wanes as the stone moves); patient cannot find a comfortable position (distinguishes from peritonism, where movement exacerbates pain). Associated: haematuria (macroscopic or microscopic), nausea/vomiting, urinary urgency if stone is at the VUJ (irritates the detrusor).

**Urological emergency: Infected obstructed ureter (pyonephrosis):**
- Same colicky pain PLUS fever/rigors + elevated WBC/CRP = infected obstruction
- Organism trapped behind the obstruction replicates without antibiotic penetration (poor drainage) → rapid progression to urosepsis and death
- **Emergency decompression required within hours** (percutaneous nephrostomy or ureteric stent + IV antibiotics)
- Any patient with renal colic + fever = infected obstructed ureter until proven otherwise

### Acute Urinary Retention (AUR)

Sudden inability to void despite a full bladder. Painful (acute) vs. painless (chronic with overflow). Male, age >60 with preceding LUTS = BPH most common cause. Other causes: urethral stricture, pelvic mass, medications (anticholinergics, opioids, anaesthesia), neurological (MS, cauda equina syndrome).

**Cauda equina syndrome:** Saddle anaesthesia + bilateral leg weakness + urinary retention/incontinence + reduced anal tone = **surgical emergency** — spinal cord compression at L2–S4; MRI spine urgently; neurosurgical decompression.

**Management:** **Urinary catheterisation** (urethral 14–16 Fr; suprapubic if urethral route fails). Monitor for post-obstructive diuresis (kidneys that have been obstructed for days may excrete massive volumes on decompression → dangerous electrolyte loss → IV fluid replacement).

### Prostate Cancer

**Risk factors:** Age (most important), Black ethnicity (2× higher risk vs. White), family history (first-degree relative → 2× risk; BRCA2 mutation → 2–9× higher risk of aggressive prostate cancer).

**Presentation:** Early (localised): often asymptomatic; detected by raised PSA or DRE. Advanced: LUTS (urethral compression), haematuria, bone pain (skeletal metastases — predominantly blastic; spine, pelvis, ribs, femur), neurological symptoms (spinal cord compression from vertebral metastases — emergency).

**PSA (prostate-specific antigen):** Serine protease produced exclusively by prostate epithelium; elevated by: cancer, BPH, prostatitis, UTI, catheterisation, DRE. PSA >10 ng/mL = high risk of significant cancer; PSA 4–10 ng/mL = "grey zone"; PSA <4 ng/mL = low risk but does not exclude cancer. PSA density (PSA/prostate volume) and PSA velocity (rate of rise) improve specificity.

**MRI prostate (mpMRI):** Now the standard before biopsy — identifies lesions within the prostate (PIRADS score 1–5; PIRADS ≥3 = suspicious → biopsy). Transperineal template biopsy under GA (preferred over transrectal — lower sepsis risk).

**Gleason grade and grade group:** Pathological grading of adenocarcinoma aggressiveness (1–5, summed from two most prevalent patterns). Grade group 1 = Gleason 6 (least aggressive); Grade group 5 = Gleason 9–10 (most aggressive). Grade group guides management choice.

### Renal Cell Carcinoma (RCC)

**Classic triad** (present in <10% at diagnosis): haematuria + flank pain + palpable flank mass = advanced disease. Most commonly detected incidentally on USS/CT for another reason. Risk factors: smoking (most important), obesity, hypertension, VHL syndrome (clear cell RCC).

**Paraneoplastic syndromes** (10–40%): hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), polycythaemia (ectopic EPO production), hypertension (ectopic renin), fever/weight loss.

### Bladder Cancer

**Transitional cell carcinoma (TCC/urothelial carcinoma):** 90% of bladder cancers. Risk factors: smoking (most important modifiable risk — 3× increased risk; synergistic with carcinogen exposure), occupational exposure (aromatic amines: dye, rubber, textile workers — historically very important), schistosomiasis (squamous cell carcinoma of bladder in endemic areas — Egypt).

**Presentation:** **Painless haematuria** (macroscopic or microscopic) — the cardinal symptom; must be investigated urgently in all adults over 45 (NICE 2WW referral criteria). Also: irritative LUTS (frequency, urgency) from tumour mass or CIS.

## Diagnosis

### Urinary Stones

**CT KUB (non-contrast CT of kidneys, ureters, bladder):** Gold standard for urolithiasis — sensitivity >95%, detects all stone types (including radiolucent uric acid stones); identifies stone size, location, and degree of hydronephrosis. **Hounsfield units (HU):** Stone density on CT (low HU = uric acid stones, amenable to medical dissolution; high HU = calcium stones, require lithotripsy or surgery).

**Ultrasound KUB:** Alternative first-line in pregnancy (no radiation); good for hydronephrosis but misses many ureteral stones.

**Plain KUB X-ray:** Only detects radio-opaque stones (calcium, struvite) — misses 10–30% of clinically significant stones; used for follow-up of known radio-opaque stones.

**IVU (intravenous urogram):** Largely replaced by CT; historical gold standard.

**Bloods:** FBC, U&E, creatinine (AKI from bilateral obstruction or solitary kidney), CRP (infection), uric acid; urine pH (alkaline = struvite infection; very acidic = uric acid stones); MSU + culture.

### Prostate Cancer

**PSA + DRE → mpMRI (if PSA raised or suspicious DRE) → transperineal biopsy (if mpMRI PIRADS ≥3).**

**Staging:** CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (nodal/visceral metastases) + isotope bone scan (skeletal metastases — osteoblastic lesions, shows as "hot spots"). PSMA PET/CT increasingly replacing conventional imaging for staging and biochemical recurrence.

### Bladder Cancer

**Flexible cystoscopy:** Outpatient; white light + narrow band imaging; visualises mucosa, identifies papillary lesions.

**TURBT (transurethral resection of bladder tumour):** Diagnostic + therapeutic — resects the tumour; allows staging (T stage depends on depth of invasion: Ta = non-invasive, T1 = lamina propria, T2 = muscle invasion, T3 = perivesical fat, T4 = adjacent organs).

**Urine cytology:** Identifies high-grade or carcinoma in situ (CIS); low sensitivity for low-grade papillary tumours.

## Management

### Urinary Stones

**Analgesia:** **Diclofenac 75 mg IM or 100 mg PR** (first-choice — NSAIDs reduce prostaglandin-mediated ureteral spasm; superior to opioids for renal colic analgesia — SOAP trial). Opioids (morphine) if NSAIDs contraindicated (CKD, GI ulcer, NSAID allergy).

**Medical expulsive therapy (MET):** **Alpha-blocker (tamsulosin 0.4 mg OD)** relaxes ureteral smooth muscle → increases stone passage rate for stones 5–10 mm; number needed to treat ~5 (meta-analyses confirm benefit, though SUSPEND trial was negative — expert opinion favours use for distal ureteral stones 5–10 mm).

**Observation vs. active intervention:**
- <5 mm: 80–90% pass spontaneously; analgesia + high fluid intake + MET + outpatient review
- 5–10 mm: 30–50% pass; consider MET; arrange lithotripsy or ureteroscopy if not passed at 4 weeks
- >10 mm: unlikely to pass spontaneously; active treatment required

**Definitive stone treatment:**
- **ESWL (extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy):** High-energy shock waves focused on the stone under fluoroscopic/ultrasound guidance → stone fragmentation; outpatient; best for renal stones <15 mm and proximal ureteral stones; contraindicated in pregnancy and bleeding diathesis; fragments passed in urine over weeks ("steinstrasse" — stone street — fragments can cause temporary obstruction)
- **Ureteroscopy (URS) + laser lithotripsy:** Semi-rigid or flexible ureteroscope passed to the stone under general/spinal anaesthesia; holmium laser pulverises the stone; ureteral stent placed post-procedure (stent symptoms: frequency, urgency, dysuria — significant quality of life impact); best for ureteral stones and small renal stones
- **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL):** Percutaneous access to the kidney (large bore access tract); nephroscope inserted to stone; laser or pneumatic lithotripsy; best for large (>15–20 mm) or staghorn renal stones

### BPH / LUTS

**Conservative:** Reduce fluid intake at night; reduce caffeine and alcohol (bladder irritants); double voiding; timed voiding.

**Alpha-1 blockers (tamsulosin 0.4 mg, alfuzosin, doxazosin):** Relax smooth muscle in prostate and bladder neck → improved voiding flow; symptom improvement within days; first-line for moderate-severe LUTS. Side effect: retrograde ejaculation (particularly with tamsulosin), orthostatic hypotension (especially doxazosin — consider in patients also hypertensive).

**5-alpha-reductase inhibitors (5-ARIs — finasteride, dutasteride):** Block conversion of testosterone to DHT → prostate volume reduction 20–30% over 6 months; slow disease progression; reduce PSA by 50% (important: correct for this when interpreting PSA in men on 5-ARIs). Best for large prostates (>30 mL). Combination therapy (alpha-blocker + 5-ARI) superior to monotherapy (MTOPS trial).

**Minimally invasive/surgical:**
- **TURP (transurethral resection of the prostate):** Gold standard surgical treatment; resection of prostatic tissue using electrocautery loop; excellent symptom relief; complications: retrograde ejaculation (75%), erectile dysfunction (5–10%), TUR syndrome (rare — dilutional hyponatraemia from irrigation fluid absorption)
- **Newer minimally invasive:** HoLEP (holmium laser enucleation of prostate), UroLift (mechanical implant), Rezum (steam ablation), Aquablation (robotic water jet); similar efficacy to TURP with lower complication rates

### Prostate Cancer Management

**Active surveillance (AS):** For Grade Group 1–2 (low-intermediate risk) localised prostate cancer; regular PSA + repeat biopsy; deferred treatment unless progression detected. ProtecT trial: no difference in 10-year cancer-specific mortality between AS, radical prostatectomy, and radiotherapy for low-risk prostate cancer.

**Radical prostatectomy (RP):** Robotic-assisted laparoscopic (RALP) standard; removes prostate + seminal vesicles; nerve-sparing technique preserves erectile function (partial); complications: incontinence (5–15% long-term), erectile dysfunction (30–70% depending on nerve-sparing).

**External beam radiotherapy (EBRT) + androgen deprivation therapy (ADT):** For localised-locally advanced disease; equivalent oncological outcomes to RP; side effects: bowel and bladder radiation changes, sexual dysfunction.

**ADT (castration therapy):** GnRH agonists (leuprorelin, goserelin) or GnRH antagonists (degarelix) → medical castration; testosterone <50 ng/dL (castrate level); used for advanced/metastatic disease, neoadjuvant/adjuvant with radiotherapy. Side effects: hot flushes, osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular risk.

**Chemotherapy (docetaxel, cabazitaxel):** For metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) + hormone-naive high-volume metastatic disease (CHAARTED, STAMPEDE trials: docetaxel + ADT upfront improves survival in high-volume metastatic disease).

**Novel AR pathway inhibitors:** Enzalutamide, apalutamide, darolutamide; abiraterone (CYP17A1 inhibitor — blocks androgen synthesis in adrenals/tumour cells); all prolong OS in CRPC and increasingly in castration-sensitive settings.

## Complications

- **Renal colic:** Ureteral obstruction → hydronephrosis → renal cortical thinning (chronic obstruction); infected obstructed ureter → pyonephrosis → urosepsis; steinstrasse (stone street) post-ESWL
- **BPH:** Acute urinary retention; chronic retention + overflow incontinence; back-pressure → hydronephrosis → CKD; haematuria; recurrent UTIs
- **Prostate cancer:** Spinal cord compression from vertebral metastases (emergency — dexamethasone 16 mg/day + emergency MRI + oncology ± neurosurgery); DIC (rare); skeletal-related events (fracture, hypercalcaemia)
- **RCC:** Venous tumour thrombus (extends into IVC, right atrium); paraneoplastic syndromes; metastatic disease (lung most common — "cannonball" metastases)
- **Bladder cancer:** TURBT complications (bladder perforation — if extraperitoneal: conservative catheter; if intraperitoneal: emergency laparotomy); recurrence (very common for NMIBC — BCG reduces recurrence/progression); progression to muscle-invasive disease

## Clinical Insight

The infected obstructed ureter is the single most dangerous urological emergency — and the one most commonly killed by delay. A patient with renal colic plus a temperature of 38.5°C and a raised WBC is in septic shock in waiting. The infected urine above the stone cannot drain, and antibiotics cannot penetrate a closed system adequately. The treatment is emergency decompression — either percutaneous nephrostomy (nephrologist/interventional radiologist: needle into the dilated renal pelvis, drain inserted) or ureteric stent (urologist: cystoscopy, retrograde passage of ureteric stent). Until the obstruction is relieved, no amount of antibiotics will control the infection. Never discharge a patient with renal colic and a fever without imaging to exclude hydronephrosis.

The PSA trap: a normal PSA does not exclude prostate cancer. Approximately 15% of prostate cancers occur with PSA <4 ng/mL (PCPT trial). More importantly, PSA is elevated by BPH, prostatitis, and even vigorous exercise — a raised PSA is not diagnostic of cancer. The diagnostic pathway is PSA → mpMRI → targeted biopsy of MRI-visible lesions. mpMRI before biopsy (the PRECISION and MRI-FIRST trials) reduces unnecessary biopsies of low-grade cancers and increases detection of clinically significant cancer.

NSAID-first for renal colic is the evidence-based approach, yet many A&E departments reflexively give intravenous morphine. Multiple meta-analyses and RCTs confirm NSAIDs (rectal or IM diclofenac) are at least as effective as opioids for renal colic and have fewer side effects (less nausea, no respiratory depression). Give diclofenac 75 mg IM (or 100 mg PR if no IM access) as first-line, with opioid reserved for failure or contraindications.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 52-year-old man, sudden right loin-to-groin pain, haematuria, vomiting. Rolling in pain (cannot find comfortable position). Right loin tenderness, no peritonism. HR 108, BP 144/88, T 37.2°C. Urinalysis 3+ blood. AXR: 6 mm radio-opaque opacity over right ureter at iliac crest level.

**Diagnosis: Right ureteral colic from 6 mm stone at the level of the iliac vessels (crossing of ureter over iliac artery)**

**Step 1 — Exclude infected obstruction (most important immediate decision):**
- T 37.2°C = low-grade — just above normal; NOT currently septic but must monitor
- WBC/CRP from bloods: if raised + clinical deterioration → infected obstruction = emergency decompression
- CT KUB to confirm and characterise stone (size, location, hydronephrosis)

**Step 2 — Analgesia:**
- **Diclofenac 75 mg IM** (first-line, superior to opioids for renal colic; check creatinine first — NSAIDs contraindicated in CKD)
- If NSAID contraindicated or insufficient: morphine 5–10 mg IV titrated

**Step 3 — CT KUB results:**
- 6 mm stone in distal right ureter (confirmed, with mild right hydronephrosis, no perinephric fat stranding = no infection)
- HU likely >500 = calcium stone (radio-opaque on AXR confirms)
- 6 mm = borderline for spontaneous passage (50% pass; 50% require intervention)

**Step 4 — Medical expulsive therapy:**
- **Tamsulosin 0.4 mg OD** (alpha-blocker — relaxes distal ureteral smooth muscle → improves passage rate for 5–10 mm stones)
- High fluid intake (2–3 L/day)
- Regular diclofenac + rescue morphine prescription for home
- **Urine strainer** to collect passed stone (stone analysis informs future prevention)

**Step 5 — Review at 4 weeks:**
- If stone has not passed → **ureteroscopy + laser lithotripsy** (preferred for distal ureteral stones)
- Metabolic workup if recurrent stones (serum calcium, uric acid, 24-hour urine oxalate, citrate)
- Stone prevention: high fluid intake (2+ L/day), reduce animal protein, reduce sodium, potassium citrate if low urinary citrate

**Exam trigger:** *Loin-to-groin colicky pain + haematuria + radio-opaque opacity on AXR* → ureteral stone; NSAID first-line analgesia; CT KUB to characterise; tamsulosin for MET; exclude infected obstruction. The critical distractor is missing fever and sending the patient home — always exclude infection before discharging renal colic.
