## Overview

A hernia is the protrusion of a viscus or part of a viscus through a defect in the wall of the cavity in which it normally lies. Most surgical hernias involve the abdominal wall, with the contents (usually bowel or omentum) protruding through a weakness in the muscular or fascial layers. The clinical priority is identifying complications: an irreducible hernia that becomes ischaemic (strangulated) will progress to bowel gangrene and perforation within hours. The anatomy of each hernia type determines its risk profile — femoral hernias, for instance, have a narrow neck and the highest strangulation rate, which is why all femoral hernias should be repaired electively regardless of symptoms.

## Pathophysiology

### Inguinal Hernias — Anatomy and Mechanism

The inguinal canal runs from the deep (internal) inguinal ring to the superficial (external) inguinal ring, passing obliquely through the lower abdominal wall. The **inferior epigastric vessels** are the anatomical landmark that distinguishes the two types:

**Indirect inguinal hernia (most common overall, 75% of all groin hernias):**
- Herniates through the **deep inguinal ring**, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels
- Travels along the course of the spermatic cord (in men) or round ligament (in women) within the inguinal canal
- May descend into the scrotum (complete indirect hernia = inguinoscrotal hernia)
- Mechanism: failure of obliteration of the processus vaginalis — a peritoneal diverticulum that descends with the testis during fetal development; the patent processus provides a preformed peritoneal sac
- More common in younger patients and in males

**Direct inguinal hernia:**
- Protrudes through a weakness in the **posterior wall of the inguinal canal (transversalis fascia)**, in **Hesselbach's triangle** — bounded by the inferior epigastric vessels laterally, the inguinal ligament inferiorly, and the lateral border of rectus abdominis medially
- Passes medial to the inferior epigastric vessels
- Does NOT pass through the deep inguinal ring; therefore does NOT descend into the scrotum
- Mechanism: acquired muscular/fascial weakness from raised intra-abdominal pressure (chronic cough, straining, obesity, heavy lifting), not a congenital defect
- More common in older men

**Memory aid:** "**In**direct: **In** the canal (through the deep ring); **Di**rect: **Di**rectly through the posterior wall."

**Mnemonic for Hesselbach's triangle:** **MALT** — **M**edial rectus border + **A**rch of inguinal ligament + **L**ateral inferior epigastric vessels + **T**ransversalis fascia floor.

### Femoral Hernias

The femoral canal is a potential space in the femoral sheath, medial to the femoral vein, below the inguinal ligament. Femoral hernias protrude through this canal into the upper medial thigh.

- **Location:** Below and lateral to the pubic tubercle (cf. inguinal hernias, which are above and medial to the pubic tubercle — but this distinction is unreliable on examination when the hernia is large)
- **Demographics:** More common in women (wider pelvis, larger femoral canal), though overall inguinal hernias are still more common in women than femoral; after age 50, femoral hernias become relatively more common
- **Risk:** The femoral canal has a rigid, unyielding neck bounded by the femoral vein laterally and the lacunar ligament medially. This predisposes to strangulation — once bowel enters and oedema develops, the rigid ring constricts venous outflow → ischaemia → gangrene within hours
- **Rule:** **All femoral hernias should be repaired** — strangulation rate is 40% at 3 months and up to 22% present acutely strangulated. There is no role for watchful waiting with femoral hernias.

### Complications of Hernias

**Reducible:** Contents can be returned to the abdominal cavity by the patient or clinician — the natural history.

**Irreducible (incarcerated):** Contents cannot be reduced. The hernia is fixed in the defect due to adhesions or an oversized contents relative to the neck. Bowel may or may not be obstructed.

**Obstructed:** Bowel within the hernia sac is obstructed (lumen blocked) but not yet ischaemic. The patient has features of small bowel obstruction: central colicky pain, vomiting, absolute constipation, distension. The bowel is still viable.

**Strangulated:** The blood supply to the hernial contents is compromised. The hernia sac contents become ischaemic → necrotic → perforated. Clinical features: a tense, tender, irreducible hernia; absent bowel sounds; the overlying skin becomes erythematous, then dusky; the patient becomes systemically unwell (fever, tachycardia). This is a surgical emergency — the window for viable bowel salvage narrows with every hour.

**Richter's hernia:** Only part of the bowel wall (the antimesenteric border) herniates, not the entire lumen. Critical feature: the lumen is not completely obstructed — so the patient does NOT develop features of bowel obstruction (no vomiting, no constipation). However, the herniated partial bowel wall becomes ischaemic and gangrenous. This is why a patient can present with strangulation without bowel obstruction symptoms. Richter's hernia is most common in the femoral canal (small, rigid ring).

**Maydl's hernia (W-hernia):** Two loops of bowel within the sac form a W-shape, with the middle limb lying within the abdomen. The intra-abdominal segment is ischaemic but not visible externally — a "reverse" strangulation. At operation, the intra-abdominal loop must be inspected even when the external contents appear viable.

## Clinical Presentation

**History:** A reducible lump in the groin that appears on standing or straining, and disappears on lying down — the classic description of an uncomplicated inguinal hernia. Aching discomfort. Ask about: duration, changes in reducibility (irreducibility = complication), symptoms of obstruction (vomiting, constipation, distension), systemic features (fever, tachycardia = strangulation).

**Examination — the essential steps:**
1. **Standing** (hernia appears), then **lying** (does it reduce?)
2. **Relation to the pubic tubercle:** Inguinal hernias are above and medial to the pubic tubercle; femoral hernias are below and lateral (though this distinction becomes unreliable when the hernia is large)
3. **Invaginate the scrotal skin** with the little finger into the external ring and ask the patient to cough — feel the impulse (direct: broad expansile impulse; indirect: impulse along the finger down the canal)
4. **Can it be transilluminated?** (hydrocele: transilluminates; hernia: does not)
5. **Strangulation signs:** tenderness, erythema, inability to cough impulse, fever

**Differential diagnosis:** Inguinal lymph node (no cough impulse, non-reducible), femoral artery aneurysm (pulsatile, expansile), hydrocele (transilluminates, confined to scrotum, can get above it vs. cannot get above a hernia), undescended testis (absent normal testis in scrotum), saphena varix (soft, compressible, disappears on lying, associated varicosities).

## Diagnosis

**Hernia is a clinical diagnosis** in most cases. Investigations are needed when:
- Diagnosis is uncertain → **ultrasound** (first-line for groin swellings of uncertain origin)
- Strangulation/obstruction suspected → **CT abdomen/pelvis** (confirms bowel in the sac, obstruction, ischaemia, free fluid)
- Sportsman's hernia (athletic pubalgia) → MRI of the pelvis
- Recurrence after prior repair → CT or MRI before re-repair planning

## Management

### Elective Repair

**Principles:** The weakened abdominal wall defect is closed, ideally with mesh reinforcement to prevent recurrence (without mesh = tissue repair = Bassini, Shouldice — higher recurrence ~10%; with mesh = tension-free repair = much lower recurrence ~1–2%).

**Open Lichtenstein repair:** The gold standard for inguinal hernia repair. A polypropylene mesh is sutured to the posterior inguinal canal wall. Tension-free. Local anaesthesia feasible. Recurrence rate ~1%. Risk of chronic groin pain (inguinodynia) from nerve entrapment (ilioinguinal nerve) in ~10%.

**Laparoscopic repair — TAPP or TEP:**
- **TAPP (transabdominal preperitoneal):** Peritoneal cavity entered; mesh placed in preperitoneal space via transabdominal approach
- **TEP (totally extraperitoneal):** Mesh placed in preperitoneal space without entering the peritoneal cavity; avoids visceral injury risk; requires greater technical skill
- Advantages over open: less post-operative pain, faster return to work, lower rates of chronic pain, preferred for bilateral hernias (both sides repaired in one anaesthetic) and recurrent hernias (virgin preperitoneal plane from prior anterior repair)
- Disadvantages: requires general anaesthesia, higher risk of serious complications (bowel/bladder/vascular injury) in inexperienced hands

**Femoral hernia repair approaches:**
- **Lockwood (low) approach:** Groin crease incision; simple, used in emergencies
- **Lotheissen (high) approach:** Through inguinal canal; allows assessment of inguinal region simultaneously
- **McEvedy (high extraperitoneal):** Vertical or oblique incision medial to femoral ring; allows easy access for bowel resection if strangulated — preferred for emergency femoral hernia repair
- **Laparoscopic TEP/TAPP:** Preferred for elective femoral hernia repair — avoids the groin scar, lower chronic pain rates

**Mesh in contaminated field:** If the bowel has been resected (strangulated hernia requiring resection), mesh is generally avoided — contaminated field increases risk of mesh infection. Primary tissue repair (McVay/Cooper's ligament repair for femoral) is used instead. Biological mesh may be used in some centres.

### Emergency Repair — Strangulated Hernia

1. **Resuscitation:** IV fluids, NG tube, urinary catheter, analgesia
2. **Attempt gentle manual reduction ONLY if no signs of strangulation** (no fever, no systemic sepsis, short duration <6h, no erythema) — Taxis: sustained gentle pressure on the hernia with the patient in Trendelenburg position. Never force a strangulated hernia.
3. **Emergency surgery:** McEvedy approach (femoral) or open inguinal approach:
   - Open the sac, assess bowel viability
   - Viable bowel (pink, peristalses, arterial bleeding on nicking) → reduce + repair
   - Non-viable bowel (purple/black, no peristalsis, no bleeding) → resect + primary anastomosis (or defunctioning stoma if contaminated/unstable) + tissue repair without mesh
4. Formal bowel resection + anastomosis if necessary

## Complications

- **Recurrence:** 1–2% with mesh repair, 10% with tissue repair; higher with contaminated field repair
- **Chronic groin pain (inguinodynia):** Up to 10% of patients; caused by ilioinguinal, iliohypogastric, or genitofemoral nerve entrapment; treated by nerve block → neurectomy
- **Mesh infection:** Rare (<1%); may require mesh removal; higher with contaminated repairs
- **Testicular complications:** Ischaemic orchitis (~1%) — cord vessel thrombosis from excessive dissection; testicular atrophy develops over weeks
- **Bowel injury:** Rare; higher risk with TAPP/TEP in inexperienced hands
- **Bladder injury:** TAPP/TEP — the bladder is in the preperitoneal space; risk in patients with prior pelvic surgery
- **Haematoma/seroma:** Common especially after laparoscopic mesh repair; most resolve spontaneously

## Clinical Insight

The most dangerous error in hernia management is watching a femoral hernia. The 40% strangulation rate at 3 months means that watchful waiting carries substantial risk of emergency presentation with potentially non-viable bowel. In contrast, uncomplicated inguinal hernias in elderly men with no symptoms and a long life expectancy can be watched with lower risk — several RCTs (O'Dwyer, Fitzgibbons) show that watchful waiting is safe for minimally symptomatic inguinal hernias, with a crossover rate to surgery of ~25% at 5 years.

The anatomical relationship to the pubic tubercle is the bedside distinguishing feature between inguinal (above and medial) and femoral (below and lateral) hernias, but this is unreliable when the swelling is large or the patient is obese. In practice, the distinction is often only confirmed at operation. The clinical imperative is to always examine the groin crease systematically and to identify reducibility — the most important sign of safety.

Richter's hernia is the hernia that kills without warning. A patient presents with a tender irreducible groin lump but no bowel obstruction symptoms — they are still passing flatus, no vomiting. Because only the antimesenteric wall is herniated, the bowel is not obstructed but it is ischaemic. The peritonitis that follows its perforation can rapidly kill. Any tender irreducible hernia, regardless of bowel obstruction symptoms, requires urgent surgery.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 68-year-old man, 2-year history of reducible right groin lump, now 4h of irreducibility, erythema, tenderness. Two vomits. Swelling is below and lateral to pubic tubercle. Absent bowel sounds, mildly distended abdomen.

**Step 1 — Diagnose a strangulated femoral hernia:** The location (below and lateral to pubic tubercle) = femoral hernia. The 4-hour history of irreducibility + erythema + tenderness + systemic vomiting = strangulation. This is a surgical emergency. The vomiting + absent bowel sounds = developing bowel obstruction, but the strangulation has begun.

**Step 2 — Resuscitation:**
- IV access + bloods (FBC, U&E, LFTs, lactate, group and save)
- IV fluid resuscitation (0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's 500 mL bolus)
- NG tube if persistent vomiting
- Urinary catheter
- IV morphine for analgesia

**Step 3 — Do NOT attempt manual reduction (taxis):**
- Strangulated hernia with >4h history, erythema, and tenderness = manual reduction risks reducing non-viable bowel into the abdomen (reduction en masse) — the ischaemic segment enters the peritoneal cavity where perforation causes peritonitis without an external sign
- Proceed directly to emergency surgery

**Step 4 — Emergency operation (McEvedy approach for femoral):**
- Oblique or vertical incision medial to the femoral canal
- Open the sac — inspect the bowel
- If bowel is viable → reduce + repair (Cooper's ligament repair or synthetic patch if clean field)
- If bowel is non-viable → resect the gangrenous segment, primary anastomosis or stoma depending on contamination; tissue repair only (no mesh in contaminated field)

**Step 5 — Post-operative care:**
- If bowel resected: IV antibiotics continuing post-operatively (co-amoxiclav + metronidazole), NG decompression
- Early feeding once bowel sounds return
- DVT prophylaxis (LMWH + TED stockings)
- Counsel regarding 2% recurrence rate; repair opposite side electively given the bilateral risk

**Exam trigger:** *Elderly man + previously reducible groin lump + acute irreducibility + erythema + tenderness + below/lateral to pubic tubercle* → strangulated femoral hernia requiring emergency surgery. The distractor is attempting Taxis (manual reduction) — absolutely contraindicated in a strangulated hernia with >4h history and systemic features. A second distractor is diagnosing an inguinal hernia based on the long history of a reducible groin lump — but the location (below and lateral to the pubic tubercle) identifies this as femoral.
