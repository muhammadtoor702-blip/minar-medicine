## Overview

Upper gastrointestinal cancers — oesophageal, gastric, and gastro-oesophageal junction (GOJ) — are among the most lethal malignancies, primarily because they present late with advanced disease. Oesophageal cancer is the 8th most common cancer worldwide, with 5-year survival of only 15–20% overall. Gastric cancer is the 5th most common cancer globally and the 4th most common cause of cancer death. The only prospect of cure lies in surgical resection, which is feasible in only 30–40% of patients at diagnosis. Multidisciplinary team (MDT) management — combining surgery, oncology, radiology, and nutrition — is essential for all upper GI malignancies.

## Pathophysiology

### Oesophageal Cancer — Two Distinct Diseases

**Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC):**
- Arises from squamous epithelium of the mid or upper oesophagus
- Risk factors: smoking, alcohol, achalasia, caustic strictures, Plummer-Vinson syndrome (iron deficiency + oesophageal web), tylosis (autosomal dominant palmoplantar keratoderma — highest relative risk)
- Epidemiology: predominant in developing countries (China, Iran, sub-Saharan Africa); declining in the West

**Adenocarcinoma (AC):**
- Arises from columnar metaplasia (Barrett's oesophagus) at the GOJ and lower oesophagus
- **Barrett's oesophagus:** GORD → chronic acid exposure → intestinal metaplasia (specialised columnar epithelium replacing squamous) → low-grade dysplasia → high-grade dysplasia → adenocarcinoma. The metaplasia-dysplasia-carcinoma sequence takes 5–10 years.
- Risk factors: GORD (prolonged), obesity (increased abdominal pressure + oesophageal acid exposure), male sex, white ethnicity
- Epidemiology: rapidly increasing incidence in Western countries; now the most common oesophageal cancer histology in the UK

**Barrett's Surveillance:** Barrett's with intestinal metaplasia → endoscopic surveillance every 3–5 years (non-dysplastic), every 6–12 months (low-grade dysplasia), 3-monthly (high-grade dysplasia). **Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) or endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) for high-grade dysplasia and early T1a cancer** — avoids oesophagectomy.

### Gastric Cancer

**Histological types:**
- **Intestinal type** (Lauren classification): Glandular, cohesive tumour; associated with H. pylori, chronic gastritis, atrophy → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → carcinoma; more common in men; declining incidence in the West; better prognosis
- **Diffuse type:** Poorly cohesive tumour with signet ring cells (intracellular mucin displaces nucleus to periphery); no H. pylori association; younger patients; worse prognosis; linitis plastica (leather bottle stomach — diffuse infiltration → rigid, non-distensible stomach)

**Molecular drivers:**
- HER2 overexpression in ~20% (trastuzumab — ToGA trial — added to chemotherapy for HER2+ advanced disease)
- MSI-high (mismatch repair deficient) tumours respond to immune checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab)

**H. pylori and gastric cancer:** H. pylori → chronic active gastritis → atrophic gastritis → intestinal metaplasia → dysplasia → cancer (Correa cascade). H. pylori eradication reduces gastric cancer risk by ~35% — one of the clearest cancer prevention interventions in medicine.

### Peptic Ulcer Disease (PUD)

**H. pylori:** Colonises gastric mucosa → urease produces ammonia → neutralises gastric acid locally → bacterial survival → chronic mucosal inflammation → ulceration. Present in 90% of duodenal ulcers, 70% of gastric ulcers.

**NSAIDs:** Inhibit COX-1 → reduced prostaglandin synthesis → reduced mucus/bicarbonate secretion → reduced mucosal protection → ulceration. Risk further increased with H. pylori co-infection.

**Complications of PUD:** Haemorrhage (most common), perforation (2nd most common), gastric outlet obstruction (chronic scarring), malignancy (gastric ulcers — must rebiopsy to exclude cancer after treatment).

## Clinical Presentation

### Oesophageal Cancer — Symptoms

- **Progressive dysphagia:** Solid food first → liquids later — hallmark of mechanical obstruction (unlike achalasia where liquids and solids affected simultaneously from the start)
- **Odynophagia** (painful swallowing)
- **Unintentional weight loss** (dysphagia → reduced intake + systemic catabolic effects)
- **Hoarseness:** Recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion (left RLN runs in tracheo-oesophageal groove)
- **Cough on swallowing:** Tracheo-oesophageal fistula — aspiration of oral content into trachea
- **Haematemesis/melaena:** Tumour bleeding

### Gastric Cancer — Symptoms

Early gastric cancer is frequently asymptomatic — explains late presentation in Western practice (unlike Japan where screening is routine). Symptoms include epigastric pain, early satiety, weight loss, nausea, dysphagia (GOJ involvement), haematemesis/melaena.

### Metastatic Spread — Examination Findings

| Sign | Mechanism |
|------|-----------|
| **Virchow's node** (left supraclavicular) | Metastasis via thoracic duct — Troisier's sign; diagnostic of intra-abdominal or thoracic malignancy |
| **Sister Mary Joseph nodule** (periumbilical) | Peritoneal metastasis tracking to umbilicus |
| **Krukenberg tumour** (bilateral ovarian masses) | Transcoelomic/haematogenous spread to ovaries — particularly from gastric signet ring cell carcinoma |
| **Blumer's shelf** (palpable rectal shelf) | Peritoneal metastasis in pouch of Douglas |
| **Hepatomegaly** | Liver metastases |
| **Ascites** | Peritoneal metastasis |

### Peptic Ulcer Perforation

- Sudden onset severe epigastric pain, rapidly becoming generalised → peritonitis
- Rigid "board-like" abdomen, guarding, rebound tenderness
- **Erect CXR: pneumoperitoneum (free air under diaphragm)** in 70–80% of perforations
- Subphrenic collection: Shoulder tip pain (diaphragmatic irritation by free gas/fluid)

## Diagnosis

### Staging Workup — Oesophageal and Gastric Cancer

**OGD with biopsy:** Confirms histology; necessary for all upper GI malignancy. EUS (endoscopic ultrasound): best modality for local T and N staging (T1–T4; nodal involvement).

**CT thorax/abdomen/pelvis:** Primary staging modality — identifies M1 disease (liver, lung, peritoneal metastases, distant nodes) and assesses resectability. All patients with upper GI cancer require staging CT.

**PET-CT (FDG):** Detects occult distant metastases not seen on CT; changes management in ~15% of cases; particularly useful before planned oesophagectomy. Not reliable for signet ring cell/mucinous tumours (low FDG avidity).

**Staging laparoscopy:** Before gastric cancer surgery — detects peritoneal metastases missed by CT/PET in up to 20% of cases; if positive peritoneal cytology → incurable, no surgery.

**Nutritional assessment:** Cachexia and sarcopenia are near-universal in upper GI cancer and predict poor surgical outcomes; early dietitian involvement + parenteral/enteral nutritional support before surgery.

### Helicobacter pylori Diagnosis

- **Urea breath test:** Most accurate non-invasive test; sensitivity/specificity >95%; stop PPIs 2 weeks before
- **Stool antigen test:** Useful in primary care; also for test-of-cure 4 weeks after eradication
- **CLO (Campylobacter-like organism) test:** Rapid urease test on endoscopic biopsy specimen; useful during OGD
- **Serology:** Detects IgG but cannot distinguish active from past infection — not recommended for test-of-cure

## Management

### Oesophageal Cancer — Curative Intent

**Neoadjuvant chemotherapy/chemoradiotherapy (CRT) before surgery:**
- FLOT (5-FU, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, docetaxel) × 4 cycles → surgery → 4 cycles: preferred for GOJ/gastric
- CROSS trial (Netherlands): Carboplatin/paclitaxel CRT → oesophagectomy vs surgery alone — 49% vs 24% R0 resection; 5-year OS 47% vs 34%
- **All resectable oesophageal cancers should receive neoadjuvant treatment before surgery**

**Oesophagectomy:**
- **Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy:** Right thoracotomy + laparotomy; resect thoracic oesophagus; gastric conduit (stomach pulled up as neo-oesophagus); oesophagogastric anastomosis in chest; standard for mid-lower oesophageal cancers
- **McKeown (3-stage) oesophagectomy:** Right thoracotomy + laparotomy + left cervical incision; anastomosis in neck; for upper oesophageal/cervical cancers
- **Transhiatal oesophagectomy:** No thoracotomy; blunt dissection through hiatus; anastomosis in neck; lower respiratory morbidity
- **Anastomotic leak:** Most feared surgical complication — occurs in 10–15%; mediastinitis, sepsis; managed with endoscopic stenting or surgical repair

**Radical CRT alone (without surgery):** For unresectable disease or if surgical risk too high; SCC is more radiosensitive than AC → CRT alone may be curative for SCC

### Gastric Cancer — Surgery

**Surgical principles:**
- R0 resection (clear margins) is the only curative option
- Adequate lymph node dissection: **D2 gastrectomy** (removal of perigastric nodes + nodes along major gastric arteries — levels N1 + N2) — standard of care; D1 (perigastric nodes only) inadequate; DUTCH trial showed D2 reduces local recurrence
- **Distal gastrectomy** (antrum/pylorus/lesser curve): For distal gastric tumours; gastrojejunostomy (Billroth II) or Roux-en-Y reconstruction
- **Total gastrectomy:** For proximal or diffuse tumours; Roux-en-Y oesophagojejunostomy; requires lifelong B12 supplementation (no intrinsic factor)
- **Staging laparoscopy + cytology** before all planned resections

**FLOT perioperative chemotherapy:** 4 cycles before + 4 cycles after surgery — standard of care for resectable gastric/GOJ cancer (Al-Batran et al.); significantly improves R0 resection rate and survival.

### PUD — H. pylori Eradication

**First-line triple therapy (7–14 days):**
- **PPI (omeprazole 20 mg BD) + clarithromycin 500 mg BD + amoxicillin 1 g BD** (standard triple therapy)
- OR PPI + metronidazole + amoxicillin (if penicillin allergy)
- **Confirm eradication with urea breath test or stool antigen 4 weeks after completing antibiotics** (stop PPI 2 weeks before)
- Bismuth quadruple therapy (PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole): second-line for clarithromycin-resistant H. pylori

### PUD Perforation — Surgery

**Graham patch repair (omental patch):**
- Emergency laparotomy or laparoscopy
- Omentum mobilised and sutured over perforation → seals defect
- Peritoneal lavage
- H. pylori eradication postoperatively

**Gastric ulcer perforation:** Unlike duodenal ulcers, gastric ulcers have malignancy risk → biopsy the ulcer edges at time of repair.

### Palliative Management

- **Oesophageal stenting:** Self-expanding metallic stent (SEMS) for malignant dysphagia — provides rapid relief, allows oral intake; palliative
- **Palliative chemotherapy:** ECF/ECX, FLOT, FOLFOX — modest survival benefit (~3–4 months) in advanced gastric/GOJ cancer; trastuzumab added for HER2+ tumours
- **Gastrojejunostomy:** For gastric outlet obstruction from tumour
- **Nutritional support:** PEG/RIG/nasojejunal tube + dietitian

## Complications

- **Post-gastrectomy syndromes:**
  - **Dumping syndrome (early):** Rapid gastric emptying → high osmotic load in small bowel → fluid shift → vasomotor symptoms (flushing, palpitations, diaphoresis) within 30 minutes of eating; treat with small, frequent meals, avoid simple carbohydrates
  - **Late dumping:** Rapid carbohydrate absorption → insulin surge → reactive hypoglycaemia 1–3 hours post-meal
  - **Bile reflux gastritis:** Post-Billroth II — bile reflux into remnant stomach
  - **B12/iron deficiency:** Intrinsic factor/acid lost → malabsorption; lifelong supplementation required after total gastrectomy
  - **Afferent loop syndrome (Billroth II):** Obstruction of afferent limb → bilious vomiting
- **Oesophagectomy complications:** Anastomotic leak (10–15%), pneumonia/ARDS (10–15%), chylothorax (thoracic duct injury), vocal cord paralysis (RLN damage), anastomotic stricture (late)
- **Barrett's oesophagus surveillance failure:** Interval cancers occur — sampling error with four-quadrant biopsies; new wide-area transepithelial sampling (WATS) and AI-assisted imaging improving detection

## Clinical Insight

Progressive dysphagia is cancer until proven otherwise. The natural instinct of both patients and clinicians is to attribute dysphagia to a functional cause — globus, anxiety, GORD — and the average time from symptom onset to diagnosis in oesophageal cancer in the UK is still over 3 months. The classic teaching is that **mechanical dysphagia is progressive** (starts with crusts and steaks, progresses to soft foods, then liquids, then saliva), whereas **dysphagia from motility disorders (achalasia)** affects solids and liquids simultaneously from the outset. This distinction alone should prompt urgent two-week-wait OGD referral.

The discovery of Virchow's node (left supraclavicular lymphadenopathy) in this patient changes the management immediately — this is likely metastatic disease. Virchow's (Troisier's) sign is explained anatomically: the thoracic duct drains the entire abdominal cavity and empties into the left subclavian vein near the left supraclavicular fossa. Tumour emboli carried in lymph can deposit in the nodes at this junction. Its presence indicates stage IV disease in most cases and mandates CT staging before any consideration of surgery.

The Barrett's to adenocarcinoma sequence is the paradigm for understanding why surveillance endoscopy programmes exist. Unlike the colorectal adenoma-carcinoma sequence (where polyps are visible and removable), Barrett's surveillance relies on random four-quadrant biopsies every 2 cm — a sampling strategy that misses up to 50% of dysplasia. The future of Barrett's surveillance is artificial intelligence-assisted endoscopy, which is already outperforming expert endoscopists in early dysplasia detection in RCTs.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 67M, 3-month progressive dysphagia (solids then liquids), 12 kg weight loss, smoker, alcohol 30 units/week, Virchow's node, OGD: irregular ulcerating mass at GOJ.

**Diagnosis: GOJ adenocarcinoma (Siewert type II — centred on GOJ) — likely advanced disease given Virchow's node**

**Step 1 — Histological confirmation:**
- OGD biopsy: adenocarcinoma (or SCC — smoking + alcohol suggests SCC risk too, but GOJ location more typical of AC)
- HER2 testing on biopsy (trastuzumab eligibility if HER2+)
- MSI/mismatch repair testing

**Step 2 — Staging:**
- **CT thorax/abdomen/pelvis with IV contrast** — first-line staging
- Virchow's node → likely metastatic (M1a/M1 disease) — CT to confirm
- **PET-CT** if CT does not clearly confirm distant metastases
- **EUS** for local T/N staging if potentially resectable

**Step 3 — MDT discussion:**
- If Virchow's node confirmed on CT as metastatic → **palliative intent**
- Goals: maintain swallowing, preserve QoL, systemic chemotherapy
- **Oesophageal SEMS (metallic stent)** if dysphagia severe
- **Palliative chemotherapy** — FLOT or FOLFOX ± trastuzumab (HER2+)

**Step 4 — Nutritional support:**
- 12 kg weight loss → significant cachexia
- Nasojejunal tube or PEG for enteral feeding (if stent does not restore adequate oral intake)
- Dietitian review immediately

**Step 5 — Supportive/palliative care:**
- Specialist palliative care involvement early
- Alcohol cessation (if not already stopping)

**If resectable (no confirmed M1):**
- **Neoadjuvant FLOT × 4 cycles → staging restaging CT/PET → Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy/total gastrectomy → FLOT × 4 cycles**
- MDT decision based on performance status, nutritional state, comorbidities

**Exam trigger:** *Progressive dysphagia + weight loss + Virchow's node* → upper GI cancer until proven otherwise; urgent OGD + CT staging. The distractor is treating with PPI for presumed reflux — this delays diagnosis and is inappropriate when progressive dysphagia is present.
