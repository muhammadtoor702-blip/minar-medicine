# Colorectal Disease

## Clinical Scenario

A 66-year-old man presents to his GP with a six-week history of altered bowel habit — alternating constipation and looser stools — with fresh blood mixed in the stool. He has lost 5kg unintentionally and feels generally fatigued. He has a first-degree family history of colorectal cancer (father, diagnosed at age 68). Examination reveals a palpable right-sided abdominal mass. He is urgently referred on the two-week wait pathway.

## Overview

Colorectal disease encompasses a spectrum from common and benign (haemorrhoids, diverticular disease) to premalignant (polyps) and malignant (colorectal cancer — the second most common cause of cancer death in the UK). These conditions share symptom overlap, and the clinical imperative is to distinguish those requiring urgent investigation from those amenable to reassurance or outpatient management.

## Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

### Pathophysiology

Most CRCs arise from **adenomatous polyps** through the adenoma-carcinoma sequence — a stepwise accumulation of genetic mutations over 10–15 years:

- APC gene mutation (familial adenomatous polyposis — germline; sporadic CRC — somatic) → loss of growth control
- KRAS oncogene activation → uncontrolled proliferation
- SMAD4/DPC4 inactivation → loss of TGF-β signalling
- TP53 inactivation → loss of apoptotic control
- Microsatellite instability (MSI) pathway — failure of DNA mismatch repair (MMR) genes; accounts for ~15% of CRC, including Lynch syndrome; better prognosis; predictive of immunotherapy response

**Risk factors for CRC:**

| Risk Factor | Notes |
|------------|-------|
| Age (>50) | Most important risk factor — ~90% of CRC in >50s |
| First-degree family history | 2–3× increased risk; earlier screening |
| Adenomatous polyps | Size (>1 cm), villous architecture, high-grade dysplasia increase risk |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Extensive UC (8–10× risk); risk rises with duration and extent |
| Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) | MMR gene mutations (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2); autosomal dominant; lifetime CRC risk 50–80%; also endometrial, ovarian, urinary tract cancers |
| Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) | APC gene mutation; thousands of colonic polyps; 100% CRC risk by 40 if not treated; prophylactic colectomy |
| Smoking, obesity, alcohol, red/processed meat | Modifiable risk factors |

**Anatomical distribution**: ~60% of CRCs are in the left colon (sigmoid, rectosigmoid, rectum); ~40% in the right colon.

### Clinical Presentation

Presentation varies by tumour location:

| Location | Symptoms |
|---------|---------|
| Right colon (caecum, ascending) | Occult blood → iron deficiency anaemia; right-sided abdominal mass; late obstruction (large calibre) |
| Left colon (descending, sigmoid) | Altered bowel habit; fresh/mixed rectal bleeding; colicky pain; obstruction (narrower calibre) |
| Rectum | Tenesmus; fresh bleeding; mucus PR; incomplete evacuation; change in stool calibre |

**Alarm features (NICE — 2-week wait urgent referral criteria):**
- Age ≥40 with rectal bleeding + change in bowel habit
- Age ≥60 with rectal bleeding, or change in bowel habit without rectal bleeding
- Age ≥60 with unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
- Palpable right-sided abdominal or rectal mass at any age
- Any age with unexplained rectal bleeding + raised FCP (faecal calprotectin)

### Diagnosis

**Colonoscopy + biopsy**: Gold standard. Full visualisation of the colon with tissue diagnosis. Allows synchronous lesion detection (~3–5% have a second CRC).

**CT colonography (virtual colonoscopy)**: Non-invasive alternative for patients unsuitable for colonoscopy; detects lesions ≥6mm.

**CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast**: Staging — identifies liver metastases (most common site), lung metastases, lymphadenopathy.

**MRI pelvis**: Essential for rectal cancer — determines local T stage, relationship to mesorectal fascia (CRM), and guides neoadjuvant treatment decision.

**CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen)**: Not diagnostic but useful for monitoring response to treatment and detecting recurrence.

**Staging (TNM):**

| Stage | Description | 5-year Survival |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| I | T1–2, N0, M0 (limited to bowel wall) | >90% |
| II | T3–4, N0, M0 (through wall, no nodes) | ~70–80% |
| III | Any T, N1–2, M0 (lymph node involvement) | ~50–70% |
| IV | Any T, any N, M1 (distant metastases) | ~10–15% |

### Management

**Curative intent (stages I–III and selected stage IV):**
- **Surgical resection** — right hemicolectomy (right colon), left hemicolectomy/sigmoid colectomy (left colon), anterior resection (upper/mid rectum), abdominoperineal resection (APR) for low rectal cancer (requires permanent colostomy)
- **Laparoscopic surgery** preferred over open — equivalent oncological outcomes, shorter recovery
- **Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy** (capecitabine + radiotherapy) — for T3/T4 or node-positive rectal cancer before surgery (reduces local recurrence, may allow sphincter preservation)
- **Adjuvant chemotherapy** (FOLFOX or CAPOX) — for stage III and high-risk stage II CRC after resection

**Metastatic CRC (stage IV):**
- FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, or CAPOX ± targeted agents (bevacizumab if RAS wild-type; cetuximab/panitumumab for RAS/BRAF wild-type)
- Pembrolizumab for MSI-high/MMR-deficient metastatic CRC (excellent response to immunotherapy)
- Selected liver metastases — hepatic resection if technically feasible and patient is fit (potentially curative in ~30% of cases)

**Lynch syndrome screening**: All CRC specimens should undergo immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or PCR for MSI. Deficiency prompts referral for genetic counselling and germline testing.

### CRC Screening (UK)

- **NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme**: FIT (faecal immunochemical test) sent to all adults aged 50–74 every 2 years. Positive FIT → colonoscopy.
- **High-risk surveillance**: Earlier and more frequent colonoscopy for patients with personal history of adenoma, IBD, or Lynch syndrome.

## Diverticular Disease

Diverticula are outpouchings of colonic mucosa and submucosa through weak points in the muscularis propria at the site of penetrating blood vessels. Predominantly affects the sigmoid colon. Prevalence increases with age and low-fibre diet.

**Diverticulosis** (uncomplicated): Asymptomatic or episodic left-sided cramping pain, bloating, and altered bowel habit. No treatment required; high-fibre diet.

**Diverticulitis** (infected/inflamed diverticulum): Left iliac fossa pain, fever, nausea, change in bowel habit. AXR/CT abdomen confirms and assesses severity (Hinchey classification: I = pericolic abscess; II = pelvic abscess; III = purulent peritonitis; IV = faecal peritonitis).

Management: Mild (Hinchey I): oral antibiotics, clear fluid diet, outpatient. Moderate-severe: IV antibiotics (co-amoxiclav or piperacillin-tazobactam), bowel rest. Abscess: CT-guided percutaneous drainage. Perforation: emergency Hartmann's procedure (left hemicolectomy with end colostomy).

**Diverticular haemorrhage**: Painless massive fresh lower GI bleeding; usually self-limiting. CT angiography or colonoscopy (when stable) to identify bleeding point; embolisation or endoscopic haemostasis.

## Haemorrhoids

Congestion and downward displacement of the anal cushions (submucosal vascular cushions at 3, 7, and 11 o'clock positions). Classified I–IV by degree of prolapse.

**Presentation**: Painless fresh rectal bleeding (coating stool or dripping into toilet pan), perianal itch, mucus discharge. **Haemorrhoids do not cause pain unless thrombosed or strangulated.**

**Management**: High-fibre diet, adequate hydration. Topical preparations (soothing but not curative). **Band ligation** — first-line for grade I–III; highly effective outpatient procedure. Haemorrhoidectomy for grade III–IV or failure of banding.

## Lower GI Bleeding — A Systematic Approach

Lower GI bleeding (LGIB) is defined as haemorrhage from a source distal to the ligament of Treitz.

**Causes by age and clinical context:**

| Clinical Context | Common Causes |
|----------------|--------------|
| Young adult | Haemorrhoids, anal fissure, IBD |
| Middle-aged | IBD, diverticular disease, polyps |
| Elderly | Diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, CRC, ischaemic colitis |

**Angiodysplasia**: Degenerative arteriovenous malformations in the caecum and right colon; elderly patients; recurrent painless LGIB; associated with aortic stenosis (Heyde syndrome — bleeding from angiodysplasia resolves after AVR). Diagnosis: colonoscopy (cherry-red lesions), CT angiography if active bleeding.

**Ischaemic colitis**: Sudden onset lower abdominal cramp + bloody diarrhoea in an elderly patient with vascular disease. Watershed areas (splenic flexure, rectosigmoid junction) most vulnerable. CT shows segmental bowel wall thickening. Usually self-limiting; supportive management.

## Complications

**CRC**: Local invasion, obstruction, perforation, fistula (to bladder, vagina), liver and lung metastases.

**Diverticular disease**: Perforation, abscess, fistula (colovesical fistula — faecaluria and pneumaturia), stricture, haemorrhage.

## Clinical Insight

Lynch syndrome is chronically under-diagnosed. Every CRC should have reflex MMR/MSI testing — this takes no additional clinical effort and can identify patients and families with a 50% inherited CRC risk. A single missed Lynch syndrome diagnosis affects not just the patient but all their first-degree relatives. Universal reflex testing is cost-effective and saves lives.

The two-week wait referral criteria for colorectal cancer are designed for primary care and should be applied without hesitation. Rectal bleeding mixed with stool — as opposed to on the surface of or after stool, which is more typical of haemorrhoids — should never be attributed to haemorrhoids in anyone over 40 without colonoscopy. Haemorrhoids are a diagnosis of exclusion in this age group.

Hartmann's procedure is not a failure — it is the right operation for perforated diverticulitis with peritonitis in a haemodynamically unstable patient. Attempting primary anastomosis in this setting risks anastomotic leak and an even worse outcome. The colostomy can often be reversed 3–6 months later once the patient has recovered.
