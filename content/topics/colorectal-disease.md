## Overview

Colorectal disease encompasses a spectrum from common and benign (haemorrhoids, diverticular disease) to premalignant (polyps) and malignant (colorectal cancer — the second most common cause of cancer death in the UK). These conditions share significant symptom overlap, and the clinical imperative is to distinguish those requiring urgent investigation from those amenable to reassurance or outpatient management. A key principle: fresh rectal bleeding mixed with stool, or altered bowel habit in any person over 40, must never be attributed to haemorrhoids without colonoscopic exclusion of malignancy.

## Colorectal Cancer (CRC)

### Pathophysiology

Most CRCs (~80%) arise from **adenomatous polyps** through the adenoma-carcinoma sequence — a stepwise accumulation of genetic mutations over 10–15 years. Understanding polyp biology explains both surveillance intervals and malignant risk.

**Polyp types and malignant risk:**
- **Tubular adenoma**: Tubular glands; <5% malignant transformation risk if <1 cm
- **Villous adenoma**: Finger-like villi; higher mucin secretion; ~40% malignant risk (also secretes potassium-rich mucus causing secretory diarrhoea and hypokalaemia)
- **Tubulovillous adenoma**: Intermediate features and risk
- **Sessile serrated lesion (SSL)**: Flat, right-sided; MSI pathway; higher malignancy risk despite benign appearance — may be missed at colonoscopy

Risk of malignant transformation increases with: size >1 cm, villous architecture, high-grade dysplasia, and number of synchronous polyps.

**The two molecular pathways of CRC:**

*Chromosomal instability (CIN) pathway* (~85%): Sequential mutations:
- **APC gene** mutation (loss of tumour suppressor) → uncontrolled proliferation (germline in FAP; somatic in sporadic CRC)
- **KRAS** oncogene activation → uncontrolled cell division
- **SMAD4/DPC4** inactivation → loss of TGF-β signalling
- **TP53** inactivation → failure of apoptotic checkpoint → malignant transformation

*Microsatellite instability (MSI) pathway* (~15%): DNA mismatch repair (MMR) gene failure → accumulation of replication errors; accounts for Lynch syndrome (~3% of all CRC) and ~12% of sporadic CRC. MSI-high CRC has better stage-for-stage prognosis and dramatically responds to immune checkpoint inhibitors.

**Risk factors for CRC:**

| Risk Factor | Notes |
|------------|-------|
| Age >50 | ~90% of CRC occurs in >50s |
| First-degree family history | 2–3× increased risk; earlier screening |
| Adenomatous polyps | Size, villous architecture, high-grade dysplasia, multiplicity increase risk |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Extensive UC (8–10× risk after 20 years); risk rises with extent and duration of active inflammation |
| Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) | Autosomal dominant; MMR gene mutations (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2); 50–80% lifetime CRC risk; also endometrial, ovarian, urinary tract, small bowel cancers |
| Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP) | APC gene mutation; hundreds to thousands of colonic polyps by age 20s–30s; 100% CRC risk by age 40 without treatment; prophylactic colectomy required |
| Smoking, obesity, alcohol, red/processed meat | Modifiable risk factors |

**Amsterdam criteria for Lynch syndrome** (clinical diagnosis before genetic testing):
- ≥3 relatives with CRC or Lynch-associated cancer (endometrium, small bowel, ureter, renal pelvis)
- Spanning ≥2 generations
- ≥1 diagnosed before age 50
- One relative is a first-degree relative of the other two

**Anatomical distribution**: ~60% of CRCs in the left colon (sigmoid, rectosigmoid, rectum); ~40% in the right colon. Right-sided tumours are more often MSI-high, present with anaemia rather than rectal bleeding (occult rather than visible blood), and tend to be larger at diagnosis.

### Clinical Presentation

Presentation varies by anatomical location:

| Location | Typical Presentation |
|---------|---------------------|
| Right colon (caecum, ascending) | Occult blood → iron deficiency anaemia; right-sided abdominal mass; weight loss; obstruction occurs late (large calibre) |
| Left colon (descending, sigmoid) | Altered bowel habit; fresh rectal bleeding mixed with stool; colicky abdominal pain; obstruction (narrower calibre) |
| Rectum | Tenesmus (persistent sensation of incomplete evacuation); fresh bleeding coating or mixed with stool; mucus PR; change in stool calibre; urgency |

**NICE alarm features — 2-week wait urgent referral criteria (NG12):**
- Age ≥40 with rectal bleeding AND persistent change in bowel habit
- Age ≥60 with rectal bleeding alone, or change in bowel habit without rectal bleeding
- Age ≥60 with unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
- Palpable right-sided abdominal or rectal mass at any age
- Any age with unexplained rectal bleeding AND raised faecal calprotectin (FCP)

Faecal calprotectin >200 µg/g is associated with a significant increase in risk of colorectal malignancy and warrants urgent colonoscopy.

### Diagnosis

**Colonoscopy + biopsy**: Gold standard. Full visualisation of the colon to the caecum with tissue diagnosis. Synchronous lesions occur in ~3–5% of patients with CRC. Bowel preparation required; CT colonography is the alternative if colonoscopy is incomplete or declined.

**CT chest/abdomen/pelvis with contrast**: Staging — identifies liver metastases (most common site; 20% of patients have hepatic metastases at diagnosis), lung metastases, lymphadenopathy, peritoneal deposits.

**MRI pelvis**: Essential for rectal cancer — determines local T stage, circumferential resection margin (CRM) involvement, and guides decision about neoadjuvant treatment. Involvement of the mesorectal fascia (CRM ≤1 mm) predicts local recurrence and mandates neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy.

**CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen)**: Not diagnostic — low sensitivity and specificity. Used for monitoring treatment response (should fall after complete resection) and detecting recurrence during surveillance.

**Staging (TNM):**

| Stage | Description | 5-year Survival |
|-------|------------|----------------|
| I | T1–2, N0, M0 — limited to bowel wall | >90% |
| II | T3–4, N0, M0 — through bowel wall, no nodes | ~70–80% |
| III | Any T, N+, M0 — lymph node involvement | ~50–70% |
| IV | Any T, any N, M1 — distant metastases | ~10–15% |

### Management

**Curative intent (stages I–III and selected stage IV):**
- **Surgical resection**: Right hemicolectomy (caecum/ascending/proximal transverse colon); left hemicolectomy or sigmoid colectomy (left/sigmoid colon); high anterior resection (upper/mid rectum); low anterior resection (lower rectum, with or without defunctioning ileostomy); abdominoperineal resection (APR) for tumours within 1–2 cm of the external sphincter — requires permanent colostomy
- **Total mesorectal excision (TME)** for rectal cancer — sharp dissection of the mesorectal fascial envelope around the rectum reduces local recurrence from ~25% to <8%
- **Laparoscopic surgery** preferred: equivalent oncological outcomes, faster recovery, less morbidity (CLASICC trial)
- **Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy** (capecitabine + long-course pelvic radiotherapy, or short-course radiotherapy alone): for T3/T4 or node-positive rectal cancer — reduces local recurrence and may allow sphincter preservation in low tumours (tumour downstaging and downsizing)
- **Adjuvant chemotherapy** (FOLFOX — folinic acid + 5-FU + oxaliplatin; or CAPOX — capecitabine + oxaliplatin): for all stage III and high-risk stage II CRC after resection

**Metastatic CRC (stage IV):**
- FOLFOX, FOLFIRI (5-FU + irinotecan), or CAPOX ± targeted biologics:
  - **Bevacizumab** (anti-VEGF) for RAS wild-type and mutant tumours
  - **Cetuximab/panitumumab** (anti-EGFR) — only for RAS/BRAF wild-type tumours
  - **Pembrolizumab** (anti-PD1) — first-line for MSI-high/MMR-deficient metastatic CRC (KEYNOTE-177 trial: superior to chemotherapy as first-line)
- Selected liver metastases — hepatic resection if technically feasible and patient fit: potentially curative in ~30% of cases. "Liver-first" approach for synchronous resectable metastases.

**Lynch syndrome identification**: All CRC specimens should undergo reflex immunohistochemistry (IHC) for MMR proteins (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or PCR for MSI. Any deficiency prompts referral for germline testing. A single missed Lynch syndrome diagnosis has implications for all first-degree relatives (50% inheritance risk). Universal reflex testing costs nothing extra and saves lives.

### CRC Screening

- **NHS Bowel Cancer Screening Programme**: FIT (faecal immunochemical test for haemoglobin) sent to all adults aged 50–74 every 2 years. FIT positive → colonoscopy. FIT threshold for colonoscopy has been lowered (higher sensitivity, more colonoscopies).
- **High-risk surveillance**: Earlier and more frequent colonoscopy for patients with personal history of adenoma (interval based on number, size, and histology), IBD, or Lynch syndrome.
- **Adenoma surveillance intervals** (BSG 2020):
  - Low risk (1–2 small tubular adenomas <1 cm): no surveillance or 5-year colonoscopy
  - Intermediate risk (3–4 small adenomas, or at least 1 adenoma ≥1 cm): 3-year colonoscopy
  - High risk (≥5 adenomas, or ≥3 with at least 1 ≥1 cm): 1-year colonoscopy

## Diverticular Disease

Diverticula are outpouchings of colonic mucosa and submucosa through weak points in the muscularis propria at the site of penetrating blood vessels (between the taeniae coli). Predominantly affects the sigmoid colon. Prevalence rises sharply with age (30% at age 50, >60% at age 80) and correlates inversely with dietary fibre intake.

**Diverticulosis** (uncomplicated): Asymptomatic or episodic left iliac fossa cramping, bloating, and altered bowel habit. No specific treatment required; high-fibre diet.

**Diverticulitis** (infected/inflamed diverticulum): Left iliac fossa pain, fever, nausea, and change in bowel habit. CRP elevated. CT abdomen with contrast is the investigation of choice — confirms the diagnosis and grades severity (modified Hinchey classification):

| Hinchey Grade | Description | Management |
|--------------|-------------|-----------|
| I | Pericolic abscess | Oral antibiotics, outpatient if systemically well |
| II | Pelvic abscess | IV antibiotics ± CT-guided percutaneous drainage |
| III | Purulent peritonitis (free pus, no faecal contamination) | Laparoscopic peritoneal washout OR Hartmann's |
| IV | Faecal peritonitis | Emergency Hartmann's procedure |

Mild diverticulitis (Hinchey I, systemically well): oral co-amoxiclav or ciprofloxacin + metronidazole, clear fluid diet, outpatient management.

**Hartmann's procedure** for perforated diverticulitis: left hemicolectomy with end sigmoid colostomy and oversewing of the rectal stump. Not a failure — it is the appropriate emergency operation for a haemodynamically compromised patient with faecal peritonitis. Primary anastomosis in this context risks anastomotic leak. Colostomy reversal (Hartmann's reversal) can be considered after 3–6 months once the patient has recovered.

**Diverticular haemorrhage**: Painless, often massive fresh lower GI bleeding; usually from arterial rupture in a diverticular neck. Self-limiting in ~80%. CT angiography if actively bleeding (detects bleeding at ≥0.5 mL/min), followed by embolisation; colonoscopy when stable for haemostasis and diagnosis.

**Diverticular fistulae**: Colovesical fistula (most common — faecaluria, pneumaturia, recurrent UTI); colovaginal (vaginal faeculent discharge); enterocolic. Require surgical repair.

## Haemorrhoids

Congestion and downward prolapse of the normal anal vascular cushions at the 3, 7, and 11 o'clock positions in the anal canal (in the anatomical position). Classified by degree of prolapse:
- **Grade I**: Bleed only; do not prolapse
- **Grade II**: Prolapse on defecation; reduce spontaneously
- **Grade III**: Prolapse; require manual reduction
- **Grade IV**: Permanently prolapsed; cannot be reduced

**Presentation**: Painless bright red rectal bleeding (coating stools, on toilet paper, or dripping into the pan — not mixed with stool), perianal itch, and mucus discharge. **Haemorrhoids are painless unless thrombosed or strangulated.** Thrombosed external haemorrhoid: sudden onset severe perianal pain with tense purple lump; treat with excision in first 72 hours (more effective than conservative management).

**Management**: High-fibre diet, adequate fluid intake, avoidance of straining. **Rubber band ligation** — first-line for grade I–III haemorrhoids; highly effective outpatient procedure (70–80% symptom resolution per session). Haemorrhoidectomy (surgical excision under general anaesthetic) for grade III–IV or failure of banding. Injection sclerotherapy (phenol in almond oil) for grade I.

## Lower GI Bleeding

Lower GI bleeding (LGIB) is defined as haemorrhage from a source distal to the ligament of Treitz. Characterised by passage of bright red blood per rectum (haematochezia) or dark red blood mixed with stool.

**Causes by age group:**

| Age | Common Causes |
|-----|--------------|
| Young adult | Haemorrhoids, anal fissure, IBD (UC — bloody diarrhoea) |
| Middle-aged | IBD, diverticular disease, polyps, CRC |
| Elderly | Diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, CRC, ischaemic colitis |

**Angiodysplasia**: Degenerative arteriovenous malformations in the caecum and right colon — arise from chronic low-grade venous obstruction caused by muscular contraction. Elderly patients with recurrent painless LGIB without an identifiable source. Associated with aortic stenosis (**Heyde syndrome** — AVWS type 2A, shear stress through the stenotic valve destroys von Willebrand factor, impairing haemostasis at the angiodysplastic lesions; treating aortic stenosis with TAVR or surgical AVR often resolves the bleeding). Diagnosis: colonoscopy (cherry-red lesions with a lacy vascular pattern) or CT angiography if actively bleeding. Treatment: argon plasma coagulation at colonoscopy.

**Ischaemic colitis**: Sudden lower abdominal cramp followed by bloody diarrhoea in an elderly patient with vascular disease or AF. Caused by impaired mesenteric blood flow to the watershed areas — **splenic flexure** (between the superior and inferior mesenteric artery territories) and **rectosigmoid junction**. CT shows segmental bowel wall thickening ± pneumatosis. Usually self-limiting with supportive management; rarely requires surgery (gangrene).

**Anal fissure**: Tear in the anateoderm of the anal canal, typically posterior midline, from passage of hard stool. Causes sharp pain during and after defecation (minutes to hours), bright red blood coating the stool or on the paper. Spasm of the internal anal sphincter perpetuates the fissure. Management: stool softeners, topical GTN or diltiazem (relax the sphincter, improve blood flow); botulinum toxin injection for chronic fissure; lateral internal sphincterotomy for failure of medical treatment.

## Complications

**CRC**: Local invasion, bowel obstruction, perforation, fistula (to bladder, vagina, skin), liver and lung metastases, peritoneal carcinomatosis.

**Diverticular disease**: Perforation, abscess, colovesical fistula (pneumaturia, faecaluria), stricture, massive haemorrhage.

## Clinical Insight

Lynch syndrome is chronically under-diagnosed. Every CRC — regardless of clinical suspicion — should have reflex MMR/MSI testing. This requires no additional clinical decision-making: it is performed automatically on the surgical specimen. A single missed Lynch syndrome diagnosis means all first-degree relatives carry an unrecognised 50% risk of CRC (and endometrial, ovarian, and other Lynch-associated cancers). Universal reflex testing is cost-effective and saves lives at population level.

Rectal bleeding mixed with stool should never be attributed to haemorrhoids in anyone over 40 without colonoscopy. Haemorrhoids produce painless bleeding that coats the stool or drips into the toilet pan — they do not cause bleeding mixed into the stool body. Blood mixed with stool suggests a more proximal and more sinister source. The 2-week wait referral criteria exist precisely because haemorrhoids are the most common misattribution that delays a potentially curable cancer diagnosis.

Hartmann's procedure is not a sign of operative failure — it is the correct operation for perforated diverticulitis with faecal peritonitis in an unstable patient. Attempting primary anastomosis under these conditions risks anastomotic leak, which carries a mortality of 20–30%. The colostomy can be reversed 3–6 months later once the inflammatory process has fully resolved and the patient has been nutritionally optimised.
