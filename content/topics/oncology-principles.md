## Overview

Modern oncology has been transformed from a discipline of cytotoxic chemotherapy to one of molecularly targeted therapy and immunotherapy. Understanding the principles of cancer biology — how cells acquire the hallmarks of malignancy, how tumours evade the immune system, and how resistance to therapy develops — is now clinically essential. Equally important are the principles governing staging, treatment intent, and the management of oncological emergencies.

## Pathophysiology

### The Hallmarks of Cancer

Cancer arises from the sequential acquisition of mutations that confer successive growth advantages. The key hallmarks (Hanahan and Weinberg):

1. **Sustained proliferative signalling** — oncogene activation (RAS, EGFR, HER2, BCR-ABL, MYC)
2. **Evasion of growth suppressors** — tumour suppressor gene loss (TP53, RB1, BRCA1/2, APC)
3. **Resistance to cell death** — BCL-2 overexpression, p53 mutation
4. **Replicative immortality** — telomerase reactivation
5. **Angiogenesis** — VEGF upregulation
6. **Invasion and metastasis** — epithelial-mesenchymal transition, MMP upregulation
7. **Immune evasion** — PD-L1 upregulation, MHC downregulation, T-reg recruitment
8. **Reprogramming energy metabolism** — Warburg effect (aerobic glycolysis)

### Oncogenes and Tumour Suppressors

**Oncogenes** are gain-of-function mutations in proto-oncogenes — one mutated allele is sufficient ("dominant"):
- **KRAS/NRAS**: Mutated in ~30% of all cancers; GTPase stuck in active state; drives proliferation in pancreatic, colorectal, and lung cancer. KRAS G12C now targetable (sotorasib, adagrasib).
- **EGFR**: Receptor tyrosine kinase; activating mutations in lung adenocarcinoma → targeted with EGFR TKIs (erlotinib, gefitinib, osimertinib).
- **HER2**: Amplification in breast and gastric cancer → targeted with trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1.
- **ALK**: Rearrangement in ~5% of NSCLC → targeted with crizotinib, alectinib.
- **BCR-ABL**: CML; targeted with imatinib (paradigm of targeted oncology).

**Tumour suppressors** are loss-of-function mutations — both alleles must be inactivated ("two-hit hypothesis," Knudson):
- **TP53**: Mutated in >50% of human cancers; guardian of the genome; triggers apoptosis or cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage
- **BRCA1/BRCA2**: DNA repair (homologous recombination); germline mutations → hereditary breast/ovarian cancer syndrome; BRCA-mutant tumours are sensitive to PARP inhibitors (olaparib, niraparib) — synthetic lethality
- **RB1**: Cell cycle regulator; lost in retinoblastoma, small cell lung cancer, osteosarcoma
- **APC**: Wnt pathway; mutated in familial adenomatous polyposis and ~80% of sporadic colorectal cancers

### Tumour Immunology and Checkpoint Inhibitors

The immune system continuously surveys for and eliminates malignant cells. Tumours escape through multiple mechanisms — most importantly by upregulating **PD-L1** on their surface, which binds **PD-1** on T-cells and delivers an inhibitory signal, effectively "switching off" T-cell killing.

**Immune checkpoint inhibitors** block this interaction:
- **Anti-PD-1** (pembrolizumab, nivolumab): Block the PD-1 receptor on T-cells
- **Anti-PD-L1** (atezolizumab, durvalumab, avelumab): Block the ligand on tumour cells
- **Anti-CTLA-4** (ipilimumab): Block CTLA-4, an earlier checkpoint inhibitor of T-cell activation

By removing these brakes, checkpoint inhibitors restore T-cell anti-tumour activity. They have transformed outcomes in melanoma, NSCLC, urothelial cancer, MSI-high colorectal cancer, and many others.

**Predictors of response**: PD-L1 expression (tumour proportion score, TPS), tumour mutational burden (TMB — high mutational burden → more neoantigens → better immune recognition), MSI/MMR status (MSI-high tumours have extremely high TMB from defective DNA mismatch repair — pembrolizumab is approved for all MSI-high solid tumours regardless of histology).

**Immune-related adverse events (irAEs)**: Checkpoint inhibitors cause immune activation in normal tissues — colitis (most common), pneumonitis, hepatitis, endocrinopathies (hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, diabetes — often permanent), dermatitis, nephritis, myocarditis (rare but potentially fatal). Manage with steroids; severe irAEs require permanent discontinuation.

## Clinical Staging and Treatment Intent

### TNM Staging

The universal staging framework for solid tumours:
- **T (Tumour)**: Size and local extent (T1–T4)
- **N (Nodes)**: Regional lymph node involvement (N0–N3)
- **M (Metastasis)**: Distant metastasis (M0 or M1)

TNM is combined into overall stages (I–IV). Stage I–II: localised, potentially curable with surgery or radical radiotherapy. Stage III: locally advanced, multimodality treatment. Stage IV: metastatic, generally incurable with standard therapies (exceptions exist: germ cell tumours, some haematological malignancies, and highly targeted molecular subtypes).

### Performance Status — ECOG Scale

Performance status determines fitness for treatment and correlates with outcomes:

| ECOG | Description |
|------|------------|
| 0 | Fully active; no restriction |
| 1 | Restricted strenuous activity; ambulatory; light work possible |
| 2 | Ambulatory >50% of waking hours; self-care possible; no work |
| 3 | Limited self-care; confined to bed/chair >50% of waking hours |
| 4 | Completely disabled; no self-care |

ECOG ≥2 is generally the threshold below which most chemotherapy regimens are harmful. Targeted therapies and immunotherapy have better tolerability and may be appropriate at ECOG 2.

## Treatment Modalities

### Surgery

The cornerstone of curative treatment for most solid tumours. Principles: adequate resection margins, lymph node dissection for staging and local control, sentinel lymph node biopsy to minimise morbidity (breast, melanoma, vulval cancer). Neoadjuvant (pre-operative) therapy can downstage tumours to allow resection.

### Radiotherapy

Ionising radiation causes DNA double-strand breaks → cell death preferentially in rapidly dividing cells. Delivered as:
- **External beam radiotherapy (EBRT)**: Photons (X-rays) or protons
- **Brachytherapy**: Radioactive source implanted adjacent to tumour (cervical, prostate, endobronchial cancers)
- **Stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR/SBRT)**: High-dose, highly conformal radiation to small volumes — used for lung oligometastases, spinal metastases, brain metastases

**Radiation sensitisers**: Concurrent chemotherapy (cisplatin, carboplatin, 5-FU) potentiates radiation — standard in head and neck cancer, cervical cancer, oesophageal cancer, rectal cancer.

**Late effects**: Fibrosis, organ dysfunction — bowel, bladder, lung, heart, spinal cord tolerance limits define radiotherapy plans.

### Systemic Therapy

**Chemotherapy**: Cytotoxic agents acting on cell division — DNA alkylators (cyclophosphamide, cisplatin), antimetabolites (5-FU, gemcitabine, methotrexate), topoisomerase inhibitors (irinotecan, etoposide), taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel — spindle poisons), anthracyclines (doxorubicin — DNA intercalation). Non-selective toxicity produces the classic side effect profile: myelosuppression, mucositis, nausea, alopecia, peripheral neuropathy.

**Targeted therapy**: Attacks specific molecular drivers:

| Target | Drug | Cancer |
|--------|------|--------|
| EGFR (mut) | Osimertinib, erlotinib | NSCLC |
| ALK/ROS1 | Alectinib, crizotinib | NSCLC |
| BRAF V600E | Vemurafenib + cobimetinib | Melanoma, NSCLC, CRC |
| HER2 | Trastuzumab, pertuzumab, T-DM1 | Breast, gastric |
| BCR-ABL | Imatinib, dasatinib | CML, Ph+ ALL |
| VEGFR | Bevacizumab | CRC, renal, ovarian |
| CDK4/6 | Palbociclib, ribociclib | HR+ breast cancer |
| PARP | Olaparib, niraparib | BRCA+ breast/ovarian |
| KRAS G12C | Sotorasib, adagrasib | NSCLC, CRC |

**Endocrine therapy**: For hormone-sensitive tumours:
- Breast cancer: Tamoxifen (premenopausal — SERM, blocks oestrogen receptor), aromatase inhibitors (postmenopausal — anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane — block oestrogen synthesis)
- Prostate cancer: LHRH agonists/antagonists (androgen deprivation therapy) + enzalutamide or abiraterone (advanced disease)

## Oncological Emergencies

### Superior Vena Cava Obstruction (SVCO)

External compression of the SVC — most commonly by lung cancer or lymphoma. Features: facial and neck swelling, arm oedema, headache (worse bending forward), distended neck veins, dyspnoea. CT with contrast confirms the diagnosis. Management: treat the primary cancer (steroids as temporising measure, radiotherapy for radio-sensitive tumours, endovascular stenting for immediate relief).

### Spinal Cord Compression (MSCC)

Metastatic spinal cord compression is a medical emergency — irreversible if not treated promptly. Most commonly from breast, lung, prostate, and myeloma. Back pain that is worse lying flat (worsened by lying down distinguishes from mechanical back pain), bilateral leg weakness and sensory disturbance, bladder and bowel dysfunction (late signs — urgency, retention, incontinence).

**Act immediately**: IV dexamethasone 16 mg immediately. Urgent whole-spine MRI. Neurosurgical decompression within 24 hours if single level and fit for surgery (superior to radiotherapy alone for ambulatory function recovery). Radiotherapy for multilevel disease or surgical ineligibility.

**Outcome is determined by neurological status at time of treatment**: Patients who are ambulatory when treated remain ambulatory in ~80%. Paraplegic patients rarely recover walking. This is why back pain in a known cancer patient demands urgent MRI — do not wait for neurological signs.

### Hypercalcaemia of Malignancy

See Endocrinology/Pituitary and Calcium section. Most common in breast cancer, lung SCC, myeloma, and renal cell carcinoma. IV saline → IV zoledronate (bisphosphonate). Denosumab for bisphosphonate-refractory cases.

### Febrile Neutropaenia

Neutrophil count <0.5 × 10⁹/L + temperature ≥38°C in a patient on chemotherapy. IV piperacillin-tazobactam within 60 minutes of presentation. Do not delay for cultures. Mortality is proportional to time to antibiotic administration.

### Tumour Lysis Syndrome (TLS)

Massive tumour cell lysis releases intracellular contents: hyperkalaemia (cardiac arrest), hyperphosphataemia (→ hypocalcaemia → tetany, seizures), hyperuricaemia (uric acid nephropathy → AKI). Highest risk: Burkitt's lymphoma, ALL, and any highly chemosensitive tumour. Prevention: aggressive IV hydration, allopurinol (or rasburicase in high-risk patients — directly metabolises uric acid), electrolyte monitoring.

## Clinical Insight

Performance status is the single most important factor in treatment decision-making after the biology of the tumour itself. It is more predictive of chemotherapy tolerance and outcomes than age. An 80-year-old with ECOG 0 tolerates treatment far better than a 55-year-old with ECOG 3. Making decisions based on chronological age rather than functional status leads to undertreating the fit elderly and overtreating the frail.

Molecular profiling has made histological diagnosis insufficient for most common cancers. An NSCLC diagnosed without EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, MET, RET, KRAS G12C, and PD-L1 testing is an incomplete diagnosis — and a missed opportunity for targeted therapy that may be dramatically more effective than standard chemotherapy. The patient in the scenario could have 3–4 years of progression-free survival on osimertinib; without testing, they would have received platinum doublet chemotherapy with median PFS of ~6 months.

Metastatic spinal cord compression kills neurological function silently before the physical signs appear. Back pain in any patient with a known malignancy is MSCC until proven otherwise, and the investigation of choice is urgent whole-spine MRI — not plain X-ray, not expectant management, not "come back if it gets worse." Once the patient is paraplegic, treatment rarely restores function. The window for intervention is narrow and defined by pain, not paralysis.
