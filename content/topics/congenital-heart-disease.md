## Overview

Congenital heart disease (CHD) affects approximately 8 per 1000 live births, making it the most common congenital anomaly. The spectrum ranges from lesions that are compatible with normal life and may never require intervention (small VSDs, mild pulmonary stenosis) to those that are incompatible with survival beyond the first hours or days of life without emergency intervention (transposition of the great arteries, hypoplastic left heart syndrome). The primary classification distinguishes **acyanotic lesions** (left-to-right shunts — blood is oxygenated but flow is increased) from **cyanotic lesions** (right-to-left shunts — deoxygenated blood enters the systemic circulation). This distinction drives the immediate clinical approach and determines whether prostaglandin E1 is needed.

---

## Pathophysiology

### Acyanotic Defects — Left-to-Right Shunts

In acyanotic CHD, a communication between the left and right sides of the heart (or between the aorta and pulmonary artery) allows oxygenated blood to flow into the pulmonary circulation. Systemic oxygen saturation remains normal because blood in the left heart and aorta is fully oxygenated. The pathophysiology is one of **volume overload** of the pulmonary circulation and the right heart:

- **Increased pulmonary blood flow** → pulmonary vascular resistance rises progressively over years → eventually pulmonary vascular resistance exceeds systemic vascular resistance → shunt reversal → Eisenmenger syndrome
- **Left-to-right shunts** are typically silent at birth (pulmonary vascular resistance is still high in the neonatal period; shunting is minimal until PVR falls over the first weeks of life)

### Cyanotic Defects — Right-to-Left Shunts

In cyanotic CHD, deoxygenated blood from the right heart enters the systemic circulation, bypassing the lungs. The mechanism depends on the specific defect:

- **Obstruction to pulmonary blood flow** (ToF, tricuspid atresia, pulmonary atresia): RV blood cannot pass through the lungs → diverts right-to-left through a septal defect
- **Parallel circulations** (TGA): the aorta arises from the RV and the pulmonary artery from the LV — systemic and pulmonary circuits run in parallel rather than in series; survival requires mixing at the atrial, ventricular, or ductal level

### The Ductus Arteriosus and Prostaglandin E1

The ductus arteriosus (DA) — a connection between the main pulmonary artery and the descending aorta — is patent in fetal life, maintained by prostaglandins from the placenta. After birth, oxygen tension rises and prostaglandin falls → the duct closes over the first days of life.

In **duct-dependent lesions** — defects where either pulmonary blood flow (most cyanotic defects) or systemic blood flow (hypoplastic left heart, severe coarctation, interrupted aortic arch) depends on the PDA — ductal closure is fatal. **Prostaglandin E1 (alprostadil)** maintains ductal patency and is life-saving in this group.

## Clinical Presentation

### Acyanotic Defects

**Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD)** — most common CHD (30–35% of all CHD):
- Small VSD: harsh holosystolic murmur at left lower sternal border (LLSB), often detected incidentally; 80% of small VSDs close spontaneously by age 4
- Large VSD: heart failure (tachypnoea, poor feeding, sweating with feeds, failure to thrive) at 4–8 weeks when PVR falls and shunting increases; loud holosystolic murmur + thrill + displaced apex; if uncorrected → Eisenmenger syndrome
- Murmur may be absent at birth (PVR still high, minimal shunting)

**Atrial Septal Defect (ASD)** — 6–8% of CHD:
- Often asymptomatic until adulthood; may present with recurrent chest infections, atrial arrhythmias, or paradoxical embolism
- **Fixed splitting of S2** — the defining sign; the pulmonary component is delayed because the RV fills with extra blood from the shunt, taking longer to eject; this delay is fixed (not respiratory variation)
- Ejection systolic murmur in the pulmonary area (from increased RV stroke volume, not from flow through the ASD itself)

**Patent Ductus Arteriosus (PDA)**:
- **Continuous "machinery" murmur** best heard at the left infraclavicular area, peaking at S2
- Prominent bounding pulses (wide pulse pressure from aortic runoff into pulmonary artery in diastole)
- In premature infants: apnoea, heart failure, necrotising enterocolitis risk; close medically with indomethacin or ibuprofen (COX inhibitors reduce prostaglandin synthesis → ductal constriction). Surgical ligation if fails.
- In term infants and older children: surgical or catheter closure

**Coarctation of the Aorta**:
- Typically juxtaductal (adjacent to ductus ligament); associated with bicuspid aortic valve (50%) and Turner syndrome (30%)
- **Radio-femoral delay** (femoral pulse delayed compared to radial pulse) and **arm-leg blood pressure differential** (>20 mmHg systolic) are the clinical hallmarks
- CXR: **rib notching** of ribs 3–8 (from dilated intercostal collaterals — rarely seen before age 5), "figure 3 sign" (indentation at coarctation site on frontal CXR)
- Critical coarctation in neonates: presents at ductal closure with severe heart failure, shock, and absent femoral pulses → prostaglandin E1 + urgent surgical repair

### Cyanotic Defects — the "5 Ts"

**Tetralogy of Fallot (ToF)** — most common cyanotic CHD (~5% of all CHD):

Four components: (1) **V**SD + (2) overriding **a**orta + (3) **R**VH + (4) **p**ulmonary stenosis. Mnemonic: **VARP** or just remember "ToF: VSD + aorta override + RVH + PS."

- CXR: **boot-shaped heart** (upturned RV apex + concave pulmonary artery segment from RVOTO)
- **Hypercyanotic (Tet) spells**: sudden severe cyanosis from acute RVOTO worsening → less pulmonary blood flow → more R→L shunting. Triggered by crying, feeding, defaecation. The child squats spontaneously (squatting increases SVR → reduces R→L shunting). Emergency management: **knee-chest position** (child's knees to chest = maximises SVR), **morphine** (reduces tachycardia and hyperpnoea that worsens RVOTO), **phenylephrine IV** (pure alpha agonist → increases SVR → reduces shunting), **sodium bicarbonate** (corrects acidosis), **IV propranolol** (reduces RVOTO)
- Definitive repair at 3–6 months: VSD closure + RVOT widening

**Transposition of Great Arteries (TGA)**:
- Aorta arises from morphological RV; pulmonary artery from morphological LV → parallel, not series, circulations → life-incompatible without mixing
- CXR: **"egg-on-a-string"** (narrow mediastinum from anteroposterior overlap of great vessels + cardiomegaly + increased pulmonary vascularity — "string" = narrow pedicle, "egg" = enlarged cardiac silhouette)
- No murmur (no obstruction)
- **Immediate treatment:** IV prostaglandin E1 (keeps PDA open for mixing) + **balloon atrial septostomy (Rashkind procedure)**: urgent catheter-based procedure to tear the atrial septum under echo guidance, creating an ASD for obligate mixing
- **Definitive: arterial switch operation (Jatene procedure)** — must be performed within the first 2 weeks of life before the LV becomes deconditioned from facing the low-pressure pulmonary circuit

**Tricuspid atresia:** No tricuspid valve → no R→L flow through AV valve → requires ASD for RA output + VSD or PDA for pulmonary blood flow. Cyanosis + absent RV impulse. Staged palliation (Fontan circulation).

**Truncus arteriosus:** Single arterial trunk giving rise to aorta + pulmonary arteries; requires VSD for survival. Cyanosis + increased pulmonary flow + LVH.

**Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Return (TAPVR):** All pulmonary veins drain to systemic venous circulation (SVC, RA, coronary sinus, or IVC) rather than left atrium. Supracardiac type (most common): **snowman/figure-of-8 CXR** (vertical vein + SVC = snowman's body on CXR). Obstructed TAPVR is a surgical emergency (pulmonary venous obstruction → pulmonary oedema + severe cyanosis).

## Diagnosis

**Antenatal detection:** Fetal echocardiography at 20 weeks anomaly scan detects ~50% of major structural defects. Improves to ~80% at specialist centres with detailed cardiac views.

**Pulse oximetry screening (newborn blood spot screen extension):** SpO₂ <95% pre- or post-ductally, or >3% difference between pre-ductal (right hand) and post-ductal (foot) = screen positive → echocardiography.

**Hyperoxia test:** Place infant in 100% O₂ for 10 minutes; measure PaO₂ via arterial blood gas:
- PaO₂ **>150 mmHg** = cardiac cause unlikely (pulmonary cause — the lungs can increase oxygenation with added O₂)
- PaO₂ **<150 mmHg** = structural cardiac cause likely (fixed R→L shunt that cannot be overcome by increasing FiO₂)

**Echocardiography:** The definitive investigation. Defines anatomy, quantifies shunts, estimates pressures, guides timing of surgical repair.

**Cardiac catheterisation:** Required pre-surgery for complex anatomy, pressure measurements, and balloon procedures (atrial septostomy, balloon valvuloplasty).

## Management

### Prostaglandin E1 (Alprostadil)

**Dose:** 0.01–0.1 mcg/kg/min IV infusion. Dose-dependent side effects: **apnoea** (most important — always have bag-mask ventilation at the bedside when starting PGE1), hypotension, fever, seizures, flushing, diarrhoea. Intubation for transfer on PGE1 is sometimes required. PGE1 is life-saving in duct-dependent lesions — do not withhold because of side-effect concern.

### Surgical Principles

| Defect | Timing | Procedure |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| TGA | Within 2 weeks | Arterial switch operation |
| ToF | 3–6 months | Complete repair (VSD patch + RVOT widening) |
| Large VSD | 3–6 months (or sooner if heart failure) | Surgical patch or catheter device closure |
| ASD | 2–5 years if haemodynamically significant | Catheter device closure (Amplatzer) or surgical |
| PDA (term) | After failed medical closure | Surgical ligation or catheter coil/device |
| Coarctation | Neonatal (critical) or 1–2 years (elective) | Surgical repair or balloon dilation + stent |
| Hypoplastic LH | Staged: Norwood → Glenn → Fontan | Staged palliation over 3 years |

## Complications

- **Eisenmenger syndrome:** Irreversible pulmonary hypertension from long-standing L→R shunt → shunt reversal → cyanosis; defect closure is then absolutely contraindicated
- **Infective endocarditis:** Risk in structural lesions with turbulent flow; antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures in those with prosthetic valves, prior IE, or unrepaired cyanotic CHD
- **Arrhythmias:** Common after cardiac surgery (intra-atrial reentrant tachycardia after Fontan; RBBB after VSD repair)
- **Residual lesions:** Pulmonary regurgitation after ToF repair → progressive RV dilation requiring re-operation
- **Neurodevelopmental impairment:** Common after cardiac surgery requiring cardiopulmonary bypass, particularly with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest

## Clinical Insight

Cyanosis in the first hours of life is structural cardiac disease until proven otherwise — and the hyperoxia test separates cardiac from pulmonary causes within minutes. The key rule is: do the test, and act on the result. A PaO₂ that fails to rise above 150 mmHg in 100% O₂ is a duct-dependent lesion until proven otherwise. Start PGE1. Do the echo. Call the cardiac surgery team. Every hour matters with TGA — the neonatal LV deconditions rapidly when it only faces the low-pressure pulmonary circuit, and the arterial switch operation requires a trained LV to function as the systemic pump. After 2 weeks, the operation becomes substantially more complex.

The murmur trap: many cyanotic defects have no murmur — or a very subtle one. TGA has essentially no murmur (no obstruction to flow). A cyanosed neonate with NO murmur is more worrying, not less. The murmur in acyanotic CHD (VSD, ASD, PDA) is the marker of turbulent flow through the defect — its absence in a cyanotic infant does not reassure, because the pathophysiology of cyanotic disease is not obstruction but misdirected parallel blood flow.

Tet spells are genuinely dangerous and genuinely treatable. The physiological logic is elegant: the RVOT obstructs flow, R→L shunting increases, cyanosis deepens, hypoxia causes acidosis, acidosis increases pulmonary vascular resistance, worsening the RVOT obstruction. Breaking the cycle requires increasing SVR (knee-chest position, phenylephrine) and reducing RVOTO (propranolol). These manoeuvres are immediately effective. The child who squats spontaneously during exercise has already learned this physiological trick.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 3-day-old male, increasing cyanosis. SpO₂ 72%. HR 168. No murmur. CXR: egg-on-a-string silhouette with narrow mediastinum + increased pulmonary vascularity. PGE1 started.

**Step 1 — Recognise TGA from the CXR:** The egg-on-a-string appearance is pathognomonic: the narrow mediastinum results from the anteroposterior overlap of the transposed aorta (anterior) and pulmonary artery (posterior) giving a narrow vascular pedicle — the "string." The enlarged cardiomegaly + increased pulmonary vascularity = the "egg." No other lesion produces this combination.

**Step 2 — Understand why there is no murmur:** In TGA, the aorta and pulmonary artery have simply swapped origin. There is no obstruction to flow, no turbulence — hence no murmur. A cyanosed neonate with no murmur and no respiratory distress (lungs are well-perfused — increased pulmonary markings on CXR) = TGA.

**Step 3 — PGE1 is correct and critical:** The PDA is the only mixing point. PGE1 keeps it open. Without the PDA, systemic and pulmonary circulations are completely separate — incompatible with life within hours. Apnoea monitoring is mandatory with PGE1.

**Step 4 — Emergency Rashkind procedure:** PGE1 is a bridge, not definitive treatment. Arrange urgent balloon atrial septostomy — catheter-based, can be done at bedside with echocardiographic guidance. This creates a large ASD for bidirectional mixing and substantially improves oxygenation.

**Step 5 — Arterial switch operation within 2 weeks:** The neonatal LV will begin to decondition (lose muscle mass and function) within the first 2 weeks because it only pumps against the low-resistance pulmonary vascular bed. The arterial switch operation must be performed before this window closes — the Jatene procedure reimplants both great arteries in the correct position. Long-term outcomes are excellent: >95% survival to adulthood with near-normal life expectancy.

**Exam trigger:** *Cyanosed term neonate + no murmur + egg-on-a-string CXR* → TGA. Start PGE1 immediately. Urgent echocardiography + Rashkind septostomy. Arterial switch within 2 weeks. The distractor is assuming a murmur must be present for structural heart disease — it must not be present for TGA, and its absence with cyanosis is the hallmark.
