## Overview

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is defined haemodynamically as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) ≥20 mmHg at rest, measured by right heart catheterisation. It is a haemodynamic finding — not a diagnosis in itself — with a broad range of underlying causes grouped into five WHO classifications. The clinical priority is to identify which group the patient belongs to, as this fundamentally determines treatment. Group 2 (left heart disease) is the most common overall; Groups 1 and 4 are less common but clinically critical because they have specific, targeted interventions.

**WHO Classification of Pulmonary Hypertension:**

| Group | Category | Common Causes |
|-------|---------|--------------|
| **1** | Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) | Idiopathic, heritable (BMPR2 mutation), connective tissue disease (SSc, MCTD, SLE), congenital heart disease, portal hypertension (portopulmonary HTN), HIV, drugs (anorexigens, methamphetamine, dasatinib) |
| **2** | PH due to left heart disease | HFrEF, HFpEF, valvular disease (mitral stenosis, aortic stenosis) — most common group overall |
| **3** | PH due to lung disease/hypoxia | COPD, interstitial lung disease, OSA, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, high altitude |
| **4** | CTEPH (chronic thromboembolic PH) | Unresolved PE with organised thrombus occluding pulmonary arteries — potentially curative with surgery |
| **5** | Unclear/multifactorial | Sarcoidosis, haematological disorders (sickle cell disease), metabolic diseases, fibrosing mediastinitis |

**Haemodynamic severity grading (right heart catheterisation):**
- Mild: mPAP 20–40 mmHg
- Moderate: mPAP 41–55 mmHg
- Severe: mPAP >55 mmHg

**Prognosis:** Despite modern targeted therapy, untreated PAH carries a poor prognosis — approximately **50% of patients die within 5 years** of diagnosis. Early diagnosis and initiation of combination therapy are the most important determinants of outcome.

## Pathophysiology

In pulmonary arterial hypertension (Group 1), the primary abnormality is in the small pulmonary arteries themselves. Endothelial dysfunction leads to an imbalance between:
- **Vasoconstrictors** (endothelin-1, thromboxane A₂) — overproduced
- **Vasodilators** (prostacyclin, nitric oxide) — underproduced

This imbalance, combined with vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and in-situ thrombosis, progressively obliterates the pulmonary vascular bed. Heritable PAH involves loss-of-function mutations in **BMPR2** (bone morphogenetic protein receptor type 2) — a member of the TGF-β superfamily — which normally suppresses pulmonary arterial smooth muscle proliferation; ~75% of heritable PAH has this mutation.

The right ventricle adapts to the increased afterload through hypertrophy and dilation. Initially, RV function is preserved (compensated phase). Eventually, as pulmonary pressures rise further, the RV fails — RV filling pressures rise, leftward septal shift impairs LV filling, cardiac output falls, and the clinical syndrome of right heart failure develops. Once cardiac output begins to fall, syncope and near-syncope occur on exertion.

In **CTEPH (Group 4)**, unresolved acute PE leads to organised thrombus incorporated into the vessel wall. This obstructive element — combined with progressive secondary vasculopathy in small vessels not directly affected by clot — progressively increases pulmonary vascular resistance. The key distinguishing feature is that the obstructive proximal thrombus is surgically accessible.

## Clinical Presentation

PH is insidious and frequently diagnosed late — a median delay of **2–3 years** from symptom onset to diagnosis is typical, because early symptoms are non-specific and often attributed to deconditioning or anxiety.

**Symptoms:** Progressive exertional dyspnoea (most common initial symptom), exertional chest pain or **presyncope/syncope** (both indicate severely limited cardiac output reserve with exertion — carry a poor prognosis and should prompt urgent evaluation), fatigue, peripheral oedema as right heart failure develops, and Raynaud's phenomenon in connective tissue disease-associated PAH.

**Signs of PH and right heart failure:**
- **Loud P2** — elevated pulmonary artery pressure transmitted as a loud, palpable pulmonary component of the second heart sound
- **Right ventricular heave** — sustained impulse at the left lower sternal border (RV hypertrophy)
- **Pansystolic murmur** at the lower left sternal border — tricuspid regurgitation (louder with inspiration — Carvallo's sign)
- **Early diastolic murmur** at the left sternal border — pulmonary regurgitation (Graham Steell murmur) from dilated pulmonary valve annulus
- **Prominent 'a' wave in the JVP** — RV non-compliance (elevated end-diastolic pressure)
- Elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, ascites, hepatomegaly — right heart failure
- Signs of underlying cause: telangiectasiae and sclerodactyly in systemic sclerosis; digital clubbing in congenital heart disease; spider naevi and palmar erythema in portal hypertension

## Diagnosis

**Echocardiography** is the key screening investigation — estimates RVSP from tricuspid regurgitation jet velocity (using modified Bernoulli equation: RVSP = 4v² + RAP), assesses RV size and function, excludes congenital or valvular left heart disease, and identifies RV-LV interactions. Echo can both over- and underestimate pulmonary pressures — it does **not** confirm the diagnosis.

**Right heart catheterisation** is the gold standard — the only investigation that definitively measures mPAP, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac output. It is required to:
- Confirm PH (mPAP ≥20 mmHg)
- Classify it: **elevated PAWP >15 mmHg = postcapillary / Group 2** (left heart disease); **normal PAWP ≤15 mmHg with high PVR = precapillary / Groups 1, 3, 4, 5**
- Perform **vasoreactivity testing** in idiopathic PAH

**Vasoreactivity testing** (inhaled nitric oxide, IV epoprostenol, or IV adenosine during catheterisation): A positive response is defined as a fall in mPAP of ≥10 mmHg to an absolute value <40 mmHg without a fall in cardiac output. Only approximately 10% of idiopathic PAH patients respond — these individuals may benefit from long-term high-dose calcium channel blockers (amlodipine or nifedipine) rather than targeted PAH therapy.

**6-minute walk test (6MWT):** A functional assessment of exercise capacity. The distance walked in 6 minutes correlates with WHO functional class, haemodynamics, and survival. Serial 6MWT is used to monitor treatment response. Distance <300m indicates severely impaired functional capacity.

**Investigations to classify PH:**

| Investigation | Purpose |
|--------------|---------|
| CXR | Enlarged main pulmonary arteries, RV enlargement, pruned peripheral vessels; underlying lung disease |
| ECG | Right axis deviation, RVH, P pulmonale, RBBB, sinus tachycardia |
| PFTs (spirometry + DLCO) | Distinguish Group 3 causes; DLCO <60% predicted in PAH and ILD — an isolated low DLCO in systemic sclerosis should prompt PAH screening |
| HRCT chest | ILD (Group 3), emphysema, mosaic attenuation (CTEPH), mediastinal lymphadenopathy |
| V/Q scan | CTEPH (Group 4) — **V/Q is more sensitive than CTPA** for chronic organised thrombus because CT shows vessel filling defects only with acute/subacute disease |
| CTPA | Confirms acute PE; less sensitive for chronic CTEPH than V/Q |
| Autoimmune screen | ANA, anti-dsDNA, anti-Scl-70, anti-centromere (SSc), anti-Ro, anti-La, ANCA — connective tissue disease |
| HIV test, LFTs, hepatitis serology | Group 1 secondary causes |
| Sleep study | OSA (Group 3) |

## Management

### Group 2 — Treat the Underlying Left Heart Disease

Optimise HF therapy, treat valvular disease surgically if indicated. **PAH-targeted therapies are contraindicated in Group 2 PH** — they cause pulmonary vasodilation, reduce pulmonary vascular resistance, and flood the left ventricle with blood it cannot accommodate, precipitating acute pulmonary oedema.

### Group 3 — Treat the Underlying Lung Disease

Supplemental oxygen for hypoxia, CPAP for OSA. Smoking cessation and optimised pulmonary disease management. PAH-targeted therapies are generally not beneficial and may worsen V/Q mismatch.

### Group 4 — CTEPH

**Pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA):** Surgical removal of organised thrombus from the proximal pulmonary arteries under cardiopulmonary bypass and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. **Potentially curative** — normalises pulmonary haemodynamics in many patients. First-line for surgically accessible disease.

**Balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA):** Percutaneous dilation of diseased pulmonary arteries for inoperable distal disease; improves haemodynamics and functional capacity.

**Riociguat** (soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator) — the only PAH-targeted drug with proven benefit specifically for inoperable CTEPH; improves 6MWT distance and pulmonary haemodynamics. Do not combine with PDE-5 inhibitors (both act on the NO-cGMP pathway; additive hypotension risk).

Lifelong anticoagulation (warfarin, INR 2–3) for all CTEPH.

### Group 1 — Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension

Management targets the three pathways driving vasoconstriction and remodelling:

| Pathway | Drug Class | Agents |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| **Endothelin** pathway | Endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs) | Ambrisentan, macitentan, bosentan |
| **Nitric oxide** pathway | PDE-5 inhibitors | Sildenafil 20mg TDS, tadalafil 40mg OD |
| **Nitric oxide** pathway | Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulators | Riociguat |
| **Prostacyclin** pathway | Prostacyclin analogues | Epoprostenol (IV, continuous), iloprost (inhaled), treprostinil (SC/IV/inhaled), selexipag (oral) |

Modern management uses **upfront combination therapy** rather than sequential monotherapy. The AMBITION trial established that **macitentan + tadalafil** as initial combination therapy was superior to either agent alone in reducing clinical failure events. Most patients with intermediate-high risk disease should start combination ERA + PDE-5 inhibitor at diagnosis.

**Epoprostenol** (IV prostacyclin) remains the most effective agent for severe/high-risk PAH and the only PAH treatment to reduce mortality in a randomised controlled trial. It requires continuous IV infusion via a central line, which limits use to specialist centres.

**Calcium channel blockers** (amlodipine, nifedipine) — only for the ~10% of idiopathic PAH patients who are vasoreactive at right heart catheterisation. Do not use empirically without vasoreactivity testing — harmful in non-responders.

**Lung transplantation** — bilateral lung transplant for patients progressing despite maximal medical therapy; 5-year survival approximately 50–60% in PAH. Refer early — waiting list times are long.

**General measures:** Supervised exercise rehabilitation (now evidence-supported — supervised exercise, not unsupervised, which was historically discouraged); supplemental oxygen for hypoxaemia at rest or on exertion (target SpO₂ ≥92%); diuretics for RV fluid overload; anticoagulation in idiopathic PAH (controversial — evidence mixed); **avoid pregnancy** (high maternal and foetal mortality in PAH — pregnancy is contraindicated).

### Eisenmenger Syndrome

**Eisenmenger syndrome** represents the end-stage pulmonary vascular consequence of a long-standing left-to-right intracardiac shunt. It is one of the highest-yield congenital heart disease complications in USMLE and UKMLA examinations.

**Pathophysiology:** A congenital L→R shunt (most commonly ASD, VSD, or PDA) causes chronically elevated pulmonary blood flow. Over years to decades, the pulmonary vasculature responds with progressive vasoconstriction, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and vascular remodelling — an identical process to Group 1 PAH — until pulmonary vascular resistance rises to equal or exceed systemic vascular resistance. At this point, the pressure gradient driving L→R flow reverses: the shunt becomes **right-to-left**, and deoxygenated blood enters the systemic circulation.

**Clinical features:**
- **Central cyanosis** — R→L shunting bypasses the pulmonary circulation; lips and mucous membranes are blue
- **Clubbing** — from chronic hypoxaemia
- **Secondary polycythaemia** — compensatory erythropoietin-driven red cell production from chronic hypoxaemia; causes hyperviscosity symptoms (headache, visual disturbance, fatigue, TIA-like episodes)
- **Paradoxical embolism** — venous thrombus can cross the reversed shunt into the systemic arterial circulation → stroke; a pulmonary embolus in a normal person is filtered by the lungs, but in Eisenmenger's, it can pass directly to the brain
- **Haemoptysis** — from pulmonary arterial rupture or in-situ thrombosis
- **Syncope** — from fixed pulmonary vascular resistance and inability to increase cardiac output
- Signs of PAH and right heart failure: RV heave, loud P2, elevated JVP, peripheral oedema

**The critical rule — surgical closure is absolutely contraindicated once Eisenmenger syndrome is established.** Closing the defect in a patient whose pulmonary vascular resistance already equals systemic resistance removes the right ventricle's "escape valve." The RV has no route to decompress and immediately fails catastrophically. This is one of the most important contraindication rules in all of cardiology.

**Management:** PAH-targeted therapy (ERAs, PDE-5 inhibitors, prostacyclin analogues) — same classes used for Group 1 PAH — slow progression and improve functional capacity. Avoid dehydration (worsens haemoconcentration), iron deficiency (impairs erythropoiesis needed for the compensatory polycythaemia), and anaemia. Venesection for symptomatic hyperviscosity (Hb >200 g/L or haematocrit >65%) — cautiously, replacing volume with saline, not aggressively, as polycythaemia is compensatory. **Pregnancy is absolutely contraindicated** — maternal mortality approaches 30–50%, among the highest of any cardiac condition. Definitive treatment is **combined heart-lung transplantation** — rarely performed, long waiting times; outcomes are guarded.

### Cor Pulmonale

Cor pulmonale refers to RV structural changes (hypertrophy, dilation) and/or RV dysfunction caused specifically by **chronic lung disease and/or hypoxaemia** (Group 3 PH). It is the right heart consequence of chronic hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction.

**Signs:** Cyanosis; elevated JVP with prominent **a and v waves**; RV heave; loud P2; **Graham Steell murmur** (early diastolic, left sternal edge, from pulmonary regurgitation due to dilated pulmonary annulus); pansystolic tricuspid regurgitation murmur; hepatomegaly; peripheral oedema; secondary polycythaemia (Hb and haematocrit elevated from chronic hypoxaemia).

**Management:** LTOT (if PaO₂ <55 mmHg — the most important intervention); optimise volume status with fluid restriction and diuretics (furosemide, amiloride or spironolactone for potassium sparing); treat underlying lung disease maximally; manage arrhythmias. PAH-targeted therapies are generally not indicated (these patients are Group 3, not Group 1).

## Complications

- **Right heart failure** — the primary cause of death in PAH; characterised by elevated JVP, peripheral oedema, ascites, low cardiac output
- **Haemoptysis** — from rupture of dilated pulmonary vessels or in-situ thrombosis; can be massive
- **Sudden cardiac death** — arrhythmia in advanced disease; implantable devices not shown to improve outcomes in PAH
- **Paradoxical embolism** — via patent foramen ovale when right atrial pressure exceeds left; causes stroke in PAH
- **Syncope and sudden death on exertion** — from fixed cardiac output and inability to augment stroke volume

## Clinical Insight

The diagnosis of PAH should never be accepted without right heart catheterisation. Echocardiography regularly overestimates and underestimates pulmonary pressures — TR jet velocity is often imprecisely measured, and RVSP depends on assumed right atrial pressure. Treating a patient with expensive, potentially harmful PAH-targeted therapy based on echo alone — without confirmed precapillary haemodynamics — is a significant error. The PAWP is the most important number: if it is >15 mmHg, the patient has Group 2 PH (left heart disease) and PAH therapy is contraindicated and harmful.

Systemic sclerosis (especially limited cutaneous SSc, formerly CREST syndrome) carries a 10–15% lifetime risk of developing PAH, and it tends to be severe and progress rapidly. Annual screening with echocardiography and DLCO measurement is recommended for all SSc patients. A declining DLCO disproportionate to the spirometry change should trigger right heart catheterisation. Early detection before significant haemodynamic deterioration substantially improves outcomes.

CTEPH is one of the few conditions in medicine where surgery can be curative. Any patient with a history of PE who develops unexplained progressive breathlessness — even years later — should be evaluated for CTEPH with V/Q scanning, which is more sensitive than CTPA for organised chronic thrombus. Don't assume the breathlessness is deconditioning or residual PE damage — CTEPH is underdiagnosed and surgical endarterectomy can restore normal pulmonary pressures.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 34-year-old woman, 18 months of progressive exertional breathlessness and two episodes of exertional near-syncope. No cardiac or pulmonary history. ECG: right axis deviation + RVH. Echo: RVSP 72 mmHg, RV dilation, septal flattening.

**Step 1 — Recognise idiopathic PAH:** The clinical picture is textbook Group 1 PAH:
- **Young woman** — PAH affects women 2–3× more than men; peak onset in the third to fourth decade
- **Progressive exertional dyspnoea** — the most common initial symptom; insidious onset with a median 2–3 year diagnostic delay
- **Exertional near-syncope** — a red flag indicating severely impaired cardiac output reserve. The failing RV cannot augment stroke volume during exercise; forward flow falls precipitously and cerebral perfusion drops. This carries a poor prognosis and demands urgent evaluation.
- **ECG**: Right axis deviation + RVH = RV pressure overload
- **Echo**: RVSP 72 mmHg (severely elevated; normal <35 mmHg) + RV dilation + septal D-sign (septal flattening from RV pushing against the septum) = advanced RV strain

**Step 2 — Right heart catheterisation is mandatory — echo is not enough:** Echo estimates RVSP but cannot confirm the diagnosis or classify PH. **Right heart catheterisation** provides the definitive diagnosis:
- **mPAP ≥20 mmHg** confirms PH
- **PAWP ≤15 mmHg** = precapillary = Groups 1/3/4/5 (the lung vasculature is the problem)
- **PAWP >15 mmHg** = postcapillary = **Group 2 — left heart disease** — PAH-targeted drugs are absolutely contraindicated in Group 2; they cause pulmonary vasodilation, flood an already struggling left ventricle, and precipitate acute pulmonary oedema

**Step 3 — Investigate for secondary causes (screen before labelling idiopathic):**
- **ANA, anti-Scl-70, anti-centromere** — SSc (limited and diffuse) is the most common CTD cause of PAH; anti-centromere suggests limited SSc with PAH risk
- **HIV, hepatitis serology** — portopulmonary and HIV-associated PAH
- **V/Q scan** — exclude CTEPH (Group 4): V/Q is **more sensitive** than CTPA for chronic organised thrombus
- **Echo** with bubble study — exclude congenital heart disease (atrial or ventricular septal defect)
- **PFTs + HRCT** — exclude significant lung disease (Group 3)

**Step 4 — Upfront combination therapy (modern standard):** For WHO functional class II–IV PAH without vasoreactivity:
- **ERA + PDE-5 inhibitor** (macitentan 10mg OD + tadalafil 40mg OD) — established by the AMBITION trial as superior to monotherapy
- Add IV epoprostenol for high-risk or rapidly deteriorating disease
- **Avoid pregnancy** — contraindicated in PAH; maternal mortality approaches 25–50%

**Exam trigger:** *Young woman + exertional SOB + near-syncope + RVH on ECG + RVSP 72 mmHg on echo* → **pulmonary arterial hypertension**. Right heart catheterisation is mandatory — echo alone does not confirm the diagnosis. Check PAWP: >15 mmHg = Group 2 and PAH drugs are contraindicated. Upfront combination ERA + PDE-5 inhibitor is now standard of care.
