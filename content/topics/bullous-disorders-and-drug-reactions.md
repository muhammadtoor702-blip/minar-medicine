## Overview

Bullous (blistering) skin diseases are caused by disruption of adhesion molecules within the epidermis or at the dermoepidermal junction, producing blisters either within the epidermis (intraepidermal — fragile, rupturing easily) or beneath it (subepidermal — tense, thick-walled, more durable). **Identifying the level of blister formation and the underlying immunological mechanism determines diagnosis and management.** Severe cutaneous drug reactions, while distinct in aetiology, produce blistering and erosive disease of potentially fatal severity.

**First Aid high-yield overview:**
- Intraepidermal blisters → flaccid, rupture easily → pemphigus, SSSS
- Subepidermal blisters → tense, thick-walled → bullous pemphigoid, dermatitis herpetiformis
- Nikolsky sign = positive in pemphigus and TEN (not in pemphigoid)
- Mucosal involvement = pemphigus (prominent), TEN/SJS (prominent), pemphigoid (minimal)

## Bullous Pemphigoid

### Pathophysiology

Bullous pemphigoid (BP) is the **most common autoimmune blistering disease** — predominantly affecting elderly patients (mean age >70). Autoantibodies (IgG) target **BP180 (type XVII collagen)** and **BP230** — transmembrane hemidesmosomal proteins that anchor basal keratinocytes to the basement membrane. Antibody binding → complement activation → neutrophil and eosinophil recruitment → protease release → **separation of epidermis from dermis** → subepidermal blister.

BP180 and BP230 ELISA antibody titres correlate with disease activity and can be used to monitor treatment response.

The pruritic prodrome (urticarial plaques preceding blistering by weeks to months) reflects eosinophilic skin inflammation before full blistering develops.

### Clinical Presentation

- Large, **tense, thick-walled blisters** on an urticarial or normal base — trunk, flexures (groin, axillae), thighs, and forearms
- **Nikolsky sign NEGATIVE** — blister roof does not extend with lateral pressure (distinguishes from pemphigus)
- **Mucosal involvement is minimal or absent** (~10–30% have mild oral involvement — distinguishes from pemphigus vulgaris where oral involvement is prominent and preceding)
- Intense pruritus — may precede blisters by weeks to months
- Erythematous urticarial plaques in prodromal phase

**Association with neurological disease**: Multiple studies associate BP with dementia, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, and stroke — perhaps from neurological expression of BP180 in the CNS triggering cross-reactive immune responses. Ask about and screen for neurological history.

**Association with drugs**: BP can be triggered by medications — **dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (gliptins — particularly vildagliptin and saxagliptin)**, furosemide, spironolactone, ACE inhibitors, penicillins, neuroleptics. Review medications and consider withdrawing suspect drugs.

### Diagnosis

**Skin biopsy (perilesional skin)**: Subepidermal blister with eosinophilic infiltrate.

**Direct immunofluorescence (DIF)**: **Linear IgG and C3 along the dermoepidermal basement membrane zone (BMZ)** — hallmark finding. DIF on perilesional skin (not from blister base).

**Indirect immunofluorescence (IDIF)**: Detects circulating anti-BMZ antibodies in serum.

**BP180 and BP230 ELISA**: Quantitative antibody titres; correlate with severity; used for monitoring.

### Management

**Localised or mild disease:**
- **High-potency topical corticosteroids (clobetasol propionate 0.05%)** applied to the whole body surface — shown in a large RCT to be as effective as systemic corticosteroids for extensive BP, with fewer systemic side effects.

**Moderate-severe disease:**
- **Oral prednisolone** — starting dose 0.5–1 mg/kg/day. Taper once blistering controlled.
- **Steroid-sparing agents**: Add **doxycycline 200 mg/day** (anti-inflammatory, reduces eosinophil activation — good evidence, fewer side effects than steroid); alternatively azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil.

**Refractory disease:**
- **Rituximab** (anti-CD20) — increasingly used for refractory BP
- IVIG, omalizumab, dupilumab — emerging evidence

## Pemphigus Vulgaris

### Pathophysiology

Pemphigus vulgaris (PV) is an **intraepidermal** blistering disease caused by IgG autoantibodies against **desmoglein 3** (Dsg3 — in mucous membranes) and **desmoglein 1** (Dsg1 — in skin). Desmogleins are cadherin-type adhesion molecules in desmosomes — the intercellular "rivets" holding keratinocytes together. Antibody binding directly disrupts desmosomal adhesion → keratinocytes separate (**acantholysis**) → intraepidermal blister.

The Tzanck smear of the blister fluid shows **acantholytic cells** — rounded keratinocytes that have lost their connections ("Tzanck cells"). On histology, this produces the appearance of **"a row of tombstones"** — the basal layer keratinocytes remain attached to the basement membrane while the suprabasal layer separates.

**Pemphigus foliaceus**: Antibodies only against Dsg1 (no Dsg3) → only superficial skin involved, not mucous membranes (Dsg3 present in the mucosa compensates). Produces superficial, easily ruptured blisters and erosions. Serum pemphigus (Fogo Selvagem) is endemic in South America — same mechanism, triggered by insect bites.

### Clinical Presentation

**Oral erosions** — almost always the **first manifestation** of PV, often preceding skin involvement by months. Painful, slow-healing erosions and ulcers in the oral cavity, pharynx, oesophagus, genitalia, and conjunctivae.

**Skin blisters**: Thin-walled, **flaccid blisters** that rupture immediately — leaving extensive, painful, weeping erosions. Blisters on normal or erythematous skin.

**Nikolsky sign POSITIVE** — lateral pressure on normal-appearing skin beside a blister causes the epidermis to shear laterally, producing a new erosion.

Higher mortality than BP (~10% with treatment) from fluid loss, infection, and treatment complications.

**DIF**: Intercellular IgG and C3 between keratinocytes — **"chicken-wire" or "fish-net" pattern** (FA buzzword).

### Management

Systemic immunosuppression is required.

**First-line**: Oral prednisolone + **rituximab** (anti-CD20 — the RITUX3 trial showed rituximab superior to conventional steroid regimens in inducing durable remission). Alternatively, prednisolone + azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil.

## Pemphigus vs Pemphigoid — First Aid Comparison Table

| Feature | Pemphigus Vulgaris | Bullous Pemphigoid |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Age | Middle-aged (40–60) | Elderly (>70) |
| Level of blister | Intraepidermal | Subepidermal |
| Blister quality | **Flaccid, ruptures easily** | **Tense, thick-walled** |
| Nikolsky sign | **Positive** | **Negative** |
| Mucosal involvement | **Prominent, often first** | Minimal (<30%) |
| Antigen target | Desmoglein 1 and 3 | BP180 and BP230 |
| DIF pattern | **Intercellular "fish-net"** | **Linear BMZ** |
| Histology | Acantholysis (suprabasal split) | Subepidermal split + eosinophils |
| Pruritus | Less prominent | Often intense prodrome |
| Drug associations | Rare | Gliptins, ACEi, furosemide |
| Mortality | ~10% | Lower (but high in elderly with comorbidities) |
| First-line treatment | Prednisolone + rituximab | Topical clobetasol or prednisolone ± doxycycline |

## Dermatitis Herpetiformis

**First Aid high-yield** — commonly tested as a clinical vignette.

### Pathophysiology

Dermatitis herpetiformis (DH) is an autoimmune blistering disease that is the **cutaneous manifestation of coeliac disease (gluten sensitivity)**. IgA antibodies against **tissue transglutaminase (tTG)** and **epidermal transglutaminase (eTG)** deposit in the **dermal papillae (papillary dermis)**, activating complement and recruiting neutrophils, leading to subepidermal microabscesses and blistering.

Nearly all patients with DH have underlying small bowel enteropathy (coeliac-type changes), though most are asymptomatic from a GI perspective.

### Clinical Features

- **Intensely pruritic** papules, vesicles, and blisters — **grouped (herpetiform)** distribution
- Distribution: **symmetrical** on extensor surfaces — **elbows, knees, buttocks, sacrum, and scalp** (the "elbows and knees" rash)
- Vesicles are often excoriated before they can be examined (due to intense itch), leaving excoriations and erosions
- Associated features: iron deficiency anaemia, folate deficiency, osteoporosis, small bowel lymphoma (same associations as coeliac disease)

**FA buzzword**: "Intensely itchy blisters on elbows and knees, patient also has coeliac disease" = dermatitis herpetiformis.

### Diagnosis

- **Skin biopsy (perilesional skin)**: Neutrophil microabscesses in dermal papillae + subepidermal blistering
- **Direct immunofluorescence (DIF)**: **Granular IgA deposits in the dermal papillae** — pathognomonic (distinguishes from linear IgA disease, which has linear BMZ deposits)
- Serology: Anti-tTG IgA, anti-endomysial antibody (EMA) — correlate with gluten intake
- Small bowel biopsy: villous atrophy (even if asymptomatic)

### Management

- **Gluten-free diet** — the primary treatment; reduces DH activity over months to years and protects against coeliac complications (lymphoma, bone disease)
- **Dapsone** — rapid symptom relief (within 24–48 hours) while waiting for GFD to take effect; does not treat the underlying bowel disease. Monitor for haemolytic anaemia (G6PD screen before) and methaemoglobinaemia.

## Stevens-Johnson Syndrome and Toxic Epidermal Necrolysis

### Overview and Pathophysiology

Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) represent a spectrum of severe, potentially fatal cutaneous drug reactions characterised by widespread epidermal necrosis and detachment.

- **SJS**: <10% body surface area (BSA) epidermal detachment
- **SJS-TEN overlap**: 10–30% BSA
- **TEN**: >30% BSA — **mortality 20–30%**

The mechanism involves **drug-specific CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes** recognising drug-HLA complexes on keratinocytes → massive keratinocyte apoptosis via FasL and perforin-granzyme pathways → epidermal necrosis. This is not a simple allergy — it involves a specific immune recognition event.

**Causative drugs (most common) — FA high-yield:**
- **Allopurinol** (most common cause of TEN in Europe and Asia)
- **Aromatic anticonvulsants** (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbitone, lamotrigine — particularly in **HLA-B\*1502** carriers — Southeast Asian populations; screen before prescribing)
- **Sulfonamides** (co-trimoxazole, sulfasalazine)
- Nevirapine
- NSAIDs (oxicam class — piroxicam, meloxicam)
- Penicillins, cephalosporins

**FA mnemonic**: "**SCALPELS**" — Sulfonamides, Carbamazepine, Allopurinol, Lamotrigine, Phenytoin, Ethosuximide, Lasix (furosemide), and Stevens-Johnson meds.

**HLA-B\*5801** — strongly associated with allopurinol-induced SJS/TEN in Han Chinese and Southeast Asian populations. CPIC guidelines recommend genotyping before allopurinol in high-risk populations.

### Clinical Presentation

**Prodrome** (1–3 days): Fever, malaise, sore throat, eye burning — often misdiagnosed as viral infection.

**Skin**: Targetoid macules or diffuse erythema → rapidly progressing epidermal necrosis → **Nikolsky positive** → sheets of skin detach leaving raw, wet, erythematous dermis ("scalded skin" appearance). Blisters form but collapse immediately.

**Mucosal involvement** (>90% of patients — this distinguishes SJS/TEN from staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome, which is **mucosal-sparing**): Oral erosions (painful, haemorrhagic), conjunctival involvement (photophobia, discharge, symblepharon — adhesion of palpebral to bulbar conjunctiva — risk of blindness), genital and urethral erosions.

**SCORTEN score** — validated severity score for TEN (age, malignancy, HR, BSA detachment, urea, glucose, bicarbonate) — predicts mortality. Score ≥3 = >35% mortality.

**SJS vs SSSS (Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome):**

| Feature | SJS/TEN | SSSS |
|---------|---------|------|
| Age | Adults (usually) | Neonates/young children |
| Cause | Drug reaction | *S. aureus* exotoxin |
| Mucous membranes | Involved | Spared |
| Cleavage plane | Dermoepidermal junction (full thickness) | Subcorneal (superficial) |
| Nikolsky sign | Positive | Positive |
| Treatment | Stop drug; cyclosporin | IV flucloxacillin |

### Management

**Withdraw the causative drug immediately** — this is the single most important intervention. Each day of continued exposure worsens prognosis.

**Burns unit or specialist dermatology ICU**: Manage as a burns patient — fluid resuscitation, temperature regulation, wound care (non-adherent dressings, barrier creams), nutritional support, infection monitoring (but prophylactic antibiotics not recommended).

**Ophthalmology involvement from day 1**: Symblepharon prevention (eye lubricants, lysis of adhesions), long-term risk of corneal scarring and vision loss.

**Systemic immunomodulatory therapy**: Controversial — no strong RCT evidence:
- **Cyclosporin** — best evidence of benefit; stops the T-cell mediated cascade; start promptly
- **Etanercept** (anti-TNF) — randomised evidence of benefit (small trials)
- IVIG — used widely but RCT evidence negative
- **Avoid corticosteroids** — associated with increased mortality in most studies (increased infection risk, delayed re-epithelialisation)

**Long-term sequelae**: Ocular (dry eyes, scarring, photophobia, visual impairment), genital scarring and stenosis, pulmonary complications (bronchiolitis obliterans), psychological trauma.

## Drug Rash with Eosinophilia and Systemic Symptoms (DRESS)

DRESS is a severe drug hypersensitivity reaction with multi-organ involvement. Distinct from SJS/TEN — the mechanism involves **viral reactivation (HHV-6, EBV, CMV)** triggered by drug exposure rather than direct cytotoxic T-cell attack on keratinocytes.

**Onset**: **2–8 weeks** after starting the culprit drug (longer latency than other drug reactions — this delay is diagnostically important).

**Features**: Morbilliform widespread rash (erythematous, often with facial oedema — "red man" appearance), fever >38.5°C, lymphadenopathy, **hepatitis** (most common organ involved), interstitial nephritis, pneumonitis, myocarditis, marked **eosinophilia**, atypical lymphocytosis.

**Causative drugs**: Aromatic anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, lamotrigine), allopurinol, sulfonamides, minocycline, **abacavir (HLA-B\*5701)**.

**FA comparison — DRESS vs SJS/TEN:**

| Feature | DRESS | SJS/TEN |
|---------|-------|---------|
| Onset after drug | 2–8 weeks | 1–3 weeks |
| Skin | Morbilliform rash, facial oedema | Epidermal necrosis, detachment |
| Mucosal involvement | Absent or mild | Prominent |
| Eosinophilia | Marked | Absent |
| Organ involvement | Liver, kidney, heart, lung | Primarily skin/mucosa |
| Mechanism | Drug + viral reactivation (HHV-6) | Cytotoxic T-cell, drug-HLA specific |
| Mortality | ~10% | TEN: 20–30% |

**Management**: Withdraw causative drug immediately. Oral or IV corticosteroids (prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg) to suppress the systemic inflammation. Monitor LFTs, renal function, FBC. Symptoms can relapse weeks after the drug is stopped due to ongoing viral reactivation.

## Complications

- **Pemphigus vulgaris**: Sepsis (Nikolsky-positive erosions are portals of entry), fluid and electrolyte loss, malnutrition, steroid complications; mortality 5–10% with treatment
- **Bullous pemphigoid**: Bacterial superinfection, aspiration pneumonia, long-term steroid morbidity (osteoporosis, diabetes); mortality increased in elderly
- **Dermatitis herpetiformis**: Enteropathy (coeliac complications — anaemia, osteoporosis, small bowel lymphoma); dapsone haemolysis
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome/TEN**: Sepsis (leading cause of death), ARDS, multi-organ failure, ocular scarring (symblepharon, blindness), oesophageal and urethral strictures; TEN mortality up to 30%
- **DRESS**: Hepatitis, myocarditis, nephritis, haematological abnormalities; mortality approximately 10%; delayed organ involvement months after apparent resolution

## Clinical Insight

The distinction between pemphigus and pemphigoid saves lives and avoids unnecessary treatment. The clinical key: **pemphigoid has tense blisters, negative Nikolsky sign, and minimal mucosal involvement in an elderly patient** — it is managed with topical clobetasol or oral prednisolone. **Pemphigus has flaccid blisters, positive Nikolsky sign, and oral erosions preceding the rash** — it requires systemic immunosuppression including rituximab.

SJS and TEN are drug reactions, not infections. The prodromal fever and oral ulceration are frequently misinterpreted as viral illness, and the true diagnosis is delayed while the causative drug continues. Any patient with progressive mucocutaneous erosions and epidermal peeling — especially with **bilateral conjunctival involvement** — must be assessed for drug causation and the responsible drug stopped immediately. Every additional day of drug exposure increases the extent of necrosis.

HLA-B\*5801 testing before prescribing allopurinol in patients of Han Chinese, Thai, Korean, and Vietnamese descent is now widely recommended — this single genetic test prevents the majority of allopurinol-associated SJS/TEN in high-risk populations. The cost of the test is trivial compared with the mortality and morbidity of TEN.

Dermatitis herpetiformis is the most commonly missed diagnosis in the blistering disease spectrum. A patient with intensely itchy vesicles on the elbows and knees, who has had diarrhoea, bloating, or a known family history of coeliac disease, should have perilesional skin biopsied for DIF — granular IgA at the dermal papillae tips is pathognomonic. The fastest symptomatic treatment is dapsone, but the only curative and preventive treatment is a lifelong gluten-free diet.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 72-year-old woman with 3 weeks of large, tense, clear blisters on trunk, thighs, and forearms. Blisters arise on normal or erythematous skin. Mild pruritus. No mucosal involvement. Blisters do not rupture easily with lateral pressure (Nikolsky negative). Biopsy: subepidermal separation. DIF: linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone.

**Step 1 — Diagnose bullous pemphigoid using the key diagnostic features:** Work through the discriminating features: (1) **Subepidermal split** on histology (not intraepidermal) → rules out pemphigus vulgaris; (2) **Tense, thick-walled blisters** that don't rupture easily (not flaccid) → consistent with BP, not PV; (3) **Nikolsky negative** (pressure does not extend the blister laterally) → BP; (4) **No mucosal involvement** → BP (PV almost always has prominent oral erosions preceding the rash); (5) **Linear IgG and C3 at the BMZ on DIF** → BP (the pathognomonic DIF pattern — PV shows intercellular "fish-net" IgG). (6) **Age >70** → the classic BP patient. All criteria point to **bullous pemphigoid**.

**Step 2 — Review medications — gliptins, furosemide, and ACE inhibitors can precipitate BP:** In a 72-year-old, review the medication list carefully. DPP-4 inhibitors (gliptins — particularly vildagliptin, saxagliptin), furosemide, spironolactone, ACEi, and penicillins are all associated with drug-induced BP. If a likely culprit is identified, discontinue it — this may allow disease resolution without systemic immunosuppression. Check also for recent BP180 antibody titres — elevated titres confirm the diagnosis and provide a quantitative baseline for monitoring treatment response.

**Step 3 — Assess severity and choose initial management:** For a 72-year-old with extensive blistering on trunk, thighs, and forearms, this is **moderate-severe disease**. Options: (1) **High-potency topical corticosteroids** (clobetasol propionate 0.05% applied to the whole body surface) — shown to be as effective as systemic steroids with fewer systemic side effects (particularly important in elderly patients where steroid complications — diabetes, bone loss, infection — are high-risk); (2) **Oral prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg/day** for rapid control of extensive or rapidly progressive disease; (3) **Add doxycycline 200mg/day** as a steroid-sparing agent — anti-inflammatory, reduces eosinophil activation, significantly reduces steroid requirement.

**Step 4 — Monitor for complications specific to this population:** Elderly patients on corticosteroids for BP face compounded risks: osteoporosis (start bone protection — bisphosphonate + calcium + vitamin D from day 1 of steroids), immunosuppression (vaccination — pneumococcal, shingles, influenza — before starting), diabetes (monitor glucose), and falls (from steroid myopathy and sedation from antihistamines used for pruritus). BP also independently associates with dementia and Parkinson's — screen neurological history and review cognitive status.

**Step 5 — Taper once disease is controlled and monitor with antibody titres:** Once no new blisters are forming and existing blisters are healing, taper prednisolone slowly over months. Use BP180 and BP230 ELISA titres to guide tapering — rising titres predict relapse before clinical flare. Doxycycline (or azathioprine/mycophenolate) allows the steroid to be tapered more quickly. Most patients achieve remission within 1–2 years; BP can resolve completely in approximately 30–50%.

**Exam trigger:** *Elderly patient + large tense blisters + Nikolsky negative + no mucosal involvement + linear IgG and C3 at BMZ on DIF* → **bullous pemphigoid**. The contrast with pemphigus vulgaris is a high-yield exam topic: PV = flaccid blisters + Nikolsky positive + oral erosions first + intercellular "fish-net" IgG on DIF + middle-aged patient. BP = tense blisters + Nikolsky negative + minimal mucosa + linear BMZ IgG + elderly. The distractor is treating all autoimmune blistering disease the same — BP has an evidence base for topical clobetasol which PV does not.
