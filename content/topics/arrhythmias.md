## Overview

Arrhythmias arise from disturbances in the generation or conduction of electrical impulses through the heart. They are common, often intermittent (making diagnosis difficult), and range from entirely benign ectopics to immediately life-threatening VF. Three mechanisms account for virtually all arrhythmias:

1. **Re-entry** — the most common mechanism. An electrical impulse travels in a self-sustaining circuit when two pathways exist with different conduction velocities and refractory periods. Accounts for AVNRT, atrial flutter, most VT, and AF.
2. **Abnormal automaticity** — an ectopic pacemaker fires spontaneously outside the sinus node (junctional rhythm, accelerated idioventricular rhythm, some VT).
3. **Triggered activity** — afterdepolarisations generate new action potentials. Early afterdepolarisations (during repolarisation) cause torsades de pointes in long QT. Delayed afterdepolarisations (after repolarisation) cause digoxin toxicity-related arrhythmias.

**Causes:**
- Cardiac: IHD, cardiomyopathy, pericarditis, myocarditis, structural abnormalities (mitral stenosis), aberrant pathways (WPW)
- Metabolic: hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypoxia, acidosis, thyrotoxicosis, phaeochromocytoma
- Drugs: digoxin toxicity, beta-2 agonists, tricyclics, antipsychotics, antiarrhythmics (proarrhythmic effect), doxorubicin
- Stimulants: caffeine, cocaine, alcohol

## Approach to Any Tachycardia

**Step 1 — Haemodynamic stability:** If the patient has shock, loss of consciousness, chest pain from ischaemia, or acute pulmonary oedema → **immediate synchronised DC cardioversion**, regardless of rhythm. Do not waste time with drugs in an unstable patient.

**Step 2 — QRS width:**
- **Narrow QRS (<120ms):** Supraventricular — originates above the His bundle. AV nodal blocking agents are safe.
- **Wide QRS (≥120ms):** Treat as VT until proven otherwise. Giving verapamil to a patient actually in VT — mistaking it for SVT with aberrancy — can cause haemodynamic collapse and death.

## Narrow Complex Tachycardias

Normal conduction begins at the sinoatrial node (SAN), spreads across the atria, pauses at the AV node, then travels down the bundle of His → left and right bundle branches → ventricular myocardium (apex to outflow tract). Narrow QRS means depolarisation is occurring via normal pathways.

### Sinus Tachycardia

Rate 100–220 bpm. Upright P waves in lead II preceding each QRS. This is a **physiological response**, not a primary arrhythmia. Causes: pain, fever, hypovolaemia, anaemia, PE, sepsis, hyperthyroidism, anxiety, drugs (caffeine, nicotine, salbutamol, cocaine). Always treat the cause — antiarrhythmics are counterproductive.

### AVNRT (AV Nodal Re-entrant Tachycardia)

The most common paroxysmal SVT. A re-entry circuit uses fast and slow pathways within or adjacent to the AV node. Rate 150–250 bpm, perfectly regular. Triggered by a PAC that finds the fast pathway refractory and conducts down the slow pathway, then back up the fast pathway → self-sustaining circuit.

**ECG:** No visible P waves (buried within QRS) or retrograde P waves immediately after QRS. Pseudo-R' in V1 (retrograde P wave distorting terminal QRS) and pseudo-S in inferior leads (II, III, aVF) are characteristic — look carefully for these subtle signs.

**Management (stepwise):**
1. **Vagal manoeuvres** — modified Valsalva (patient strains then is repositioned supine with passive leg raise for 15 seconds) is the most effective, with a success rate ~40–50%. Carotid sinus massage (avoid if carotid bruit present; risk of atherosclerotic embolism).
2. **Adenosine IV** — 6mg rapid IV bolus with immediate 20ml saline flush; repeat 12mg if fails; then 18mg. Half-life ~10 seconds — must be given rapidly into a large vein (antecubital preferred). Transiently blocks the AV node, breaking the re-entry circuit. Warn the patient of transient chest tightness, flushing, and sense of doom. **Contraindicated in asthma** (severe bronchospasm) and in **AF with WPW** (forced conduction down accessory pathway → VF risk).
3. **Verapamil 5–10mg IV** — alternative if adenosine contraindicated (not in WPW; not in broad complex tachycardia).
4. **Synchronised DC cardioversion** — if drugs fail or patient is unstable.
5. **Long-term:** Catheter ablation of the slow pathway — curative in >95% of cases; recommended for symptomatic recurrent episodes.

### Atrial Flutter

Macro re-entry circuit within the right atrium around the tricuspid annulus (cavotricuspid isthmus). Atrial rate ~300 bpm, ventricular rate determined by degree of AV block:
- 2:1 block → ventricular rate 150 bpm (most common; a regular tachycardia at exactly 150 bpm should always raise suspicion of flutter)
- 3:1 block → 100 bpm
- 4:1 block → 75 bpm

**ECG:** Classic sawtooth flutter waves at ~300 bpm, best seen in inferior leads (II, III, aVF) and V1. The ventricular response is regular or regularly irregular.

**Management:** Same anticoagulation principles as AF (same thromboembolic risk). Rate control as for AF. DC cardioversion highly effective. Cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) ablation is highly curative (>95%) and often recommended as first-line for symptomatic or recurrent flutter because recurrence rates on medical therapy are high.

### Focal Atrial Tachycardia

A discrete group of atrial cells outside the SAN acts as a pacemaker, outpacing it. Rate 100–250 bpm. P-wave morphology is different from sinus P waves. Causes include digoxin toxicity (characteristic — do not give more digoxin; treat toxicity).

### Junctional Tachycardia

The AV node itself becomes the pacemaker. Narrow QRS; P waves may be inverted and immediately follow the QRS (retrograde atrial activation).

## Wide Complex Tachycardias

**Assume ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise.** The consequences of misidentifying VT as SVT with aberrancy and giving verapamil are potentially fatal. Treating SVT with aberrancy as VT (e.g., with amiodarone) is far less dangerous than the reverse.

**ECG features that strongly favour VT over SVT with aberrancy:**

| Feature | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| AV dissociation (P waves marching independently through QRS) | Pathognomonic of VT |
| Fusion beats (sinus + ventricular impulse fusing) | Pathognomonic of VT |
| Capture beats (normal QRS amid VT — sinus impulse briefly captures ventricles) | Pathognomonic of VT |
| QRS >160ms | Extreme width favours VT |
| Positive or negative concordance across all chest leads (all QRS upright or all negative V1–V6) | VT |
| Northwest axis (positive in aVR, negative in I and aVF) | VT |
| History of MI, LV dysfunction, or cardiomyopathy | Almost always VT |

### Ventricular Tachycardia (VT)

≥3 consecutive ventricular beats at >100 bpm. **Monomorphic** (uniform QRS) = typically from a single re-entry circuit, often in scar tissue post-MI. **Polymorphic** (varying QRS) = multiple circuits or changing focus; associated with prolonged QT, ischaemia, or channelopathies.

| Situation | Management |
|-----------|-----------|
| Pulseless VT | CPR + immediate unsynchronised defibrillation (200–360J) |
| Haemodynamically unstable with pulse | Synchronised DC cardioversion |
| Haemodynamically stable | IV amiodarone 150mg over 10 min, then 1mg/min for 6h, then 0.5mg/min. Procainamide as alternative. |

Long-term: ICD for secondary prevention (sustained VT or VF with structural heart disease). Beta-blocker. Treat underlying cause. Catheter ablation for recurrent monomorphic VT.

### Torsades de Pointes

Polymorphic VT with a sinusoidal twisting QRS axis around the baseline — "twisting of the points." Always associated with a **prolonged QT interval** (QTc >500ms significantly increases risk). The QT is corrected for heart rate using Bazett's formula; it is temperature- and rate-dependent.

**Causes of long QT:**

| Category | Examples |
|---------|---------|
| Antiarrhythmics | Amiodarone, sotalol, quinidine, procainamide |
| Antipsychotics | Haloperidol, quetiapine, chlorpromazine |
| Antibiotics | Azithromycin, clarithromycin, fluconazole, moxifloxacin |
| Other drugs | Methadone, ondansetron, domperidone |
| Electrolytes | Hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hypocalcaemia |
| Congenital | Romano-Ward syndrome (autosomal dominant); Jervell-Lange-Nielsen (autosomal recessive + congenital deafness) |

**Management:**
- **IV magnesium sulphate 2g over 5–15 minutes** — first-line for stable torsades, even when Mg²⁺ is normal; shortens QT and suppresses early afterdepolarisations
- Remove the offending drug immediately
- Correct electrolytes: target K⁺ >4.5 mEq/L, Mg²⁺ >1.0 mEq/L
- DC cardioversion if unstable
- Temporary pacing at a faster rate (70–90 bpm) — shortens QT by increasing rate; useful for bradycardia-dependent torsades
- ICD for congenital LQTS with recurrent events or cardiac arrest

### Ventricular Fibrillation

Chaotic, irregular electrical activity — no coordinated contraction, no cardiac output. Treated as cardiac arrest — CPR + immediate unsynchronised defibrillation (200–360J). See ALS algorithm.

### Ventricular Ectopics (Extrasystoles)

Common and usually benign in structurally normal hearts. Broad QRS (>120ms), occur earlier than expected, followed by a compensatory pause. On ECG, may appear as:
- **Bigeminy** — ectopic every other beat (ECG machines may halve the calculated rate)
- **Trigeminy** — every third beat
- **Couplets** — two consecutive ectopics
- **Triplets** — three consecutive ectopics (= 3-beat run of VT)

Occasional ectopics in healthy patients: reassure. Frequent ectopics (>60/hour), couplets, or triplets in a patient with structural heart disease or symptoms: Holter monitoring, echo, consider electrophysiology referral.

## Bradyarrhythmias and Heart Block

### Heart Block

| Degree | ECG | Clinical Significance | Management |
|--------|-----|----------------------|-----------|
| First degree | PR >200ms; all P waves conduct | Benign; common in athletes and on beta-blockers | None required |
| Second degree Mobitz I (Wenckebach) | Progressive PR lengthening then dropped QRS; PR resets | Block at AV node; often benign; common in inferior MI | Usually conservative; atropine if symptomatic |
| Second degree Mobitz II | Fixed PR then sudden non-conducted P wave, without warning | Block below AV node (His-Purkinje); high risk of progression to complete block | Permanent pacemaker |
| Third degree (complete) | Complete AV dissociation; P waves and QRS completely independent; escape rhythm (junctional or ventricular) | Inferior MI → often transient, narrow escape rhythm, responds to atropine; Anterior MI → usually permanent, wide escape rhythm, requires pacing | Transcutaneous pacing acutely; permanent pacemaker |

The RCA supplies the AV node in 90% of people — inferior MI causes AV block that is often transient and responds to atropine. Anterior MI damages the bundle branches and causes permanent complete heart block requiring pacing.

### Sinus Node Dysfunction (Sick Sinus Syndrome)

Fibrosis of the sinoatrial node — typically elderly patients. Causes sinus bradycardia, sinus pauses, or alternating tachycardia and bradycardia ("tachy-brady syndrome"). Management: thromboembolism prophylaxis if AF episodes detected; permanent pacemaker for symptomatic bradycardia. Tachy-brady syndrome: pacing for bradycardia + rate-slowing drugs for tachycardia.

## Pacemakers

When the heart does not pace itself adequately, artificial pacing is required. Options range from transcutaneous (through defibrillator pads) to temporary transvenous pacing to subcutaneously implanted permanent devices.

**Indications for permanent pacemaker (PPM):**
- Complete AV block (symptomatic or asymptomatic)
- Mobitz type II block
- Persistent AV block after anterior MI
- Symptomatic bradycardia (sinus node dysfunction)
- Drug-resistant tachyarrhythmias (overdrive pacing)
- Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT) in HFrEF

**Pacemaker code (first 3 letters minimum):**
- 1st letter: chamber paced (A=atria, V=ventricle, D=dual)
- 2nd letter: chamber sensed (A, V, D, O=none)
- 3rd letter: response (T=triggered, I=inhibited, D=dual)
- 4th letter: R=rate modulation (adjusts rate with activity)

VVI = paces ventricle, senses ventricle, inhibited if spontaneous beat detected (most common single-chamber mode).

**Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT):** Biventricular pacing — both the septum and lateral wall of the LV are stimulated simultaneously, correcting the dyssynchrony in LBBB. Indicated for EF <35% + LBBB + QRS >130ms + NYHA II–IV despite optimal medical therapy. Reduces mortality, improves EF and symptoms. Often combined with ICD (CRT-D).

## Wolff-Parkinson-White Syndrome

WPW involves a congenital **accessory pathway** (bundle of Kent) connecting atria to ventricles, bypassing the AV node. Pre-excitation produces the characteristic triad:
- Short PR (<120ms)
- Delta wave (slurred initial upstroke of QRS from early ventricular activation via accessory pathway)
- Broad QRS (fusion of pre-excited and normally conducted beats)

Two subtypes: WPW type A (positive delta wave in V1 — left-sided pathway), WPW type B (negative delta wave in V1 — right-sided pathway).

**The critical danger: AF in WPW.** The accessory pathway has no decremental conduction properties — it conducts AF impulses at the full rate of 300+ bpm directly to the ventricles, causing extreme tachycardia that can degenerate to VF and sudden cardiac death.

**ECG of AF in WPW:** Chaotically irregular, extremely wide, bizarre QRS complexes at >200 bpm — unmistakable and terrifying in appearance.

**Treatment of AF in WPW:**
- Haemodynamically unstable → immediate DC cardioversion
- Stable → IV procainamide or flecainide (slows accessory pathway conduction)
- **Absolutely contraindicated: adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin** — all block the AV node, forcing all conduction through the accessory pathway, potentially triggering VF

**Long-term:** Catheter ablation of the accessory pathway — curative, success rate >95%, first-line recommendation.

## Congenital Arrhythmia Syndromes

These are clinically silent until potentially fatal and are likely responsible for most cases of sudden arrhythmic death syndrome (SADS) in young people.

**Long QT Syndrome (LQTS):** Channelopathies (mutated K⁺ or Na⁺ channels) prolonging repolarisation → early afterdepolarisations → torsades de pointes. Symptoms: syncope during exertion or emotional stress, or sudden death. Management: avoid QT-prolonging drugs, beta-blocker, ICD for high-risk.

**Brugada Syndrome:** Sodium channelopathy. Diagnosis: classic coved ST elevation in V1–V3 on ECG (spontaneous or unmasked by fever, drugs, or electrolyte imbalance) + suggestive clinical history (family history of SCD, nocturnal syncope). Check www.brugadadrugs.org for a comprehensive drug list to avoid. ICD for symptomatic patients.

**ARVC (Arrhythmogenic Right Ventricular Cardiomyopathy):** Fibro-fatty replacement of RV myocardium from desmosomal protein mutations. ECG: epsilon wave (terminal notch after QRS in V1–V3), T-wave inversion in V1–V3. Presents with VT (LBBB morphology — ectopic in RV), palpitations, syncope in young athletes. Restrict from competitive sport; beta-blocker; ICD.

## Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillator (ICD)

Prevents sudden cardiac death from VT/VF. Continuously monitors cardiac rhythm; delivers shocks or anti-tachycardia pacing automatically.

**Indications (primary prevention):**
- HFrEF with EF <35% despite ≥3 months of optimal medical therapy (NYHA II–III)
- Prior MI + LVEF ≤30%
- High-risk inherited arrhythmia syndromes (LQTS, Brugada, HCM, ARVC)

**Indications (secondary prevention):**
- Prior sustained VT or VF without reversible cause
- Resuscitated cardiac arrest

## Complications

- **Sudden cardiac death** — VF, WPW with AF, torsades de pointes, HCM-related VT
- **Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy** — reversible with adequate rate control
- **Stroke and systemic embolism** — from AF; treated with anticoagulation
- **Syncope and trauma** — from arrhythmia-induced hypotension
- **ICD complications** — inappropriate shocks (from oversensing or AF), lead dislodgement, infection, tricuspid valve damage

## Clinical Insight

The most dangerous error in arrhythmia management is giving verapamil to a patient with VT, or to a patient in AF with WPW. Both can precipitate haemodynamic collapse or VF. In any wide complex tachycardia in a patient with a prior history of MI or structural heart disease, the rhythm is VT until proven otherwise.

Adenosine is both diagnostic and therapeutic in narrow complex SVT. If the rhythm terminates, the diagnosis is confirmed as AV-nodal dependent (AVNRT or AVRT). If the ventricular rate slows transiently but the atrial activity continues — revealing flutter waves or irregular AF — the diagnosis is an atrial arrhythmia and the treatment strategy changes entirely.

A QTc >500ms in a patient just started on a new antiarrhythmic, antipsychotic, or antibiotic is a clinical alarm requiring action — either dose reduction, drug change, or electrolyte correction — before torsades occurs.

Screening young competitive athletes with a personal history of syncope during exertion, or a family history of sudden cardiac death in a relative under 40, is essential. The ECG may be the only warning before a fatal event.
