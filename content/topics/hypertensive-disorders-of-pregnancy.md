## Overview

Hypertensive disorders complicate approximately 10% of pregnancies and remain a leading cause of maternal mortality globally. Getting the classification right is not just semantic — it determines management, timing of delivery, and surveillance intensity.

**Classification:**

| Condition | Definition |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Chronic hypertension** | Pre-existing BP ≥140/90 before 20 weeks' gestation or pre-pregnancy |
| **Gestational hypertension** | New BP ≥140/90 after 20 weeks without significant proteinuria |
| **Pre-eclampsia** | New hypertension after 20 weeks + significant proteinuria (≥300 mg/24h or PCR ≥30 mg/mmol) OR end-organ dysfunction without proteinuria |
| **Eclampsia** | Tonic-clonic seizures complicating pre-eclampsia |
| **Superimposed pre-eclampsia** | Pre-eclampsia developing in a woman with pre-existing chronic hypertension |

The **severity threshold** that defines severe pre-eclampsia: sBP ≥160 or dBP ≥110 on two occasions, or severe end-organ features (severe headache, visual disturbance, epigastric pain, pulmonary oedema, oliguria, eclampsia, HELLP, thrombocytopenia <100 × 10⁹/L, renal impairment).

## Pathophysiology

This is one of the most important pathophysiology stories in obstetrics — understanding it explains every management decision.

**Normal placentation**: Extravillous trophoblasts invade the decidua and myometrium in the first trimester, remodelling the spiral arteries. These high-resistance vessels are converted into wide, low-resistance, high-flow conduits — essential to meet the growing placental perfusion demands of the second and third trimesters.

**In pre-eclampsia**: This remodelling fails. Spiral arteries retain their musculoelastic wall and remain high-resistance. The result is **placental ischaemia and oxidative stress**, which triggers release of:
- **sFlt-1** (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1): an anti-angiogenic protein that binds and sequesters VEGF and PlGF, preventing them from acting on maternal endothelium
- **Endothelin-1** and reduced nitric oxide: promote vasoconstriction

These circulating factors cause **systemic maternal endothelial dysfunction** — the unifying explanation for every manifestation of pre-eclampsia:
- Endothelial dysfunction in renal vasculature → glomerular endotheliosis → proteinuria + reduced GFR
- In the cerebral vasculature → cerebral oedema → headache, visual disturbance, eclampsia
- In the hepatic vasculature → hepatocyte ischaemia → elevated transaminases, epigastric pain, subcapsular haematoma
- In the systemic vasculature → hypertension, peripheral oedema
- In the uteroplacental circulation → IUGR, oligohydramnios, placental abruption

**The critical teaching point**: The placenta is the cause. Delivery is the cure. No antihypertensive drug treats the underlying disorder — it only manages the BP to buy time and prevent maternal end-organ damage.

**sFlt-1/PlGF ratio**: This biomarker test is now used clinically. A ratio >38 (using the Elecsys assay) predicts development of pre-eclampsia within 4 weeks with sensitivity ~80% and specificity ~95%. A ratio <38 has a strong negative predictive value — it can help rule out pre-eclampsia in women presenting with suspected symptoms. Useful when the clinical picture is ambiguous.

## Clinical Presentation

Mild pre-eclampsia may be entirely **asymptomatic** — detected only on routine BP checks and urine dipstick at antenatal appointments. This is why regular surveillance matters.

**Symptomatic pre-eclampsia** (warning features that demand urgent assessment):
- Severe headache — frontal, not relieved by paracetamol
- Visual disturbance — "flashing lights" (photopsia), blurred vision, scotomata
- Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain (hepatic capsule distension or HELLP)
- Sudden severe oedema — especially facial and hand swelling
- Reduced fetal movement

**Examination findings**: Hypertension, facial/peripheral oedema, hyperreflexia, clonus (≥3 beats is significant — indicates neurological irritability and impending eclampsia), papilloedema in severe cases.

**HELLP syndrome** deserves separate mention. It stands for:
- **H**: Haemolysis — microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (raised LDH >600 IU/L, raised bilirubin, low haptoglobin, fragmented red cells on blood film)
- **EL**: Elevated Liver enzymes — ALT/AST >70 IU/L (or twice the upper limit of normal)
- **LP**: Low Platelets — <100 × 10⁹/L

HELLP typically presents with epigastric/RUQ pain, nausea, malaise, and right upper quadrant tenderness. Here is the **critical exam trap**: HELLP may occur with **minimal or no hypertension** in up to 25% of cases. Do not dismiss the diagnosis because the BP is only mildly elevated. The most feared complication is **hepatic subcapsular haematoma** → rupture → haemoperitoneum → cardiovascular collapse.

## Diagnosis

**Investigations for suspected pre-eclampsia:**

| Investigation | What you're looking for |
|--------------|------------------------|
| BP (two readings ≥4 hours apart) | Confirms hypertension |
| Urine PCR or 24-hour protein | ≥30 mg/mmol PCR = significant proteinuria |
| FBC | Low platelets (HELLP), haemoconcentration |
| U&E | Rising creatinine (normal in pregnancy is lower than non-pregnant — creatinine >0.9 mg/dL is abnormal in pregnancy) |
| LFTs | Elevated transaminases (HELLP) |
| LDH | Haemolysis marker (HELLP) |
| Blood film | Schistocytes/fragmented cells |
| Urate | Raised, not a management criterion but correlates with severity |
| Coagulation screen | PT, APTT, fibrinogen (DIC screen) |
| sFlt-1/PlGF ratio | Diagnostic adjunct |
| CTG | Fetal wellbeing |
| USS + Doppler | Fetal growth, liquor volume, umbilical artery Dopplers (absent/reversed end-diastolic flow = severe placental insufficiency) |

## Management

**Antihypertensive treatment**: The goal is to prevent maternal cerebrovascular events (haemorrhagic stroke is a major cause of death). Target sBP 130–150 mmHg, dBP 80–100 mmHg. Avoid excessive reduction — the fetus depends on uteroplacental perfusion pressure.

*Acute severe hypertension (sBP ≥160 or dBP ≥110)*:
- **IV labetalol** (combined alpha + beta blockade) — first-line, 50 mg IV over 1 minute, repeat every 5 minutes to max 200 mg; infusion if boluses insufficient. **Contraindicated in asthma** (beta blockade).
- **Oral nifedipine** (modified-release, calcium channel blocker) — 10–20 mg oral; also effective for acute control.
- **IV hydralazine** (direct vasodilator) — alternative, but more reflex tachycardia.
- **Avoid ACE inhibitors** — teratogenic (fetal renal agenesis/oligohydramnios) and contraindicated in pregnancy.

*Oral maintenance*: labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa.

**Magnesium sulphate for seizure prophylaxis** (and treatment of eclampsia):

*Zuspan regimen*: 4 g IV loading dose over 20–30 minutes, then 1 g/hour IV maintenance infusion. Continue for 24 hours after delivery or after last seizure, whichever is later.

**Magnesium toxicity monitoring** — one of the most commonly tested topics:

| Serum Mg level (mEq/L) | Effect |
|----------------------|--------|
| 4–8 | Therapeutic range (seizure prophylaxis) |
| 7–10 | Loss of patellar reflexes — first sign of toxicity |
| 10–13 | Respiratory depression — **check reflexes hourly** |
| >15 | Cardiac arrest |

Clinical monitoring: **check patellar reflexes hourly** (loss = stop infusion), respiratory rate ≥12/min, urine output ≥25 mL/hour (Mg excreted renally — renal impairment = toxicity risk).

**Antidote**: **Calcium gluconate 10 mL of 10% solution IV** over 10 minutes. Keep it at the bedside.

**Eclampsia management**:
1. Call for help, place in lateral decubitus (reduces aortocaval compression + recovery position)
2. Protect airway, O₂
3. **Magnesium 4 g IV over 5–10 minutes** (first-line over benzodiazepines — the **Magpie trial** showed Mg reduces seizure recurrence by 58% and maternal death, superior to diazepam and phenytoin)
4. If seizures recur on Mg: 2 g bolus, or consider diazepam
5. Stabilise BP, deliver when safe

**Aspirin prophylaxis** (prevention, not treatment): **150 mg OD from 11–13+6 weeks** in high-risk women. High-risk criteria include: previous pre-eclampsia, chronic hypertension, diabetes, CKD, autoimmune disease (SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome), multifetal pregnancy, BMI >35, first pregnancy. Two or more moderate-risk factors (nulliparity, age >40, family history, Afro-Caribbean ethnicity, inter-pregnancy interval >10 years) also warrant prophylaxis. The **ASPRE trial** demonstrated a 62% reduction in pre-eclampsia <37 weeks with 150 mg aspirin vs. placebo in a high-risk screen-positive population.

**Timing of delivery**:
- Mild pre-eclampsia with good control: aim for delivery at **37 weeks**
- Pre-eclampsia with controlled BP on medication: delivery at **37 weeks**
- Severe pre-eclampsia or uncontrolled BP: **delivery as soon as safely possible** regardless of gestational age (after stabilisation)
- HELLP syndrome: **deliver promptly** — rarely improves without delivery
- <34 weeks: give **corticosteroids** (betamethasone 12 mg IM two doses 24 hours apart) for fetal lung maturity if time allows

## Complications

**Maternal**: Eclampsia (seizures → cerebral haemorrhage, aspiration), cerebrovascular accident (haemorrhagic stroke — major cause of maternal death), HELLP syndrome with hepatic rupture, DIC, pulmonary oedema (from fluid overload/capillary leak), acute kidney injury, placental abruption, death.

**Fetal/neonatal**: IUGR, oligohydramnios, prematurity (iatrogenic delivery), placental abruption, stillbirth, neonatal hypomagnesaemia (if delivery occurs near cessation of Mg infusion — neonates can't excrete Mg efficiently).

**Long-term maternal risks**: Women who have had pre-eclampsia have a 3–4× increased lifetime risk of hypertension, 2× risk of ischaemic heart disease, and 2× risk of stroke. Pre-eclampsia is a window into future cardiovascular health — follow up with GP for cardiovascular risk assessment.

## Clinical Insight

The woman in this scenario has **severe pre-eclampsia**: BP 158/106, proteinuria 2+, headache, visual disturbance, brisk reflexes, and clonus. The clonus (3 beats) signals significant neurological irritability — this is a woman who is at risk of eclampsia in the next hours, not days.

Management priorities in order:
1. Urgent IV access, bloods (FBC, U&E, LFTs, LDH, coagulation), urine PCR
2. Antihypertensive therapy — IV labetalol to bring dBP below 110 mmHg
3. Magnesium sulphate (Zuspan) for seizure prophylaxis — do not wait for a seizure
4. Senior obstetrician involvement
5. CTG for fetal surveillance
6. Counselling about delivery — at 34 weeks, corticosteroids first (betamethasone), then delivery planning

Common mistake: giving diazepam for seizure prophylaxis or treating eclamptic seizures before magnesium. The Magpie trial settled this — magnesium is superior.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**Working through the case**: Primigravida, 34 weeks, booking BP 112/70 (normal), presenting with headache, photopsia, facial and hand oedema. BP now 158/106 on two readings. Proteinuria 2+. Brisk reflexes with 3 beats of clonus.

This is a textbook presentation of **severe pre-eclampsia**. The new hypertension (after 20 weeks, in a previously normotensive woman) + significant proteinuria meets the diagnostic criteria. The neurological features (headache, visual disturbance, clonus) confirm severity.

Step-by-step:
- Admit immediately to a consultant-led unit
- Bloods: FBC (platelets!), U&E, LFTs, LDH, coagulation, G&S
- IV labetalol for acute BP control — aim dBP 80–100 mmHg
- Magnesium sulphate (Zuspan): 4 g loading → 1 g/hour infusion; monitor reflexes and urine output hourly
- Betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2 doses (24 hours apart) for fetal lung maturity at 34 weeks
- Continuous CTG, USS for fetal growth and Doppler assessment
- Plan delivery after 48 hours (once steroids complete) unless maternal or fetal deterioration demands earlier delivery

**Exam trigger:** The classic presentation is a primigravida with new-onset hypertension after 20 weeks, proteinuria, headache, and visual disturbance — the answer is pre-eclampsia requiring magnesium sulphate prophylaxis. The most common distractor is using diazepam for seizure prophylaxis or treatment instead of magnesium, or forgetting to check patellar reflexes during the infusion (loss of reflexes is your earliest warning of Mg toxicity before respiratory depression).
