## Overview

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is the most common metabolic disease of childhood, affecting approximately 1 in 300 children under 20 in the United Kingdom and United States. It results from autoimmune destruction of insulin-producing beta cells in the pancreatic islets of Langerhans, leading to absolute insulin deficiency and a lifelong requirement for exogenous insulin. The acute presentation — often diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) — can be rapidly fatal without prompt recognition and carefully titrated management. The danger is not only hyperosmolarity and acidosis, but the specific complication of **cerebral oedema**, which occurs in 0.5–1% of paediatric DKA and carries a mortality of 20–25%. Cerebral oedema is largely an iatrogenic complication of over-rapid rehydration and dramatic osmolarity shifts — the management protocol is specifically designed to prevent it.

## Pathophysiology

### Autoimmune Beta-Cell Destruction

T1DM is an organ-specific autoimmune condition. Autoreactive CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, activated by molecular mimicry (viral antigens that share epitopes with beta-cell proteins) or other triggers, infiltrate the pancreatic islets (insulitis) → progressive beta-cell destruction over months to years. **Autoantibodies** (anti-GAD65, anti-IA-2, anti-insulin, anti-ZnT8) appear in the serum before clinical onset and are markers of this autoimmune process.

By the time symptoms appear, approximately **80–90% of beta cells have been destroyed**. The remaining 10–20% ("honeymoon phase") may partially restore insulin secretion for weeks to months after diagnosis, leading to reduced insulin requirements before the final destruction completes and lifelong insulin deficiency is established.

### Diabetic Ketoacidosis — Metabolic Cascade

DKA is the metabolic consequence of absolute or near-absolute insulin deficiency:
1. **No insulin** → cells cannot take up glucose → hyperglycaemia + cellular starvation
2. **Glucagon/cortisol/adrenaline surges** (counter-regulatory hormones) → gluconeogenesis + glycogenolysis → worsening hyperglycaemia
3. **Adipose tissue lipolysis** (unopposed counter-regulatory hormones) → free fatty acids → hepatic beta-oxidation → **ketone body production** (acetoacetate, beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetone — "pear drop" smell)
4. **Ketonaemia + ketonuria** → metabolic acidosis (anion gap: Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻) = elevated by ketoacid accumulation)
5. **Osmotic diuresis** from glycosuria → dehydration → electrolyte loss (K⁺, Na⁺, phosphate)
6. **Compensatory hyperventilation** (Kussmaul breathing) → reduction in PaCO₂ → partially compensates metabolic acidosis

### Cerebral Oedema in Paediatric DKA

The mechanism remains debated:
- **Rapid osmolarity reduction hypothesis:** Aggressive fluid administration rapidly reduces serum glucose and osmolarity → water moves down the osmotic gradient into cerebral cells → cytotoxic oedema. However, many cases of cerebral oedema occur before treatment begins, suggesting the mechanism is more complex.
- **Vasogenic oedema:** Cerebral hypoperfusion during DKA → blood-brain barrier disruption → vasogenic oedema with fluid treatment
- **RAAS activation + AQP4:** Vasopressin (activated by dehydration) + AQP4 water channels may facilitate cerebral oedema onset

**Critically:** The prevention of cerebral oedema requires avoiding rapid osmolarity shifts — this is why BSPED (British Society for Paediatric Endocrinology and Diabetes) guidelines mandate slow fluid replacement and slow glucose reduction.

## Clinical Presentation

### New-Onset T1DM — The 3 Ps

**Polydipsia** (excessive thirst), **polyuria** (frequent urination, including nocturia/enuresis), **polyphagia** (increased appetite, often paradoxically with weight loss). Also: fatigue, blurred vision (lens shape changes from osmotic shifts), recurrent infections (particularly candida — glucose is a bacterial/fungal growth medium), unexplained weight loss.

**Children with DKA at presentation (30–40% of new T1DM in UK):** Nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain (mimics acute abdomen), Kussmaul breathing (deep, sighing respirations — compensatory hyperventilation for metabolic acidosis), ketotic breath (acetone — "pear drops"), altered consciousness (from cerebral hypoperfusion or incipient cerebral oedema), haemodynamic compromise.

### DKA Severity (BSPED/ISPAD)

| Severity | pH | Bicarbonate |
|---------|-----|------------|
| Mild | 7.2–7.29 | 10–15 mEq/L |
| Moderate | 7.1–7.19 | 5–10 mEq/L |
| Severe | <7.1 | <5 mEq/L |

**This patient:** pH 7.09, HCO₃ 7 mEq/L = **severe DKA**.

Additional severity markers: Kussmaul breathing, altered consciousness, dehydration (CRT 3s), haemodynamic compromise (HR 124, BP 98/62), high blood ketones (5.8 mmol/L).

### Hyperosmolar Hyperglycaemic State (HHS)

Rare in children but increasingly recognised in obese adolescents (often with new-onset T2DM). Extreme hyperglycaemia (glucose >600 mg/dL) without significant ketosis or acidosis (insulin levels just sufficient to suppress ketogenesis but not glucose disposal). Profound dehydration + hyperosmolarity. High thrombosis risk. Rehydrate even more slowly than DKA (48h) due to extreme hyperosmolarity.

## Diagnosis

**Blood glucose:** Capillary glucose (converted: mg/dL = mmol/L × 18). DKA definition: glucose >200 mg/dL (>11 mmol/L).

**Blood ketones:** Point-of-care capillary beta-hydroxybutyrate; >3 mmol/L = significant ketonaemia (DKA confirmed). Urine ketones (dipstick): less accurate, less quantifiable.

**Blood gas:** pH + bicarbonate + PaCO₂ (confirm degree of acidosis and respiratory compensation). Venous blood gas is sufficient — no need for arterial unless respiratory failure suspected.

**Electrolytes:** Sodium (corrected sodium = measured Na + 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100 mg/dL — because hyperglycaemia dilutes Na by osmotic shift of water out of cells); potassium (may appear normal or high despite total body K⁺ depletion — insulin moves K⁺ into cells; as insulin is given, K⁺ will fall rapidly and hypokalaemia is common); chloride; bicarbonate.

**Anion gap:** Na⁺ − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻); normal <12 mEq/L; elevated in DKA from ketoacid accumulation. (Normal AG metabolic acidosis = hyperchloraemic = not DKA — check for other causes.)

**HbA1c:** At diagnosis — reflects glycaemic control over prior 3 months; very high in new T1DM (typically 10–15%); useful baseline.

**Autoantibodies:** Anti-GAD65, anti-IA-2, anti-insulin, anti-ZnT8 — confirm autoimmune T1DM; useful for type classification in atypical presentations.

**FBC, CRP, blood culture:** Sepsis can precipitate DKA; leucocytosis is common even in uncomplicated DKA (stress demargination) — use CRP/clinical picture to distinguish infective from non-infective leucocytosis.

## Management

### DKA — BSPED Protocol (2021)

**Phase 1 — Resuscitation (bolus fluid for shock only):**
- If **haemodynamically compromised (BP low, CRT >3s, prolonged capillary refill, signs of shock):** 10 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl over 15–30 minutes; may repeat up to 20 mL/kg if persistent shock
- **This is NOT the rehydration fluid** — it is emergency resuscitation only. Do NOT give large volumes up front.
- **If haemodynamically stable (no shock signs):** Start rehydration fluid directly, no bolus required

**Phase 2 — Rehydration (48-hour deficit replacement):**

**Deficit calculation:**
- Estimate dehydration: mild DKA = 5%; moderate = 7%; severe = 10%
- **This patient: severe DKA → 10% dehydration × 38 kg = 3800 mL deficit**
- Add **maintenance** fluid (weight-based, 24-hourly): for 38 kg → approximately 1500 + (20 × 38–20) = 1500 + 360 = 1860 mL/24h (Holiday-Segar simplified)
- Total fluids over 48h: (3800 mL deficit + 2 × maintenance) ÷ 48h = hourly rate
- **MAXIMUM total fluid in severe DKA = 10 mL/kg/h** — never exceed this rate (reduces cerebral oedema risk)
- **Fluid type:** 0.9% NaCl initially; switch to 0.45% NaCl + 5% dextrose when blood glucose falls to ≤250 mg/dL (prevents hypoglycaemia while maintaining osmolarity)
- Add KCl **immediately** to all DKA fluids (not bolus phase): 40 mEq/L if K⁺ normal/low; 20 mEq/L if K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L. Do not give insulin without potassium replacement in place (insulin shifts K⁺ into cells → rapid hypokalaemia → cardiac arrhythmia).

**Phase 3 — Insulin:**
- **Wait 1–2 hours after starting IV fluids before starting insulin** (fluid alone begins resolving acidosis and reducing ketosis; early insulin causes rapid K⁺ shift → dangerous hypokalaemia)
- **IV insulin infusion:** 0.05–0.1 units/kg/h (start low end in young children/severe acidosis)
- **Do NOT give IV insulin bolus** in children with DKA — rapid glucose fall → rapid osmolarity shift → cerebral oedema risk
- **Target glucose reduction:** 90 mg/dL (5 mmol/L) per hour — NOT faster

**Phase 4 — Monitoring:**
- Hourly blood glucose + ketones
- 2-hourly blood gas (pH, bicarbonate)
- 2-hourly electrolytes (especially K⁺)
- Continuous cardiac monitoring (K⁺ abnormalities → arrhythmia)
- Neurological observations **hourly** (headache, confusion, bradycardia, Cushing's triad = cerebral oedema)

**Resolution of DKA:**
- pH >7.3 AND bicarbonate >15 mEq/L AND ketones <1 mmol/L AND glucose <250 mg/dL AND clinically well
- Transition to SC insulin regimen when child is eating and drinking

### Cerebral Oedema — Recognition and Emergency Treatment

**Warning signs (may precede herniation by minutes to hours):**
- New headache or worsening headache during DKA treatment
- Altered behaviour, confusion, agitation
- Falling HR or rising BP (Cushing's triad: hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing = raised ICP, late sign)
- Declining GCS, abnormal eye movements, papilloedema (late)

**Immediate management:**
1. **Mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg IV** (20% solution, 2.5–5 mL/kg) over 20 minutes — osmotic agent, draws water out of cerebral cells; dramatic and immediate response if given early. OR
2. **3% hypertonic saline 2.5–5 mL/kg over 15 minutes** (alternative/adjunct) — raises serum sodium, drawing water out of brain cells
3. Reduce IV fluid rate by 30% (less osmolarity driving force)
4. Elevate head of bed 30° (reduces ICP)
5. PICU — urgent CT brain (to confirm diagnosis and look for alternative causes)
6. **Do NOT intubate routinely** unless GCS deteriorating to <8 — intubation with positive pressure ventilation can worsen ICP; if intubation essential, target normal PaCO₂ (35–40 mmHg) — aggressive hyperventilation causes cerebral vasoconstriction

### Long-Term Insulin Therapy

**Multiple daily injection (MDI) regimen:**
- **Basal insulin** (once-daily glargine or twice-daily detemir): 40–50% of total daily dose; provides background insulin preventing ketosis
- **Bolus insulin** (rapid-acting lispro/aspart/glulisine): given before each meal; dosed to carbohydrate content (insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio, ICR)
- **Correction dose:** Extra rapid-acting insulin to correct hyperglycaemia above target

**Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII — insulin pump):** Delivers basal insulin continuously + bolus doses at mealtimes; can vary basal rates; preferred in young children and those with hypoglycaemia unawareness.

**Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM):** Real-time glucose sensing via interstitial fluid sensor (e.g., Libre, Dexadite G6/G7); reduces HbA1c and time-in-hypoglycaemia; standard of care in UK for all T1DM children (NHS England decision 2022).

**Glycaemic targets (NICE/ADA paediatric):**
- HbA1c: <48 mmol/mol (6.5%) — target; <53 mmol/mol (7.0%) acceptable without undue hypoglycaemia
- CGM: Time-in-range 70–180 mg/dL >70% of the time; time-in-hypoglycaemia (<70 mg/dL) <4%; time >250 mg/dL <25%

## Complications

- **Cerebral oedema (acute DKA complication):** 0.5–1% of DKA; mortality 20–25%; survivors 10–30% have permanent neurological deficit; risk factors: young age, new-onset T1DM, low pCO₂ (severe acidosis), over-rapid fluid replacement
- **Hypoglycaemia:** Most feared chronic complication; impairs counter-regulatory response over time → hypoglycaemia unawareness; seizures, LOC, brain injury
- **DKA recurrence:** Often from insulin omission (adolescents) or illness; every episode causes cerebral microinjury
- **Microvascular complications (long-term, tight glycaemic control reduces risk):** Retinopathy, nephropathy (microalbuminuria → CKD → ESRD), peripheral neuropathy
- **Macrovascular complications (cardiovascular disease):** T1DM carries 10× increased cardiovascular risk; primary prevention: HbA1c control, lipid management (statin from age 18 if LDL >100 mg/dL), BP control
- **Psychological:** Depression, anxiety, disordered eating (insulin omission for weight loss — "diabulimia") are common and poorly recognised; quality of life significant issue in adolescent T1DM

## Clinical Insight

The most dangerous moment in DKA management is the temptation to rehydrate rapidly. A severely dehydrated, haemodynamically compromised child in DKA appears to need aggressive fluids urgently — and a clinician experienced in adult medicine or non-DKA paediatrics may instinctively push 20 mL/kg boluses. But the evidence is clear (the PECARN DKA fluid trial confirmed: rapid vs. slow vs. isotonic vs. hypotonic — slower is better for neurological outcomes in paediatric DKA). Every 10 mL/kg bolus beyond haemodynamic resuscitation increases cerebral oedema risk. The correct approach is: one resuscitation bolus if shocked, then calculate a slow 48-hour replacement and stick to it.

The potassium in DKA is a common exam trap. The serum K⁺ may appear normal or elevated at presentation because acidosis drives K⁺ out of cells into the extracellular space (H⁺ in, K⁺ out exchange). The total body K⁺ is, however, severely depleted from osmotic diuresis. When insulin is started, K⁺ re-enters cells rapidly → serum K⁺ plummets → potentially fatal hypokalaemia and arrhythmia. The rule: always add KCl to the IV fluid before starting insulin (unless K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L, in which case start with lower KCl concentration and recheck in 30 minutes). Never start insulin without having potassium replacement running.

T2DM in children is increasing as a consequence of the childhood obesity epidemic. It presents later in adolescence, is more common in certain ethnic groups, and may initially respond to metformin alone. The distinction from T1DM matters because the treatment is different and the risk of DKA is much lower — but obese adolescents can have T1DM, and thin adolescents can have T2DM. Autoantibodies and C-peptide (if low = T1DM, if detectable = T2DM) help classify when the phenotype is unclear.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 11-year-old, 3-day history of polyuria/polydipsia/weight loss + vomiting. Kussmaul breathing, pear-drop breath, lethargy. HR 124, BP 98/62, CRT 3s. Glucose 583 mg/dL (32.4 mmol/L), pH 7.09, HCO₃ 7 mEq/L. Ketones 5.8 mmol/L.

**Diagnosis: Severe DKA at presentation of T1DM**

**Step 1 — Resuscitation (haemodynamic compromise present):**
- HR 124, BP 98/62, CRT 3s = compensated shock
- **10 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl** = 380 mL over 15–30 minutes
- Reassess: if CRT remains ≥3s → repeat up to 20 mL/kg total (subtract bolus fluid from 48h calculation)

**Step 2 — Calculate 48-hour rehydration:**
- Deficit: 10% × 38 kg = 3800 mL
- Maintenance (48h): approximately 3720 mL
- Total: 7520 mL over 48h minus bolus given (380 mL) = 7140 mL ÷ 48h ≈ 149 mL/h
- Fluid: 0.9% NaCl + KCl 40 mEq/L (switch to 0.45% NaCl + 5% dextrose + KCl when glucose ≤250 mg/dL)

**Step 3 — Potassium replacement:**
- Check K⁺ from lab: if ≤5.5 mEq/L → add KCl 40 mEq/L to all fluids immediately
- If K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L → add 20 mEq/L and recheck in 1h

**Step 4 — Start insulin at 1–2 hours:**
- IV insulin infusion: 0.05 units/kg/h = 1.9 units/h (start 1h after fluids + potassium running)
- Target glucose reduction: ≤90 mg/dL/hour (≤5 mmol/L/hour)

**Step 5 — Neurological monitoring:**
- Hourly GCS/conscious level
- If new headache/confusion/bradycardia → cerebral oedema → **mannitol 1 g/kg IV immediately** + reduce fluid rate + PICU

**Step 6 — DKA resolution and transition to SC insulin:**
- pH >7.3, HCO₃ >15, ketones <1 mmol/L = biochemical resolution
- Start SC rapid-acting insulin with first meal → run SC and IV overlap for 1h → discontinue IV
- Educate family on T1DM management, glucose monitoring, insulin administration, sick day rules

**Exam trigger:** *Child + polyuria/polydipsia + Kussmaul breathing + pear-drop breath + blood glucose 583 mg/dL + pH 7.09* → severe DKA. Slow, calculated 48-hour rehydration + KCl replacement + delayed insulin. The distractor is rapid rehydration with large crystalloid boluses → cerebral oedema risk.
