## Overview

The thyroid gland is the most common site of endocrine malignancy, and thyroid nodules are found in up to 50% of the population on ultrasound. The vast majority are benign — the challenge is identifying the small proportion (approximately 5–15% of biopsied nodules) that are malignant. Thyroid cancer has an excellent overall prognosis — 10-year survival exceeds 95% for well-differentiated (papillary and follicular) cancers — yet treatment decisions must account for the consequences of surgery: recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury causing permanent hoarseness, and hypoparathyroidism from inadvertent parathyroid removal or devascularisation.

Parathyroid disorders — primarily primary hyperparathyroidism — are the most common cause of hypercalcaemia in the outpatient setting and frequently require surgical management with parathyroidectomy.

## Pathophysiology

### Thyroid Cancer — Classification

**Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC):** ~80% of all thyroid cancers. Derived from follicular cells. Most common in young women. Slow-growing; excellent prognosis. Characteristic histological features: **Orphan Annie eye nuclei** (empty-looking, ground-glass nuclei with nuclear inclusions), **Psammoma bodies** (calcified laminar concentric spherules — seen on cytology and histology). Spread: lymphatic (to cervical nodes), rarely haematogenous. Molecular drivers: **BRAF V600E** mutation (most common, ~60%), RET/PTC rearrangements.

**Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC):** ~10%. Derived from follicular cells; encapsulated. **FNA cannot distinguish follicular adenoma from follicular carcinoma** — diagnosis requires surgical excision to demonstrate capsular or vascular invasion. Spread: **haematogenous** (to bone and lung) rather than lymphatic. Molecular drivers: PAX8-PPARγ rearrangement, RAS mutations.

**Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC):** ~5%. Derived from **parafollicular C cells** (calcitonin-secreting). Sporadic (75%) or hereditary (25% — MEN2A, MEN2B, FMTC). **Calcitonin** is both the biomarker and functional product. **RET proto-oncogene mutation** in all hereditary forms — screen all patients with MTC for RET mutation; family members screened and offered prophylactic thyroidectomy if RET-positive. MEN2A: MTC + phaeochromocytoma + primary hyperparathyroidism. MEN2B: MTC + phaeochromocytoma + marfanoid habitus + mucosal neuromas.

**Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC):** <2%. Rapidly lethal — median survival 3–5 months. Dedifferentiated from pre-existing well-differentiated cancer. Presents as rapidly enlarging neck mass with invasion of adjacent structures (trachea, oesophagus). BRAF V600E mutation in ~50% → dabrafenib + trametinib (targeted therapy) provides modest palliation. Surgery only for tracheal decompression.

**Lymphoma:** Primary thyroid lymphoma — associated with Hashimoto's thyroiditis; treated with chemotherapy/radiotherapy, not surgery.

### Thyroid Nodule Ultrasound — U classification (BTA/RCR 2023)

| U Grade | Description | Action |
|---------|-------------|--------|
| U1 | Normal | No action |
| U2 | Benign (cystic, iso/hyperechoic, regular margins) | No action |
| U3 | Indeterminate/equivocal | FNA |
| U4 | Suspicious (hypoechoic, irregular margins, absent halo) | FNA |
| **U5** | **Highly suspicious (hypoechoic, microcalcifications, irregular margins, taller-than-wide, intranodular vascularity)** | **FNA** |

### Parathyroid Physiology and Hyperparathyroidism

**Normal PTH physiology:** Four parathyroid glands (superior and inferior pairs) on the posterior surface of the thyroid lobes. PTH secreted in response to low ionised calcium → increases bone resorption (osteoclast activation) + increases renal tubular calcium reabsorption + increases renal phosphate excretion + increases renal 1-alpha-hydroxylation of vitamin D → indirect intestinal calcium absorption. Net effect: raises serum calcium.

**Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT):** Autonomous PTH secretion from:
- Solitary adenoma: **85%**
- Multiple adenomas or hyperplasia (4-gland): 15% (associated with MEN1 — MEN1 gene mutation; MEN2A)
- Parathyroid carcinoma: <1% (very high calcium + very high PTH)

**Secondary hyperparathyroidism:** Chronic hypocalcaemia (usually chronic kidney disease → reduced 1-alpha-hydroxylase → low vitamin D → low calcium → compensatory PTH elevation). PTH elevated but appropriate; calcium normal or low.

**Tertiary hyperparathyroidism:** Prolonged secondary hyperparathyroidism → parathyroid glands become autonomous → hypercalcaemia develops (glands no longer respond to calcium feedback); occurs in longstanding CKD/renal transplant patients.

## Clinical Presentation

### Thyroid Nodule — History and Examination

**Red flags for malignancy (high-risk history):**
- Age <20 or >70 years
- Male sex (thyroid nodule in a male carries higher malignancy risk)
- Rapid growth
- Hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion)
- Dysphagia or stridor (tracheal/oesophageal invasion)
- Cervical lymphadenopathy
- History of neck irradiation in childhood
- Family history of thyroid cancer, MEN2, FMTC
- History of Cowden syndrome (PTEN mutation — increased thyroid cancer risk)

**Examination:** Inspect nodule on swallowing (thyroid moves up with swallowing); assess for fixation (malignant invasion); palpate cervical nodes; check voice quality (RLN function).

### Primary Hyperparathyroidism — Symptoms

**"Bones, groans, stones, and psychic moans":**
- **Bones:** Osteitis fibrosa cystica (severe chronic PHPT — now rare in West), osteoporosis, subperiosteal bone resorption (radial aspect of middle phalanges on X-ray), "salt and pepper" skull
- **Groans:** Constipation, anorexia, nausea, peptic ulcer disease (PTH stimulates gastrin secretion → hypercalcaemia → increased acid secretion; association with Zollinger-Ellison syndrome in MEN1)
- **Stones:** Nephrolithiasis (calcium oxalate/phosphate stones in 15–20%) and nephrocalcinosis
- **Psychic moans:** Depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, fatigue, weakness

**Most PHPT in the UK is now asymptomatic** — diagnosed incidentally on routine biochemistry (hypercalcaemia noted on TFTs, U&E ordered for other reasons).

## Diagnosis

### Thyroid Nodule Workup

**TFT (TSH):** First test. Suppressed TSH → hyperfunctioning ("hot") nodule on isotope scan — hot nodules are almost never malignant. Normal/elevated TSH → proceed to ultrasound ± FNA.

**Ultrasound:** Characterise nodule (U1–U5), identify additional nodules, assess nodes.

**FNA (fine needle aspiration cytology) — Thy classification:**
| Thy Grade | Interpretation | Action |
|-----------|---------------|--------|
| Thy1 | Non-diagnostic (insufficient cells) | Repeat FNA |
| Thy2 | Non-neoplastic (benign) | Surveillance ± repeat USS |
| Thy3a | Atypia of undetermined significance | Repeat FNA |
| Thy3f | Follicular lesion (cannot exclude follicular neoplasm) | Diagnostic hemithyroidectomy |
| Thy4 | Suspicious of malignancy | Surgery |
| **Thy5** | **Malignant** | **Surgery** |

**Molecular testing:** For Thy3f/Thy4 — BRAF V600E, RAS, RET, PAX8-PPARγ testing improves diagnostic accuracy and may avoid unnecessary hemithyroidectomy.

**Vocal cord assessment:** Indirect or fibreoptic laryngoscopy before thyroidectomy — baseline documentation of vocal cord function; essential if malignancy suspected (RLN invasion assessment).

### Primary Hyperparathyroidism — Diagnosis

**Biochemistry:** Elevated corrected serum calcium + elevated or inappropriately normal PTH (in the context of hypercalcaemia, PTH should be suppressed — any detectable PTH with hypercalcaemia = inappropriate).

**24-hour urine calcium:** To exclude **familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)** — autosomal dominant, calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR) mutation; benign condition; calcium elevated but 24-h urine calcium is very low (<100 mg/day; fractional calcium excretion <0.01); does NOT require parathyroidectomy.

**Localisation before surgery:**
- **Sestamibi parathyroid scintigraphy (MIBI scan):** Radiolabelled tracer taken up by hyperfunctioning parathyroid adenoma; identifies ~75–85% of solitary adenomas
- **Neck ultrasound:** Identifies adenoma adjacent to thyroid; complements MIBI
- **4D-CT parathyroid:** Superior to MIBI for multiglandular disease and re-operative cases
- **PET-CT with 11C-methionine:** Used in re-operative cases where initial imaging negative

## Management

### Thyroid Surgery

**Total thyroidectomy:** Both thyroid lobes removed. Indications:
- Confirmed papillary or follicular cancer (allows radioiodine ablation; avoids residual disease)
- Bilateral multinodular goitre
- Large goitre with compression (dysphagia, stridor)
- Medullary thyroid carcinoma (always total thyroidectomy + central neck dissection)
- Graves' disease (when antithyroid drugs/radioiodine not suitable)

**Hemithyroidectomy (lobectomy + isthmusectomy):** One lobe removed. Indications:
- Thy3f (follicular lesion — completion thyroidectomy if invasive cancer found on histology)
- Unilateral benign nodule causing compressive symptoms
- Low-risk unifocal PTC <4 cm with no nodal disease (active surveillance or hemithyroidectomy in selected cases)

**Central neck dissection (level VI):** Removes pre-tracheal and paratracheal nodes. Performed with thyroidectomy for confirmed papillary cancer (prophylactic or therapeutic if nodes palpable/visible on imaging). Lateral neck dissection (levels II–V) if lateral nodal disease confirmed.

**Radioiodine (I-131) ablation:** After total thyroidectomy for higher-risk PTC/FTC — ablates residual thyroid tissue, enables whole-body scan to detect metastases, enables thyroglobulin as tumour marker. Not appropriate for low-risk microcarcinomas.

**TSH suppression therapy:** Levothyroxine dose titrated to suppress TSH to <0.1 mIU/L in high-risk thyroid cancer (prevents TSH-driven tumour growth); less suppression for low-risk disease.

**Targeted therapy:**
- BRAF V600E+ advanced/metastatic PTC: **dabrafenib + trametinib** (BRAF + MEK inhibitor)
- RET mutation+ MTC or PTC: **selpercatinib** or **cabozantinib**
- Lenvatinib/sorafenib: multi-kinase inhibitors for radioiodine-refractory differentiated thyroid cancer

### Parathyroid Surgery

**Indications for parathyroidectomy (PHPT):**
- Symptomatic disease (nephrolithiasis, osteoporosis with fragility fracture, symptoms)
- **Corrected calcium >0.25 mmol/L above upper limit of normal (approx. >2.85 mmol/L)**
- Age <50 years (regardless of symptoms)
- DEXA T-score < –2.5 at any site
- eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73m²
- 24h urine calcium >400 mg/day

**Focused minimally invasive parathyroidectomy:** For single adenoma localised on MIBI + ultrasound — single gland removal under local/general anaesthesia; intraoperative PTH monitoring (rapid PTH assay — PTH should fall >50% within 10 minutes of adenoma removal confirming cure: "Miami criterion"). Minimally invasive approach avoids bilateral neck exploration.

**Bilateral exploration:** If imaging non-concordant or multiglandular disease suspected; examine all 4 glands; remove all hyperplastic glands; leave half a gland in most favourable position (or autotransplant to forearm).

## Complications

### Thyroid Surgery Complications

**Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) injury:**
- The RLN runs in the tracheo-oesophageal groove and must be identified and preserved during thyroidectomy
- Unilateral injury: **hoarse voice** (dysphonia) — unilateral cord palsy; often temporary (neuropraxia), may be permanent
- Bilateral injury: **bilateral cord palsy → stridor → respiratory compromise → emergency tracheostomy**
- Incidence: temporary palsy ~5–10%; permanent palsy ~1%
- **Intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM):** Electromyography of the RLN during dissection — reduces permanent palsy rate; standard of care in major centres

**Hypoparathyroidism:**
- Inadvertent removal or devascularisation of parathyroid glands → hypocalcaemia
- **Symptoms of hypocalcaemia:** Perioral numbness, tingling in fingers/toes, Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm on BP cuff inflation), Chvostek's sign (facial muscle twitch on tapping facial nerve)
- Temporary (most common — resolves as glands recover) vs permanent (<1% with experienced surgeon)
- Treatment: calcium carbonate + calcitriol (1-alpha-hydroxycholecalciferol); IV calcium gluconate for severe symptomatic hypocalcaemia

**Haemorrhage:** Post-thyroidectomy haematoma — rapidly expanding neck haematoma → tracheal compression → airway obstruction. **Emergency — open wound at bedside to decompress, then return to theatre.** Occurs in first 6–24h.

**Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis):** Post-operative in incompletely prepared Graves' patients → fever, tachycardia, hyperthermia, altered consciousness. Treatment: propylthiouracil + iodine (Lugol's) + beta-blocker + dexamethasone + cooling.

## Clinical Insight

The most dreaded complication of thyroid surgery — bilateral RLN injury causing respiratory distress — is preventable with meticulous surgical technique and, increasingly, intraoperative nerve monitoring. But the critical pre-operative step is **baseline vocal cord assessment**. A patient with pre-existing unilateral vocal cord palsy from RLN invasion by thyroid cancer must be identified before surgery — because if the contralateral nerve is damaged during surgery, the result is bilateral palsy and an emergency airway. Laryngoscopy is mandatory before thyroidectomy for malignancy, and many centres perform it for all patients.

Hypoparathyroidism after total thyroidectomy can be profoundly debilitating and is the most common long-term complication after thyroid surgery in experienced centres. The parathyroid glands are tiny (3–4 mm, 30–40 mg each), difficult to distinguish from fatty tissue or lymph nodes intraoperatively, and highly dependent on their blood supply from the inferior thyroid artery. A technically excellent thyroidectomy preserves parathyroid glands with their blood supply intact — which is why surgeon volume (number of operations per year) is the strongest predictor of parathyroid complication rates.

The management of differentiated thyroid cancer has undergone a significant paradigm shift toward de-escalation. For low-risk papillary microcarcinomas (≤1 cm, no high-risk features), **active surveillance** (monitoring without immediate surgery) is now guideline-endorsed — studies from Japan show that 95% of microcarcinomas do not grow over 10 years. This is important because even "excellent" surgery carries 1–5% complication rates, and a tiny cancer with essentially no mortality risk does not necessarily warrant those risks.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 38F, 2.4 cm hypoechoic solid nodule, microcalcifications, irregular margins (U5), Thy5 (malignant), TSH 1.2 mIU/L normal. Normal voice. No family history of thyroid cancer.

**Diagnosis: Papillary thyroid carcinoma (most likely — U5 + microcalcifications + Thy5)**

**Step 1 — Pre-operative assessment:**
- **Vocal cord assessment:** Fibreoptic laryngoscopy — document baseline vocal cord function (essential before any thyroid cancer surgery)
- Neck ultrasound review: assess for cervical lymphadenopathy (levels II–VI); FNA of suspicious nodes
- Consider CT neck/chest if significant lymphadenopathy or local invasion suspected
- RET mutation testing (exclude hereditary MTC — though this is likely PTC based on FNA)
- Calcitonin level (to screen for co-existent MTC)

**Step 2 — Staging and MDT discussion:**
- Present at thyroid cancer MDT
- Staging: T2 (2–4 cm) N0 M0 = Stage I (age <55, well-differentiated)
- Low-intermediate risk PTC based on staging + features

**Step 3 — Surgery:**
- **Total thyroidectomy** (confirmed malignancy, >1 cm, allows post-operative radioiodine monitoring, enables thyroglobulin as tumour marker)
- **Prophylactic central neck dissection (level VI)** — for PTC >1 cm; removes nodes most likely to contain micrometastases
- Intraoperative RLN monitoring
- Parathyroid gland preservation (identify all 4 glands; preserve blood supply)

**Step 4 — Post-operative:**
- Monitor calcium and PTH at 6–12h post-op
- If calcium dropping: calcium carbonate + calcitriol prophylactically
- Levothyroxine started immediately (thyroid hormone replacement)
- Histology confirms PTC with clear margins

**Step 5 — Radioiodine (decision-making):**
- T2 N0 M0 intermediate-risk → radioiodine ablation likely recommended (BTA guidelines)
- TSH stimulation (stop levothyroxine or use recombinant TSH — Thyrogen) before radioiodine
- Whole-body scan + thyroglobulin post-ablation

**Step 6 — Long-term follow-up:**
- Thyroglobulin + anti-thyroglobulin antibodies every 6–12 months (tumour marker)
- Neck ultrasound at 6–12 months post-treatment
- TSH target: mildly suppressed (0.1–0.5 mIU/L) for intermediate-risk disease
- Prognosis: >98% disease-specific 10-year survival for Stage I PTC

**Exam trigger:** *Thy5 thyroid nodule* → total thyroidectomy ± central neck dissection; voice check pre-operatively; calcium monitoring post-operatively. Know the two major complications: RLN injury (hoarseness → emergency if bilateral) and hypoparathyroidism (tingling, Trousseau's, Chvostek's).
