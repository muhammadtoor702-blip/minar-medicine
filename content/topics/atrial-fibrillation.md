## Overview

Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, affecting 1–2% of the general population and rising to around 9% in people over 65. It is caused by chaotic, disorganised electrical activity in the atria — predominantly arising from ectopic foci at the pulmonary vein ostia — producing loss of coordinated atrial contraction and an irregularly irregular ventricular response.

The primary danger of AF is not the arrhythmia itself but its consequence: thrombus formation in the left atrial appendage (LAA) and subsequent cardioembolic stroke. AF increases stroke risk fivefold; anticoagulation with warfarin reduces this to approximately 1% per year from 4%.

**Classification — clinically important because it drives management:**

| Type | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| First detected | First diagnosed episode, regardless of symptoms or duration |
| Paroxysmal | Self-terminating, usually within 48 hours; recurrent episodes |
| Persistent | Duration >7 days, or requiring cardioversion to terminate |
| Long-standing persistent | Continuous AF lasting >12 months |
| Permanent | Decision made by clinician and patient not to pursue rhythm control; rate control accepted |

**"Lone" AF** — AF with no identifiable underlying cause on thorough investigation.

## Pathophysiology

Ectopic foci at the pulmonary vein ostia fire at 300–600 impulses per minute, overwhelming the sinus node. The AV node acts as a gatekeeper, allowing only a fraction through — producing the characteristic irregularly irregular ventricular response. Cardiac output drops by 10–20% because the atria no longer prime the ventricles reliably before systole.

Three major pathophysiological consequences:

1. **Loss of atrial kick** — atria contribute 20–30% of ventricular filling at rest, more in patients with impaired relaxation (hypertension, LVH, diastolic dysfunction). Losing this contribution can precipitate acute pulmonary oedema in those with mitral stenosis or HFpEF.

2. **Left atrial appendage stasis** — sluggish, chaotic flow in the LAA promotes thrombus formation. This is the source of the vast majority of AF-related strokes. If AF has been present for >48 hours, intracardiac thrombus may have formed and cardioversion — whether electrical or chemical — risks systemic embolism.

3. **Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy** — uncontrolled ventricular rate over weeks to months causes reversible LV systolic dysfunction. Critically, this can normalise with effective rate or rhythm control — making it essential to consider in any patient with both AF and reduced EF.

**Causes and associated conditions:**
- Hypertensive heart disease (most common)
- Ischaemic heart disease (occurs in 22% of MI patients acutely)
- Heart failure (AF and HF mutually perpetuate each other)
- Valvular disease — mitral stenosis particularly (LA enlargement → trigger)
- Hyperthyroidism — always check TFTs in new-onset AF
- Acute illness: pneumonia, PE, sepsis (stress-related catecholamine surge)
- Alcohol — acute binge-drinking can trigger AF ("holiday heart syndrome")
- Obstructive sleep apnoea
- Post-cardiac surgery — AF occurs in up to 30% after cardiac surgery; usually self-terminating

## Clinical Presentation

AF may be completely asymptomatic and discovered incidentally on pulse palpation or routine ECG. When symptomatic: palpitations (described as an irregular, "jumpy" heartbeat), breathlessness, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, chest discomfort, presyncope.

**On examination:** Irregularly irregular pulse; the apical pulse rate is greater than the radial rate (pulse deficit) because some beats are too weak to reach the radial artery; first heart sound of variable intensity (mitral valve closes at different positions in each cycle depending on filling time).

Examine the whole patient: AF is often the presenting feature of an underlying illness — find it.

## Diagnosis

**ECG is diagnostic:** Absence of P waves (or an irregular fibrillatory baseline at ~350–600 per minute), irregularly irregular RR intervals, and typically narrow QRS complexes (unless aberrant conduction, bundle branch block, or pre-excitation via accessory pathway).

**Differential for irregular pulse:**
- Frequent ectopics — P waves visible; discrete premature beats interspersed with sinus rhythm
- Atrial flutter with variable block — sawtooth flutter waves at ~300 bpm; ventricular rate regularly irregular (150, 100, or 75 bpm)
- Multifocal atrial tachycardia — ≥3 distinct P wave morphologies; associated with COPD

**Mandatory investigations in new AF:**
- TFTs — mandatory; hyperthyroidism is a common, reversible, and treatable cause
- U&E (including Mg²⁺) — hypokalaemia and hypomagnesaemia predispose to AF and arrhythmias during cardioversion
- FBC, LFTs, troponin if ischaemia clinically suspected
- Echocardiogram — assess LA size (enlarged LA predicts AF recurrence), LV function (tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy?), valvular disease (mitral stenosis?)
- Transoesophageal echo (TOE) — to exclude LAA thrombus before cardioversion if AF duration >48 hours or unknown

**Stroke risk — CHA₂DS₂-VASc score:**

| Letter | Risk Factor | Points |
|--------|------------|--------|
| C | Congestive heart failure or LV dysfunction | 1 |
| H | Hypertension | 1 |
| A₂ | Age ≥75 | 2 |
| D | Diabetes mellitus | 1 |
| S₂ | Prior Stroke, TIA, or thromboembolism | 2 |
| V | Vascular disease (MI, PAD, aortic plaque) | 1 |
| A | Age 65–74 | 1 |
| Sc | Sex category (female) | 1 |

- Men with score ≥2, women with score ≥3: anticoagulate (annual stroke risk ~2.2% or higher)
- Men with score 1, women with score 2: consider anticoagulation; shared decision-making
- Men with score 0, women with score 1: anticoagulation generally not recommended

**Bleeding risk — HAS-BLED score:** 1 point each for uncontrolled Hypertension, Abnormal renal/liver function, Stroke history, Bleeding history or predisposition, Labile INR, Elderly (>65), Drugs/alcohol (antiplatelets, NSAIDs, alcohol abuse). Score ≥3 = high bleeding risk. This should prompt review of correctable risk factors (e.g., uncontrolled hypertension, NSAIDs), not refusal to anticoagulate — the stroke benefit outweighs bleeding risk in most patients.

## Management

### Haemodynamically Unstable

Immediate synchronised DC cardioversion (120–150 J biphasic), regardless of AF duration. Do not delay cardioversion to start anticoagulation in a compromised patient.

### Haemodynamically Stable — Two Decisions

**Decision 1: Rate Control vs Rhythm Control**

Rate control (target resting HR <110 bpm) is appropriate for older or less symptomatic patients, those with permanent AF, or those in whom cardioversion attempts have failed.

Rhythm control — restoring and maintaining sinus rhythm — is preferred in younger patients, those with significant symptoms, and those with recent-onset AF. The **EAST-AFNET 4 trial (2020)** showed that early rhythm control significantly reduces cardiovascular events (including stroke, HF hospitalisation, and CV death) compared with rate control alone — shifting practice toward rhythm control in most patients who are suitable.

**Rate control agents:**

| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| Bisoprolol | 2.5–10mg OD | First-line; avoid if decompensated HF or severe bronchospasm |
| Diltiazem | 120–360mg/day (modified release) | Preferred if bronchospasm; avoid if LVEF <40% |
| Verapamil | 120–360mg/day | Avoid with beta-blockers (risk of complete AV block) |
| Digoxin | 125–250 mcg OD | Only for sedentary patients; poor rate control on exertion; useful adjunct in HFrEF |
| Amiodarone | 200mg OD (maintenance) | Last resort for rate control; significant toxicity profile |

**Rhythm control — cardioversion:**
- **DC cardioversion** (electrical) — synchronised shock starting 120–150 J. Most effective method.
- **Chemical cardioversion** — flecainide 200–300mg PO stat (CI: structural heart disease, IHD, any LV dysfunction) or IV amiodarone (if structural disease or HF).
- **AF duration >48 hours or unknown:** must anticoagulate for ≥3 weeks before elective cardioversion + ≥4 weeks after. Alternative: TOE-guided cardioversion (image LAA to exclude thrombus, then cardiovert — allows earlier cardioversion in selected patients).
- If cardioversion is expected to fail (prior failures, structural disease), give amiodarone for 4 weeks before the procedure to improve success.

**Long-term rhythm control drugs:**
- Flecainide (50–150mg BD) — highly effective; use only if no structural disease or IHD
- Propafenone — similar to flecainide
- Amiodarone 200mg OD — most effective antiarrhythmic but extensive SE: thyroid dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, peripheral neuropathy, hepatotoxicity, corneal microdeposits, photosensitivity. Monitor TFTs and LFTs every 6 months.
- **Catheter ablation (pulmonary vein isolation)** — increasingly first-line for paroxysmal AF; superior to antiarrhythmic drugs for maintaining sinus rhythm. Success rate ~70–80% at 1 year; may need repeat procedure.

**Paroxysmal AF — "pill in the pocket":** Flecainide or propafenone taken PRN at onset of symptomatic episode, in patients with infrequent attacks and no structural disease. BP must be >100 mmHg systolic and no prior LV dysfunction.

**Decision 2: Anticoagulation**

DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) are preferred over warfarin in non-valvular AF — more predictable anticoagulation, fewer drug interactions, no routine monitoring required, lower rates of intracranial haemorrhage.

**Warfarin (INR 2–3) is mandatory in valvular AF:**
- Mechanical prosthetic heart valves
- Moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis
DOACs are **not validated** in these settings and are contraindicated.

**Pre-excited AF (WPW + AF):** The accessory pathway conducts AF impulses unfiltered by the AV node — ventricular rates can exceed 300 bpm, risking VF. ECG shows irregular, extremely wide bizarre complexes. **Absolutely avoid: adenosine, verapamil, diltiazem, digoxin** — all block the AV node and force all conduction through the accessory pathway, potentially triggering VF. Treatment: DC cardioversion or IV procainamide/flecainide. Long-term: catheter ablation of the accessory pathway.

## Complications

- **Cardioembolic stroke** — the most serious complication; 20× more disabling than other stroke types because of larger clot burden
- **Tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy** — reversible LV dysfunction from uncontrolled rate; complete recovery expected with adequate rate control
- **Heart failure** — both a cause and consequence of AF; the two conditions form a self-perpetuating cycle
- **Cardioversion without anticoagulation** — thrombus embolism; devastating and preventable

## Clinical Insight

Hyperthyroidism is identified in 10–15% of patients presenting with new AF, and rate control alone — without antithyroid treatment — will fail. Beta-blockers control both rate and symptoms while awaiting euthyroidism, after which AF often resolves spontaneously without needing rhythm control.

The risk of cardioversion in inadequately anticoagulated AF is real and irreversible. A thrombus that has been silently forming in the LAA for days can be dislodged by cardioversion and cause a devastating stroke. When the duration of AF is >48 hours or unknown, either anticoagulate for 3 weeks before cardioversion or perform TOE to exclude LAA thrombus first — there is no shortcut.

Correctable bleeding risks should prompt action, not refusal to anticoagulate. Uncontrolled hypertension, NSAIDs, and labile INR are modifiable. A patient with a high HAS-BLED score and high CHA₂DS₂-VASc score almost always benefits from anticoagulation after risk factors have been optimised — the alternative (untreated AF stroke) carries far higher mortality and morbidity than well-managed anticoagulation bleeding.
