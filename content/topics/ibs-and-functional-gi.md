## Overview

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is the most common functional gastrointestinal disorder, affecting approximately 10–20% of the population globally, with a 2:1 female predominance and typical onset before age 40. It is characterised by recurrent abdominal pain related to defecation, associated with a change in stool frequency or form — in the absence of structural or biochemical abnormalities to explain symptoms. IBS is **not a diagnosis of exclusion in younger patients without alarm features** — it is a positive clinical diagnosis based on symptom criteria and targeted investigation. Making this diagnosis confidently, and communicating it clearly, is itself therapeutic.

---

## Pathophysiology

IBS does not have a single unifying mechanism — it is best understood as a disorder of **gut-brain axis dysregulation**, in which multiple interacting factors lower the threshold for symptom perception:

**Visceral hypersensitivity**: Altered central processing of afferent gut signals — normal bowel contractions and luminal distension produce exaggerated pain responses. This explains why IBS patients report pain from colonic activities that would be imperceptible in healthy individuals. The mechanism involves sensitisation of nociceptors in the gut wall, altered spinal processing, and changes in descending pain modulation from the brain.

**Altered gut motility**: Accelerated transit in IBS-D (diarrhoea-predominant), delayed transit in IBS-C (constipation-predominant). In IBS-M (mixed), motility oscillates. Smooth muscle contractility is abnormal — disordered rather than simply "too fast" or "too slow."

**Post-infectious IBS**: Develops in ~10–25% of patients following acute gastroenteritis (*Campylobacter*, *Salmonella*, Norwalk virus). The mechanism involves sustained low-grade mucosal inflammation, increased submucosal mast cells and enterochromaffin cells (serotonin-producing), altered mucosal permeability, and changes in the enteric nervous system. This is IBS with a clear biological trigger — not simply psychological.

**Microbiome dysbiosis**: Altered gut microbiome composition is consistently found in IBS compared with controls. Whether this is causal or consequential is unclear, but it explains the symptom response seen with dietary interventions (low-FODMAP diet modulates substrate availability for bacterial fermentation).

**Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO)**: In a subset of IBS-D patients, colonic bacteria colonise the small intestine, fermenting carbohydrates and producing hydrogen/methane gas. Hydrogen breath testing can identify this subgroup; rifaximin produces significant symptom improvement in SIBO-driven IBS.

**Psychological factors**: Anxiety, depression, and psychosocial stress are bidirectionally linked to IBS through the gut-brain axis. The relationship is not one of "psychological causes IBS" — it is one of mutual amplification. Psychological distress lowers the pain threshold (central sensitisation), and ongoing bowel symptoms generate anxiety and impair quality of life (gut-to-brain signalling). Neither direction should be dismissed or overemphasised.

## Clinical Presentation

**Rome IV criteria for IBS** (internationally accepted diagnostic standard):

Recurrent abdominal pain on average ≥1 day/week over the last 3 months, associated with ≥2 of:
1. Related to defecation (onset or improvement with defecation)
2. Associated with a change in stool frequency
3. Associated with a change in stool form (appearance)

Symptoms must have been present for ≥6 months before diagnosis, with the above pattern established for the last 3 months.

**IBS subtypes** (Bristol Stool Form Scale — type 1–2 hard; 3–4 normal; 6–7 liquid):
- **IBS-C** (constipation-predominant): Bristol 1–2 >25% of bowel movements, Bristol 6–7 <25%
- **IBS-D** (diarrhoea-predominant): Bristol 6–7 >25%, Bristol 1–2 <25%
- **IBS-M** (mixed): Both Bristol 1–2 and 6–7 >25%
- **IBS-U** (unclassified)

**Associated features**: Bloating and visible abdominal distension (worse after meals; partially relieved by defecation or flatulence), mucus per rectum, incomplete evacuation. Extraintestinal features — all reflecting central sensitisation: fatigue, fibromyalgia, chronic pelvic pain, headache (especially migraine), urinary urgency and frequency, dyspareunia. The presence of multiple functional syndromes supports, rather than negates, IBS.

**Alarm features that mandate investigation regardless of IBS-pattern symptoms:**
- Age ≥50 at onset
- Rectal bleeding
- Unexplained weight loss
- Nocturnal symptoms (waking from sleep — organic disease; IBS does not wake patients at night)
- Family history of CRC or IBD
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Change in a previously stable symptom pattern
- Objective findings (mass, fever)

## Diagnosis

IBS is a **positive clinical diagnosis** based on Rome IV criteria in patients without alarm features. In younger patients (<50), appropriate targeted investigations exclude the key organic differentials; extensive investigation is neither needed nor helpful (and may reinforce health anxiety).

**Minimum investigations (NICE NG61):**
- FBC — anaemia (IBD, malignancy, coeliac)
- CRP — elevated in IBD, infection; normal in IBS
- Coeliac serology (anti-tTG IgA + total IgA) — coeliac affects 1% of the population and perfectly mimics IBS; cheap and highly sensitive; **patient must be on a gluten-containing diet for the test to be valid**
- Thyroid function — hypo/hyperthyroidism alters bowel habit
- **Faecal calprotectin** — highly sensitive for mucosal inflammation; a normal value (typically <50 µg/g) essentially excludes active IBD and strongly supports IBS in a patient with chronic GI symptoms

**Colonoscopy**: Only if alarm features, age >50 at onset, or ongoing diagnostic doubt. Routine colonoscopy is not required for a diagnosis of IBS in younger patients meeting Rome IV criteria without alarm features.

**Key organic conditions to exclude:**

| Condition | Why Mimics IBS | How to Differentiate |
|-----------|---------------|---------------------|
| **Coeliac disease** | Bloating, alternating stool habit, abdominal pain | Anti-tTG IgA (on gluten-containing diet); duodenal biopsy |
| **Inflammatory bowel disease** | Diarrhoea, cramping pain, urgency | Faecal calprotectin; CRP; colonoscopy |
| **Microscopic colitis** | Chronic watery diarrhoea, normal colonoscopy | Colonic biopsies (colonoscopy appears normal; biopsy is diagnostic) |
| **Bile acid malabsorption** | Chronic watery diarrhoea, especially post-cholecystectomy or ileal disease | SeHCAT scan (selenium-labelled taurocholic acid retention) |
| **Lactose/fructose intolerance** | Bloating, diarrhoea, gas after dairy/fruit | Hydrogen breath test; dietary exclusion trial |
| **SIBO** | Bloating, diarrhoea, gas | Hydrogen/methane breath test |
| **Ovarian cancer** | Bloating, abdominal pain, altered bowel habit | CA-125 + USS pelvis in women >50 with new-onset bloating |

## Microscopic Colitis

A condition worth knowing: colonoscopy appears completely normal (normal mucosal colour and vascular pattern), but **colonic biopsies show characteristic histological changes**:
- **Collagenous colitis**: Thickened subepithelial collagen band >10 µm
- **Lymphocytic colitis**: Increased intraepithelial lymphocytes >20 per 100 epithelial cells

**Presentation**: Chronic, profuse, watery non-bloody diarrhoea (10–15 stools/day); predominantly middle-aged to elderly women; often nocturnal (unlike IBS). Frequently misdiagnosed as IBS until biopsies are obtained.

**Risk factors**: NSAIDs (most important — proton pump inhibitors, SSRIs, and statins also implicated); stop the offending drug where possible. Autoimmune conditions (coeliac, thyroid disease) associated.

**Treatment**: Budesonide 9 mg/day for 8 weeks is the most effective induction treatment — >80% response rate. Taper and consider long-term maintenance in frequent relapsers. Cholestyramine for mild disease.

## Bile Acid Malabsorption

Approximately 25–30% of patients with a diagnosis of IBS-D have bile acid malabsorption (BAM) — unabsorbed bile acids in the colon stimulate water secretion and accelerate colonic transit, causing chronic watery diarrhoea.

**Causes**: Primary (idiopathic BAM — most common, mechanism unknown), post-cholecystectomy, terminal ileal disease or resection (Crohn's — the ileum is the site of active bile acid reabsorption), post-infectious, and radiation enteritis.

**Diagnosis**: **SeHCAT scan** — a radiolabelled synthetic bile acid (75Se-homocholic acid conjugated with taurine) is ingested; retention at 7 days measured by gamma scintigraphy. Retention <15% = BAM; <10% = severe BAM. Where SeHCAT is unavailable, empirical trial with cholestyramine or colesevelam is diagnostic.

**Treatment**: **Cholestyramine** (bile acid sequestrant, 4 g before meals) — highly effective, but poor palatability and interferes with absorption of other drugs (give 1 hour apart). **Colesevelam** (alternative sequestrant — better tolerated, fewer drug interactions). Response rates >70% — when effective, the diagnosis is confirmed.

## Gastroparesis

Delayed gastric emptying in the absence of mechanical obstruction. Post-prandial bloating, nausea, vomiting, early satiety, and upper abdominal pain.

**Causes**: Diabetes mellitus (~30% of cases — vagal and autonomic neuropathy impairs antral contractions and pyloric relaxation), idiopathic (~35%), post-surgical (post-vagotomy, post-oesophagectomy or -gastrectomy), post-viral (norovirus, rotavirus — usually transient), systemic sclerosis, hypothyroidism, medications (opioids, GLP-1 agonists, anticholinergics).

**Diagnosis**: Gastric emptying scintigraphy with a ⁹⁹technetium-labelled standardised meal (4-hour imaging) — gold standard. Wireless motility capsule is an alternative. Upper GI endoscopy to exclude mechanical obstruction (large food residue in the stomach 8+ hours after a meal suggests gastroparesis).

**Management**: Dietary modification — small, frequent, low-fat, low-fibre meals (fat and fibre delay gastric emptying). **Metoclopramide** (dopamine antagonist — prokinetic; limited by extrapyramidal side effects with long-term use) or **domperidone** (dopamine antagonist — less CNS penetration; caution with QTc). **Erythromycin** (motilin agonist — potent short-term prokinetic; limited by tachyphylaxis and antibiotic effects). NG/NJ feeding in severe cases to bypass the stomach.

## Management of IBS

Management requires a **biopsychosocial approach** — addressing physiological, psychological, and lifestyle components simultaneously. Symptom severity and patient health beliefs determine the intensity of intervention.

### Communication and Education

**The positive diagnosis** is itself therapeutic. Patients who have been told "we can't find anything" feel dismissed; those given a clear, confident diagnosis of IBS with an explanation of the mechanism feel validated and are more likely to engage with treatment. Explain the gut-brain axis model: the enteric nervous system is genuinely sensitised, the pain is real, but it is driven by abnormal signal processing rather than structural damage. Reassure explicitly that IBS does not progress to IBD or cancer. Address health anxiety compassionately and directly.

### Dietary Modification

**Low-FODMAP diet** (Fermentable Oligosaccharides, Disaccharides, Monosaccharides, and Polyols — poorly absorbed, rapidly fermented by colonic bacteria, causing gas, distension, and water shifts):

Randomised evidence shows symptom response in ~60–70% of IBS patients. Requires **dietitian supervision** to implement correctly:
1. **6-week elimination phase**: Avoid high-FODMAP foods (wheat/gluten, onions, garlic, lactose, fructose, polyols — stone fruits, artificial sweeteners)
2. **Reintroduction phase**: Systematic reintroduction of individual FODMAP categories to identify personal triggers
3. **Personalisation**: Long-term avoidance only of identified triggers — permanent strict low-FODMAP is unnecessary, nutritionally restrictive, and socially isolating

Patients who self-attempt low-FODMAP without dietitian guidance frequently skip the reintroduction phase and remain on a permanently restrictive diet — this is an avoidable harm.

**General advice**: Regular small meals, adequate fluid intake, reduce caffeine and carbonated drinks, limit alcohol, avoid known personal food triggers. Soluble fibre (ispaghula/psyllium) supplements may benefit IBS-C — avoid insoluble fibre (wheat bran), which can worsen bloating without improving constipation.

### Pharmacological Treatment

| Symptom | Treatment | Mechanism and Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| Abdominal pain/cramps | Antispasmodics: **mebeverine 135 mg TDS**, **hyoscine butylbromide 10 mg TDS**, peppermint oil capsules | Smooth muscle relaxants; take **20–30 minutes before meals** to blunt post-prandial spasm |
| IBS-C | **Ispaghula husk** (Fybogel), macrogol (Movicol), linaclotide 290 µg OD | Linaclotide is a guanylate cyclase-C agonist — increases intestinal fluid secretion AND reduces visceral pain; NICE-approved for moderate-severe IBS-C |
| IBS-D | **Loperamide 2 mg PRN** (before meals, or before planned activities) | Mu-opioid receptor agonist; slows colonic transit; does not cross BBB — no CNS effects |
| Global symptom improvement | **Amitriptyline 10–30 mg nocte** | Central pain modulation; slows bowel transit; improves sleep |
| Global symptoms with psychological comorbidity | **SSRIs** (low-dose fluoxetine or citalopram) | If TCA not tolerated; may worsen IBS-D through accelerated transit |
| IBS-D with suspected SIBO | Rifaximin 550 mg TDS for 14 days | Non-absorbable antibiotic reducing fermentation; repeat courses effective |
| IBS-C | Prucalopride 2 mg OD | 5-HT4 agonist — prokinetic; licensed for constipation; emerging evidence in IBS-C |

**Antidepressants in IBS are analgesics, not antidepressants**: Amitriptyline 10–30 mg is far below the antidepressant dose (75–150 mg). Its benefit derives from central pain modulation (descending inhibitory pathways) and delayed colonic transit. Making this distinction explicit to patients is critical for acceptance — "I am prescribing this to reduce gut pain sensitivity, not because I think this is depression."

### Psychological Therapies

For moderate-severe IBS, refractory IBS, or prominent psychological comorbidity — offered alongside, not instead of, other treatments:
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**: Strongest evidence base; addresses catastrophising, symptom amplification, and avoidance behaviours
- **Gut-directed hypnotherapy**: Highly effective in RCTs — reduces visceral hypersensitivity and improves both bowel symptoms and quality of life
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction**: Addresses the anxiety-pain amplification loop
- **Psychodynamic psychotherapy**: For patients with significant unresolved psychological contributors

NICE NG61 recommends psychological therapies if symptoms do not respond to pharmacotherapy after 12 months.

## Complications

IBS does not cause structural damage to the bowel and does not progress to IBD or colorectal cancer. The primary morbidity is functional:
- Impaired quality of life — social restriction (fear of urgency in public), inability to work, relationship strain, loss of intimacy
- Anxiety and depression — bidirectional relationship; each amplifies the other
- **Iatrogenic harm** — unnecessary investigations (radiation), inappropriate surgeries (appendicectomy for right iliac fossa pain, laparoscopy for chronic pelvic pain, hysterectomy for pain attributed to the uterus) — IBS is a leading cause of negative surgical exploration
- Economic burden: top cause of GP consultations and work absenteeism for chronic GI disease

## Clinical Insight

Coeliac disease is the most important organic condition to exclude in patients presenting with IBS-type symptoms. It affects ~1% of the population, causes identical symptoms to IBS, and requires a specific treatment (gluten-free diet) — not low-FODMAP or antispasmodics. Anti-tTG IgA serology is cheap, highly sensitive, and should be routine in every new IBS presentation. The patient must be consuming gluten at the time of testing — a false-negative result in a patient already trialling gluten avoidance is a common diagnostic pitfall.

Antispasmodics should be taken before meals, not reactively after symptoms start. The pain of IBS is driven by exaggerated colonic smooth muscle contractions in response to food-stimulated peristalsis — the spasm that follows eating is predictable. Taking mebeverine or peppermint oil 20 minutes before meals blunts this response. Taking it after symptoms have started treats a spasm that has already occurred.

Microscopic colitis mimics IBS-D perfectly — watery diarrhoea, normal colonoscopy, negative blood tests. The diagnosis requires colonic biopsies even from normal-appearing mucosa. Any patient with diarrhoea-predominant symptoms, especially a middle-aged woman on NSAIDs or SSRIs, in whom colonoscopy is "normal," requires biopsies. Treating microscopic colitis as IBS exposes the patient to continued symptoms and the ongoing offending drug — budesonide produces rapid and dramatic improvement.
