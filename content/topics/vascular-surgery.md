## Overview

Vascular surgery encompasses diseases of the arterial and venous systems outside the heart. The common thread is atherosclerosis — the progressive endothelial dysfunction, lipid accumulation, and smooth muscle proliferation that underlies the vast majority of peripheral arterial disease, aortic aneurysms, and carotid stenosis. The clinical presentations range from the incidental aortic aneurysm, detected on screening before rupture, to the acutely ischaemic limb requiring revascularisation within hours to prevent amputation, to the catastrophic ruptured aortic aneurysm, where 50% of patients die before reaching hospital. The overarching principle is risk stratification: identify the patients at risk of the catastrophic event and intervene electively before it occurs.

## Pathophysiology

### Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

An aneurysm is defined as a permanent localised dilation of a vessel to more than 1.5× its normal diameter. The infrarenal aorta is the most common site (normal diameter: 2 cm; aneurysm: ≥3 cm). Risk factors for AAA: male sex (6× more common in men), smoking (most important modifiable risk — smokers have 7× higher risk), hypertension, atherosclerosis, family history (first-degree relative with AAA → 20% risk in males), age >65.

**Mechanism of expansion and rupture:**
- Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) secreted by macrophages in the aortic wall degrade elastin and collagen → loss of structural integrity → progressive expansion
- **LaPlace's law:** Wall tension (T) = Pressure × Radius; as the aneurysm grows, wall tension increases disproportionately → accelerating expansion → rupture risk rises sharply above 5.5 cm
- **Rupture risk:** <5.5 cm: 1–2%/year; 5.5–6 cm: 10–15%/year; 6–7 cm: 20–25%/year; >7 cm: 30–50%/year
- Average expansion rate: ~3 mm/year (but highly variable; rate >10 mm/year = high rupture risk)

### Peripheral Arterial Disease (PAD)

Progressive atherosclerotic narrowing of the iliac, femoral, popliteal, tibial, and peroneal arteries → reduced blood flow to the lower limb. The ischaemic cascade:
- **Intermittent claudication:** Blood supply is adequate at rest but insufficient during exercise → anaerobic metabolism → lactic acid accumulation → muscle cramping (claudication)
- **Critical limb ischaemia (CLI):** Blood flow insufficient even at rest → rest pain (nocturnal pain, often in the foot, relieved by hanging foot down), tissue loss (ischaemic ulcers — punched-out, painful, on pressure areas; vs. venous ulcers — shallow, over medial malleolus, less painful)
- **Acute limb ischaemia:** See below

**ABPI (ankle-brachial pressure index):**
- Normal: 0.9–1.3
- Mild PAD: 0.7–0.9
- Moderate PAD: 0.5–0.7 (intermittent claudication typical)
- Severe PAD/CLI: <0.5 (rest pain, tissue loss)
- >1.3: Non-compressible calcified vessels (diabetics, CKD) — falsely elevated; use toe-brachial index instead

### Acute Limb Ischaemia — The 6 Ps

Sudden cessation of blood flow to a limb from:
- **Thrombosis in situ** (acute-on-chronic PAD — on a background of atherosclerotic stenosis; slower onset, some collateral flow); or
- **Embolism** (from proximal source — AF, LV thrombus post-MI, aortic mural thrombus, paradoxical embolus; sudden onset, no collaterals, dramatic presentation)

**Rutherford classification and the 6 Ps:**
- **P**ain (severe, constant)
- **P**allor (white, then mottled/dusky)
- **P**ulselessness (absent distal pulses)
- **P**araesthesia (tingling — earliest neurological sign = incomplete ischaemia, potentially salvageable)
- **P**aralysis (motor loss = advanced ischaemia — complete loss ≥6h = irreversible; muscle rigidity = non-viable)
- **P**erishing cold

**Rutherford Grade I** (viable, no neurological deficit) — elective revascularisation. **Grade IIa** (marginally threatened, mild sensory loss only) — urgent revascularisation. **Grade IIb** (immediately threatened, sensory + motor loss, no paralysis) — emergency within hours. **Grade III** (irreversible, paralysed, anaesthetic, muscle rigor) — amputation only.

## Clinical Presentation

### AAA — Presentations

**Asymptomatic (incidentally detected/screening):** The majority. Detected on abdominal USS or CT for another reason.

**Symptomatic (unruptured):** Back pain or abdominal pain from expansion or leak; pulsatile abdominal mass; thromboembolic complications (blue toe syndrome from distal embolisation of mural thrombus).

**Rupture:** Catastrophic triad: sudden severe abdominal/back pain → haemodynamic collapse → pulsatile abdominal mass. Classic "herald bleed" (retroperitoneal haematoma tamponade with transient haemostasis → false period of relative stability → re-bleed). 50% die before reaching hospital; of those reaching hospital, operative mortality 40–50% (IMPROVE trial: endovascular repair vs. open repair — no mortality difference overall, but EVAR faster and fewer complications in patients anatomically suitable).

### Carotid Artery Disease

**Asymptomatic carotid stenosis:** Found incidentally on USS; risk of ipsilateral stroke is low (~1%/year for >70% stenosis without symptoms). ACST-1 trial: carotid endarterectomy for asymptomatic stenosis >60% reduced 5-year stroke risk (6% vs. 12%) but absolute benefit is small when best medical therapy is given.

**Symptomatic carotid stenosis:** TIA or minor stroke ipsilateral to the stenosis. Risk of recurrent stroke: ~10% at 48h, ~15% at 7 days, ~18% at 30 days. Treatment: **carotid endarterectomy (CEA) within 2 weeks of symptom onset** dramatically reduces recurrence (NASCET, ECST trials: CEA reduced absolute stroke risk from 26% to 9% at 3 years for 70–99% stenosis). CEA vs. carotid artery stenting (CAS): CEA superior for elderly patients and those without access anatomy issues; CAS preferred for high-risk surgical patients.

**NASCET criteria for stenosis measurement:** The percentage stenosis is calculated relative to the diameter of the distal internal carotid artery (not the common carotid); this is the internationally accepted method.

## Diagnosis

**AAA:**
- **Ultrasound abdomen:** Screening test and surveillance measurement — sensitivity >95% for AAA; cheaper and no radiation; limited for juxtarenal/suprarenal anatomy
- **CT aortogram (with IV contrast):** Definitive pre-operative planning — defines AAA morphology (neck length/diameter/angulation, iliac involvement, renal artery relationship) to determine EVAR vs. open suitability
- **MRI/MRA:** Avoided in emergency (time); used for surveillance in young patients (no radiation)

**PAD:**
- **ABPI:** First-line non-invasive test
- **Duplex ultrasound:** Identifies stenosis location and severity; used for planning endovascular interventions
- **CT angiography (CTA):** Full arterial runoff from aorta to foot; pre-operative planning for bypass or endovascular
- **Digital subtraction angiography (DSA):** Gold standard but invasive; used when intervention is planned at the same session

**Acute limb ischaemia:** Clinical diagnosis + immediate heparinisation; CT angiography if time allows and limb viable; proceed to theatre if grade IIb/III.

## Management

### AAA — Surveillance and Intervention

**UK NHS AAA Screening Programme:** One-time USS for all men at age 65; if AAA detected:
- <3 cm: normal, no follow-up
- 3–4.4 cm: annual surveillance USS
- 4.5–5.4 cm: 3-monthly surveillance USS
- ≥5.5 cm: refer to vascular surgery for repair

**Indication for repair:** AAA ≥5.5 cm in men (≥5 cm in women — rupture risk higher for equivalent size) OR rapid expansion (>1 cm/year) OR symptomatic (pain/tenderness regardless of size).

**EVAR (endovascular aortic aneurysm repair):** Stent-graft delivered via femoral artery access, deployed to exclude the aneurysm sac from circulation. Suitable only when anatomy is favourable (adequate neck length ≥15 mm, neck angle <60°, suitable iliac access). **IMPROVE trial** (UK, 2014): Randomised EVAR vs. open for ruptured AAA — no survival advantage overall but lower 30-day mortality in anatomically suitable patients. EVAR-1 trial (elective): EVAR has lower 30-day mortality (1.7% vs. 4.7%) but no difference in long-term survival; EVAR requires lifelong CT surveillance for endoleak (incomplete aneurysm exclusion → continued pressurisation → ongoing rupture risk).

**Open repair:** Transperitoneal or retroperitoneal; tube or bifurcated Dacron graft sewn in after aortic clamping. Higher peri-operative mortality than EVAR (4–5% vs. 1.7%) but durable long-term — no endoleak surveillance required. Preferred when anatomy unsuitable for EVAR.

**Ruptured AAA:** Resuscitate with permissive hypotension (SBP 50–80 mmHg until aortic clamping — CRASH-2 principle; avoid over-resuscitation → clot disruption); EVAR preferred over open if anatomy suitable (faster, less physiological insult); O-negative blood immediately.

### PAD — Medical and Interventional

**Best medical therapy (all PAD regardless of severity):**
- **Antiplatelet therapy:** Aspirin 75 mg or clopidogrel 75 mg/day (CAPRIE trial: clopidogrel superior to aspirin for PAD)
- **Statin:** Regardless of baseline cholesterol — reduces MACE and may slow PAD progression
- **ACE inhibitor or ARB:** For CV risk reduction + renal protection
- **BP control target:** <130/80 mmHg
- **Smoking cessation:** The most effective intervention for slowing PAD progression; reduces amputation risk
- **Structured exercise programme:** 30 min walking exercise 3× weekly supervised → improves claudication distances by 100–150%; as effective as endovascular intervention for intermittent claudication (CLEVER trial)

**Intermittent claudication intervention:** Lifestyle-limiting despite exercise programme → angioplasty ± stenting for iliac/femoral disease; bypass surgery for multilevel disease. **Cilostazol** (phosphodiesterase III inhibitor — vasodilator + antiplatelet): modest improvement in claudication distance; licensed for intermittent claudication but limited uptake.

**Critical limb ischaemia:** Emergency — threatened limb within days/weeks. Revascularisation essential:
- **Angioplasty/stenting** (endovascular): first-line if anatomy suitable (shorter segment disease, iliac/SFA)
- **Bypass surgery** (open): fem-pop bypass (above/below knee — reversed or in-situ long saphenous vein; vein superior to synthetic); fem-tibial bypass for distal disease; synthetic (PTFE, Dacron) if no vein available
- **Amputation:** Last resort — below-knee preferred (better rehabilitation, prosthetic fitting); phantom limb pain common

### Acute Limb Ischaemia

1. **Immediate heparinisation** (unfractionated heparin 5000 unit IV bolus → infusion) — prevents clot propagation
2. **Analgesia** (IV morphine)
3. **Revascularisation (time-critical):**
   - **Embolectomy** (Fogarty catheter): for embolic cause — under local anaesthesia; transverse arteriotomy at the site; pass Fogarty balloon catheter beyond the clot, inflate, withdraw; may restore flow immediately
   - **Catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT):** For thrombotic cause (slower onset, more amenable to chemical dissolution); infuse alteplase or urokinase directly into the thrombus via catheter; takes hours; requires monitoring for bleeding
   - **Surgical bypass:** For complex arterial disease not amenable to other approaches
4. **Fasciotomy:** If ischaemia >6h or severe swelling after reperfusion — compartment syndrome (high pressure in closed fascial compartments compresses vessels → ongoing ischaemia after successful revascularisation); four-compartment calf fasciotomy

**Reperfusion injury:** After revascularisation → reactive oxygen species, inflammatory mediators + potassium from necrotic muscle released into circulation → hyperkalaemia (can cause cardiac arrest), rhabdomyolysis, AKI (myoglobin nephrotoxicity), ARDS, systemic inflammatory response.

## Complications

- **AAA repair:** Endoleak (EVAR — Types I–V; Type I = proximal/distal seal failure = high rupture risk → urgent reintervention), graft infection, aortoenteric fistula (late — typically 5–7 years), sexual dysfunction (inferior mesenteric artery ligation), renal failure
- **PAD revascularisation:** Re-stenosis (in-stent restenosis: endovascular stents), graft thrombosis (bypass), wound infection, lymphocele, cardiac events (peri-operative MI)
- **CEA:** Peri-operative stroke (embolism during carotid manipulation), wound haematoma (risk of tracheal compression), cranial nerve injury (vagus, hypoglossal), recurrent stenosis, hyperperfusion syndrome (post-CEA headache, seizures, haemorrhagic stroke from autoregulatory failure)
- **Acute limb ischaemia reperfusion:** Compartment syndrome, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, hyperkalaemia, amputation despite revascularisation

## Clinical Insight

The management of an AAA growing from 4.8 cm to 5.7 cm in 12 months is not simply "it's now ≥5.5 cm, operate." The rate of growth (9 mm in 12 months) is itself an indication for repair even if the aneurysm had not reached the 5.5 cm threshold, because rapid expansion (>10 mm/year) is an independent predictor of rupture risk regardless of absolute size. This patient clearly needs referral to vascular surgery, risk factor optimisation (BP control, statin, aspirin), EVAR anatomy assessment (CT aortogram), and repair.

The distinction between claudication (which can be treated conservatively with exercise and medical therapy) and critical limb ischaemia (which requires urgent revascularisation) is the most important triage decision in PAD. An ABPI of 0.52 with claudication at 80 metres is uncomfortable but not immediately threatening the limb. Critical limb ischaemia — rest pain + ABPI <0.5, or tissue loss (ulcer, gangrene) regardless of ABPI — is a vascular emergency. The ischaemic forefoot at 2 AM is not a condition that waits until the morning list.

The CEA-within-2-weeks rule for symptomatic carotid stenosis is one of the most evidence-based time-critical interventions in all of vascular surgery. The stroke risk after TIA or minor stroke is highest in the first 48–72 hours (ABCD2 score predicts this: age, blood pressure, clinical features, duration, diabetes — score ≥4 = high 7-day stroke risk). CEA performed within 2 weeks of symptom onset (ideally within 2–7 days) reduces absolute 3-year stroke risk from ~26% to ~9% in patients with 70–99% stenosis. CEA performed after 2 weeks loses much of this benefit because the highest-risk period has passed.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 72-year-old man, AAA 5.7 cm (grown from 4.8 cm in 12 months) + symptomatic PAD (ABPI 0.52, claudication at 80 metres). Hypertension, T2DM, heavy smoker.

**AAA management:**
**Step 1 — Refer to vascular surgery:** Size ≥5.5 cm + rapid expansion (9 mm/year) = both independent indications for repair.

**Step 2 — Pre-operative workup:**
- CT aortogram: define neck anatomy (length, diameter, angulation), iliac morphology → EVAR vs. open decision. EVAR preferred if anatomy suitable (less peri-operative mortality, faster recovery) given his age and comorbidities.
- Cardiopulmonary risk: CPET; cardiology review (RCRI ≥2: diabetes + high-risk surgery)
- Optimise: BP <140/90 (amlodipine dose increase/add ACEi); statin; stop smoking urgently

**Step 3 — Repair:** Assuming suitable anatomy → EVAR. Post-EVAR: CT surveillance at 1 month, 12 months, then annually for endoleak.

**PAD management:**
- ABPI 0.52 = moderate PAD with claudication (not critical limb ischaemia)
- Best medical therapy: Aspirin → switch to **clopidogrel 75 mg** (PAD indication); statin (atorvastatin 80 mg); strict BP control; smoking cessation
- **Structured exercise programme** as first-line intervention for claudication (3× weekly supervised walking, 6 weeks minimum)
- If claudication remains lifestyle-limiting after 3 months → duplex USS/CTA → angioplasty ± stenting (femoral/popliteal lesion likely in this patient)

**Exam trigger:** *72-year-old + AAA ≥5.5 cm + rapid expansion >10 mm/year + symptomatic PAD* → both require management. AAA = refer for EVAR planning. PAD with claudication (ABPI 0.52) = best medical therapy + exercise first; only consider revascularisation if conservative management fails. The distractor is urgently revascularising the PAD before the AAA repair — the ruptured AAA is immediately life-threatening; the claudication is not.
