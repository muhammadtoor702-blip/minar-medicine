## Overview

A pneumothorax is the presence of air in the pleural space, between the visceral and parietal pleura, causing lung collapse. It is classified by aetiology as primary (no underlying lung disease) or secondary (complicating known lung disease), and clinically as spontaneous, traumatic, or iatrogenic. The distinction between primary and secondary is the most clinically important because management differs substantially — a small pneumothorax that is safely observed in a young athlete can be immediately life-threatening in a patient with severe COPD.

---

## Pathophysiology

The pleural space is normally maintained at slightly negative pressure relative to the atmosphere, which keeps the lungs inflated against the chest wall. Introduction of air — whether through a tear in the visceral pleura or a penetrating chest wound — equalises pleural pressure with atmospheric pressure, collapsing the lung.

**Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (PSP)** occurs in young, tall, thin individuals without diagnosed lung disease. The classic patient is a male aged 20–30 with a high arm span-to-height ratio. The cause is rupture of subpleural blebs or bullae — small air-filled cysts that develop preferentially at the lung apices, where pleural pressure gradients are greatest in tall individuals. Despite the absence of clinically apparent "lung disease," these patients have subclinical structural vulnerability at the apex.

**Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax (SSP)** complicates underlying pulmonary pathology. The lung has reduced reserve, making even a small pneumothorax potentially life-threatening because the patient cannot compensate with the contralateral lung. Common causes include:
- **COPD** (most common) — rupture of emphysematous bullae; the classic diagnostic difficulty on CXR is distinguishing a bulla from a pneumothorax
- **Asthma** — particularly during severe acute attacks with high intrathoracic pressures
- **Cystic fibrosis** — bilateral, recurrent; a major complication
- **Tuberculosis** — cavitating disease; may produce bronchopleural fistula
- **Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP)** — bilateral pneumothoraces in an HIV patient with bilateral infiltrates is PCP until proven otherwise
- **Marfan syndrome and connective tissue disorders** — structural collagen defects
- **Lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM)** — young women, bilateral recurrent pneumothoraces, CT shows diffuse thin-walled lung cysts; associated with TSC2 mutations
- **Langerhans cell histiocytosis** — smoking-related; upper lobe cysts

**Iatrogenic pneumothorax:** Central venous catheter insertion (subclavian or internal jugular approach — hit apex of lung), pleural aspiration or biopsy, transbronchial lung biopsy, mechanical ventilation (particularly with high PEEP or large tidal volumes — barotrauma), liver biopsy (right-sided, via diaphragm). All percutaneous thoracic procedures carry a risk.

**Tension pneumothorax** is a medical emergency. A one-way valve effect — air enters the pleural space on inspiration but cannot escape — causes progressive accumulation, mediastinal shift **away** from the affected side, compression of the contralateral lung, and kinking of the great veins (SVC, IVC). The result is catastrophic reduction in venous return and cardiac output. Without immediate decompression, tension pneumothorax is rapidly fatal.

## Clinical Presentation

**Typical features:** Sudden ipsilateral pleuritic chest pain and dyspnoea, often at rest. In PSP, symptoms may be mild and the patient may wait hours before presenting — do not be falsely reassured by a delay in presentation.

**Ipsilateral examination findings:** Hyperresonance to percussion, reduced or absent breath sounds, reduced chest expansion, reduced tactile vocal fremitus. Tracheal deviation **away** from the affected side occurs in large or tension pneumothorax.

**Tension pneumothorax — a clinical diagnosis:** Do not wait for CXR. Features include the above plus haemodynamic instability (hypotension, tachycardia, impending cardiovascular collapse), elevated JVP, cyanosis, and deteriorating conscious level. **Decompress immediately** with a large-bore cannula; do not delay for imaging.

**Differential for sudden pleuritic chest pain:**
- PE — risk factors for VTE, tachycardia, hypoxia, normal percussion
- Pneumonia — fever, productive cough, consolidation on CXR, dullness to percussion
- ACS — central, radiates to arm/jaw, ECG changes, not positional
- Aortic dissection — tearing, maximal at onset, mediastinal widening, BP asymmetry
- Musculoskeletal — reproducible on palpation, positional

## Diagnosis

**CXR (erect, PA):** Visible pleural line (white) with absent lung markings beyond it (black) — do not confuse with skin folds (which extend beyond the chest wall) or bullae (no definite lung edge). Mediastinal shift in large or tension pneumothorax.

**BTS 2023 sizing:**
- **Small:** rim of air <2cm at the level of the hilum
- **Large:** rim of air ≥2cm at the level of the hilum

**CT thorax:** Indicated when CXR is equivocal (especially in COPD — distinguishing bullae from pneumothorax is genuinely difficult and mistaking a bulla for a pneumothorax and draining it causes bronchopleural fistula), for pre-surgical planning, or to assess underlying pulmonary pathology causing SSP.

**ABG:** In SSP or if SpO₂ is low — may show type 1 respiratory failure from V/Q mismatch.

## Management

### Tension Pneumothorax — Immediate Action

**Clinical diagnosis — do not wait for CXR.** Insert a large-bore (14–16G) cannula into the 2nd intercostal space at the midclavicular line (or 4th/5th ICS at the anterior axillary line — increasingly preferred as less likely to be obstructed by pectoral muscle). A rush of air confirms the diagnosis. This is temporising only — **insert a chest drain immediately** after needle decompression.

### Primary Spontaneous Pneumothorax (BTS 2023)

The 2023 BTS guidelines introduced a significantly more conservative approach, reflecting evidence that many PSPs resolve without intervention:

**Small PSP (<2cm) and minimally symptomatic (ambulatory, SpO₂ ≥90%, HR <120, RR <20):**
- **Conservative management is now first-line** — discharge with clear safety netting (return immediately if worsening breathlessness, pain, or any concern), analgesia, and advice
- High-flow oxygen (15 L/min) if admitted — accelerates nitrogen reabsorption (oxygen replaces nitrogen in pleural air; nitrogen is then absorbed down concentration gradient)
- Review at 2–4 weeks with repeat CXR

**Large PSP (≥2cm) or significantly symptomatic:**
- **Needle aspiration** — first-line intervention. 2nd ICS MCL or 4th/5th ICS anterior axillary line; aspirate with a 16G cannula attached to a 3-way tap; aspirate up to 2.5L. Successful if the lung re-expands and remains expanded after 1 hour.
- If aspiration fails or is not tolerated → **small-bore Seldinger chest drain** (8–14 Fr); connect to underwater seal drain

### Secondary Spontaneous Pneumothorax

All SSP patients require admission — even a small SSP can be life-threatening in a patient with compromised pulmonary reserve.
- Small (<2cm) and mildly symptomatic → admit, oxygen, consider needle aspiration
- Large (≥2cm) or symptomatic → chest drain
- Low threshold for specialist respiratory/cardiothoracic referral
- **Do not attempt needle aspiration in COPD if the cause might be a bulla** — CT first to distinguish

### Surgical Referral Indications

- **Second ipsilateral** episode of pneumothorax
- First **contralateral** episode
- Bilateral **simultaneous** pneumothorax
- Failure of drainage (persistent air leak >5 days despite adequate drain position)
- **At-risk occupation** — pilots and divers require bilateral surgical pleurodesis before returning to work; a single PSP permanently grounds a pilot until pleurodesis has been performed

Surgery involves **VATS (video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery)** with bullectomy (removal of apical blebs) and mechanical or chemical pleurodesis (abrasion of visceral and parietal pleura to induce inflammation and adhesion). Recurrence rate after VATS pleurodesis is <5% (vs ~25–50% after first PSP without intervention).

**Advice on discharge:**
- No flying for at least 1 week after confirmed radiological resolution (2 weeks if intervention was required)
- **Diving is permanently contraindicated until bilateral surgical pleurodesis has been performed** — the pressure changes on ascent from depth are incompatible with even treated recurrence risk

## Complications

- **Tension pneumothorax** — haemodynamic collapse if not immediately decompressed; rapidly fatal without intervention
- **Haemothorax** — particularly with traumatic pneumothorax; blood and air in pleural space; requires large-bore drain (28–32 Fr)
- **Persistent air leak** (bronchopleural fistula) — bubbling persists >5 days; requires surgical assessment
- **Re-expansion pulmonary oedema** — rare, occurs after rapid re-expansion of a large or long-standing pneumothorax; limit initial drainage rate to 1L in first hour for large/tension pneumothorax; clamp drain if patient develops cough and ipsilateral infiltrates
- **Recurrence** — 25–50% after first untreated PSP within 5 years; ~80% after second untreated episode

## Clinical Insight

Tension pneumothorax is a clinical diagnosis. Waiting for a CXR while a patient with a deviated trachea, absent breath sounds, and haemodynamic collapse deteriorates is a potentially fatal error. Recognise the clinical picture and decompress. The needle insertion takes 10 seconds. If you are wrong, you have made a small pneumothorax slightly larger — this is far better than failing to act.

In a COPD patient with sudden deterioration, the differential between a pneumothorax and a large bulla on CXR is genuinely difficult. The CXR may show a lucency with what appears to be a lung edge, but this could be the wall of a bulla. If in doubt, CT thorax clarifies the anatomy before inserting a drain. Draining a large emphysematous bulla in a patient with severe COPD creates a bronchopleural fistula and is very difficult to manage.

The combination of HIV, bilateral infiltrates (ground-glass pattern), bilateral spontaneous pneumothoraces, and marked LDH elevation is PCP (*Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia) until proven otherwise. The cystic destruction characteristic of PCP creates a predisposition to bilateral pneumothoraces, and bilateral cases in a young adult should always prompt an HIV test if not already done.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 19-year-old tall, thin university student. Sudden left-sided sharp chest pain and breathlessness while sitting in a lecture. RR 20, SpO₂ 96%, HR 94, BP 118/74. Hyperresonant on left, reduced breath sounds left. CXR: visible pleural line with a **3 cm rim of air** at the apex.

**Step 1 — Diagnose primary spontaneous pneumothorax:** The classic patient — young, tall, thin male (high arm-span to height ratio, apical subpleural blebs under greatest mechanical tension). No underlying lung disease. Spontaneous onset at rest. The examination findings confirm it: **hyperresonance** (air in pleural space has lower density than consolidated or collapsed lung) + **reduced breath sounds**. The CXR shows the definitive feature — a **visible pleural line** (the visceral pleura) with absent lung markings lateral to it.

**Step 2 — Exclude tension pneumothorax (rule this out first):** Tension features would be: haemodynamic compromise (hypotension, marked tachycardia), severe distress, deviated trachea away from left, elevated JVP, deteriorating consciousness. This patient is haemodynamically stable (BP 118/74, HR 94, SpO₂ 96%) — this is NOT a tension pneumothorax. Do not perform emergency needle decompression in a stable patient.

**Step 3 — Size classification (BTS 2023): LARGE pneumothorax:** A 3 cm rim of air at the level of the hilum = **large** (≥2 cm). This requires active intervention.

**Step 4 — First-line management for large PSP: needle aspiration (not chest drain):** BTS 2023 guidelines:
- Insert a 16G cannula into the 2nd ICS midclavicular line (or 4th/5th ICS anterior axillary line)
- Aspirate with a 3-way tap; maximum 2.5 L
- CXR after 1 hour — if lung has re-expanded and remains expanded → discharge with follow-up CXR at 2–4 weeks
- If aspiration fails or does not achieve re-expansion → **small-bore Seldinger chest drain** (8–14 Fr)

**Step 5 — Discharge advice (these are exam-favourites):**
- **No flying** for at least **1 week** after confirmed radiological resolution (2 weeks if intervention was required)
- **Diving is permanently contraindicated** until bilateral surgical pleurodesis is performed — pressure changes on ascent from depth will cause expansion of any residual pleural abnormality
- Recurrence risk: **25–50% within 5 years** after first untreated PSP. If this recurs → refer for **VATS** pleurodesis (bullectomy + mechanical pleurodesis — reduces recurrence to <5%)

**Exam trigger:** *Tall thin young man + sudden pleuritic chest pain + hyperresonance + 3 cm rim on CXR = large PSP*. Per BTS 2023, **needle aspiration is first-line** (not chest drain). No tension features = do NOT decompress with emergency needle. The distractor is immediately inserting a chest drain (first-line is aspiration) or discharging without intervention (3 cm = large = intervention required).
