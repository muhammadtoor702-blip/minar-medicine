## Overview

The first hours of life are physiologically extraordinary — and dangerous. The neonatal period (birth to 28 days) carries the highest mortality of any period in childhood. Most deaths occur within the first 24 hours, and most are preventable. The common neonatal emergencies cluster around respiratory transition, infection, jaundice, and metabolic disturbance. Recognising them requires understanding that neonatal physiology differs fundamentally from paediatric or adult physiology: normal values, clinical signs, and drug doses are entirely different, and the margin for error is narrow.

---

## Pathophysiology

### Respiratory Transition at Birth

At birth, the fluid-filled fetal lungs must transform into an air-breathing organ within minutes. Adrenaline surge and chest compression through the birth canal expel lung fluid. The first breath generates enormous negative pleural pressure, opening collapsed airways. Surfactant — produced by type II pneumocytes from approximately 24 weeks' gestation — reduces alveolar surface tension and prevents alveolar collapse at end-expiration.

**Respiratory distress syndrome (RDS):** Caused by surfactant deficiency, predominantly in preterm infants. Without surfactant, alveoli collapse after each breath (atelectasis), increasing work of breathing, causing V/Q mismatch, hypoxia, and hypercapnia. The lecithin-to-sphingomyelin (L/S) ratio in amniotic fluid reflects surfactant maturity: an L/S ratio ≥2 indicates fetal lung maturity.

**Transient tachypnoea of the newborn (TTN):** Retained lung fluid, particularly common after elective caesarean section (bypasses the thoracic compression that expels fluid). The fluid reabsorbs over 24–48 hours via lymphatics and pulmonary capillaries.

**Meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS):** In utero hypoxia → fetal gasping → aspiration of meconium into airways. Thick meconium causes: (1) ball-valve obstruction causing air trapping and pneumothorax; (2) chemical pneumonitis from bile acid toxicity; (3) inactivation of surfactant → secondary surfactant deficiency; (4) persistent pulmonary hypertension of the newborn (PPHN).

### Neonatal Jaundice

Unconjugated bilirubin (fat-soluble, crosses blood-brain barrier) is produced from red cell breakdown. Neonates have higher RBC mass, shorter RBC lifespan (70 days vs 120 days in adults), and immature hepatic conjugation capacity — they are physiologically prone to hyperbilirubinaemia.

Phototherapy converts unconjugated bilirubin to water-soluble geometric isomers (predominantly lumirubin) that are excreted in bile and urine without hepatic conjugation. Exchange transfusion replaces the infant's blood and removes both bilirubin and sensitised red cells.

## Clinical Presentation

### Signs of Neonatal Respiratory Distress

All neonates with respiratory distress show some combination of:
- **Tachypnoea** (RR >60/min) — the earliest sign
- **Grunting** — forced expiration against a partially closed glottis, generating intrinsic PEEP to prevent alveolar collapse; pathological when present at rest
- **Intercostal and subcostal recession** — increased work of breathing
- **Nasal flaring** — attempts to reduce nasal airway resistance
- **Central cyanosis** — SpO₂ <94% on room air; requires immediate oxygen

**Differentiating common causes:**

| Feature | RDS | TTN | MAS |
|---------|-----|-----|-----|
| Gestation | Preterm (especially <32w) | Term, especially CS | Term/post-term |
| Onset | Birth or first hours | First hours | At or shortly after birth |
| Severity | Moderate to severe | Usually mild | Variable, can be severe |
| CXR | Ground-glass + air bronchograms | Fluid in fissures, hyperinflation | Patchy infiltrates, hyperinflation |
| Course | Worsens first 48h then improves | Improves by 24-48h | Variable; PPHN risk |

### Neonatal Jaundice

- **Physiological jaundice**: appears after 24 hours, peaks day 3–5, resolves by day 14. Caused by the normal neonatal haemolysis + immature conjugation.
- **Pathological jaundice**: jaundice within 24 hours of birth is haemolytic until proven otherwise — Rhesus incompatibility, ABO incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis.
- **Prolonged jaundice** (>14 days in term infants, >21 days in preterm): check conjugated bilirubin fraction — conjugated jaundice (direct >1 mg/dL or >20% of total) = biliary atresia or other hepatic disease until proven otherwise. Split bilirubin is mandatory.
- **Kernicterus**: unconjugated bilirubin deposition in basal ganglia and hippocampus — acute: hypotonia, poor feeding, seizures, opisthotonus; chronic: choreoathetoid cerebral palsy, sensorineural hearing loss, intellectual disability.

### Neonatal Sepsis

Neonates are immunocompromised — absent IgA, immature neutrophil function, reduced complement. Signs are non-specific: poor feeding, temperature instability (hypothermia more common than fever in neonates), lethargy, abdominal distension, apnoea, tachycardia, mottled skin, prolonged CRT.

## Diagnosis

**Respiratory distress:**
- Arterial blood gas: assess PaO₂, PaCO₂, and pH (acidosis worsens PPHN)
- CXR: ground-glass + air bronchograms (RDS); fluid in interlobar fissures (TTN); patchy bilateral infiltrates ± pneumothorax (MAS)
- Pulse oximetry: pre-ductal (right hand) and post-ductal (foot) SpO₂ — a gap >3% suggests right-to-left ductal shunting (PPHN)
- Echocardiography: assess for PPHN and congenital heart disease

**Neonatal jaundice:**
- Total and direct (conjugated) serum bilirubin — not transcutaneous bilirubinometry alone if clinical concern
- Plot on Bhutani normogram (hour-specific bilirubin nomogram) to determine phototherapy/exchange transfusion threshold
- Blood group + direct antiglobulin test (DAT/Coombs) — if jaundice within 24h or rapidly rising
- FBC: haemolytic anaemia, polycythaemia
- G6PD assay in males from high-prevalence populations (Mediterranean, African, Asian)
- If conjugated fraction elevated: liver function tests, USS liver and biliary tree, HIDA scan (hepatobiliary scintigraphy)

**Neonatal sepsis:**
- Blood cultures (most important — draw before antibiotics)
- FBC: leucocytosis or leucopenia + neutropaenia; thrombocytopaenia
- CRP and procalcitonin (elevated in bacterial infection but may be low early)
- Lumbar puncture: if clinically stable — bacterial meningitis is more common in neonates than at any other age; do not withhold LP if the infant is well enough
- Urine MC&S, chest X-ray

## Management

### Respiratory Distress

**Initial stabilisation:** Warm, dry, stimulate. APGAR score at 1 and 5 minutes (Appearance/Pulse/Grimace/Activity/Respiration — each 0–2, total 0–10; score ≤7 at 1 min or ≤5 at 5 min requires active intervention).

**Supplemental oxygen:** Target SpO₂ 91–95% in preterm infants (excessive O₂ causes retinopathy of prematurity and bronchopulmonary dysplasia). In term infants with MAS/PPHN: target SpO₂ 95–98%.

**CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure):** First-line for RDS in preterm infants. Delivered via nasal prongs or mask. Prevents alveolar collapse without needing intubation; reduces need for surfactant and ventilation.

**Exogenous surfactant:** Indicated for confirmed RDS — give early (within 2 hours). Administered via endotracheal tube. Beractant (Survanta) or poractant alfa (Curosurf). Dramatically reduces mortality and air leak complications in preterm RDS.

**Antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone 12 mg IM × 2 doses, 24h apart):** Given to mothers at risk of preterm delivery 24–34 weeks' gestation — accelerate fetal surfactant production, reduce RDS mortality by ~50%. One of the most effective interventions in neonatal medicine.

**MAS:** CPAP or mechanical ventilation; high-frequency oscillatory ventilation (HFOV) for refractory hypoxia; inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) for PPHN — selective pulmonary vasodilator, reduces right-to-left shunting; ECMO for severe refractory cases.

### Neonatal Jaundice

**Phototherapy:** Indicated when total serum bilirubin reaches the threshold on the Bhutani nomogram (depends on gestational age and postnatal age in hours). Blue-green light (wavelength 430–490 nm) converts bilirubin to lumirubin. Double phototherapy (two light sources above and below) is more effective. Continue until bilirubin falls below threshold.

**Exchange transfusion:** Indicated when bilirubin exceeds the exchange transfusion threshold on the nomogram, or when phototherapy fails, or when acute bilirubin encephalopathy develops. Double-volume exchange (160 mL/kg) removes ~85% of sensitised RBCs and reduces bilirubin by ~50%. Risks: thrombocytopenia, electrolyte disturbance, infection, necrotising enterocolitis, line complications.

**Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG):** For haemolytic disease (Rh or ABO incompatibility) — IVIG 0.5–1 g/kg reduces haemolysis and may avoid exchange transfusion.

### Neonatal Sepsis

**Empirical antibiotics — early onset sepsis (≤72 hours):**
- IV benzylpenicillin 25–50 mg/kg every 12h (covers GBS) + IV gentamicin (covers Gram-negatives)
- If Listeria suspected (maternal history): replace with amoxicillin
- Duration: 48h minimum; extend to 7–14 days if culture positive or CRP persistently elevated

**Late-onset sepsis (>72 hours):** Organisms shift toward healthcare-associated pathogens (coagulase-negative Staphylococci, S. aureus, Gram-negatives). Vancomycin + gentamicin; target gentamicin levels; line removal if central line-associated.

**Neonatal hypoglycaemia (glucose <47 mg/dL):** Risk groups — infants of diabetic mothers (macrosomic, high insulin), IUGR infants (diminished glycogen stores), preterm infants. Management: early breast or formula feeds; if unable to feed or glucose remains low, IV 10% dextrose (2–3 mL/kg bolus then infusion at 4–6 mg/kg/min). Do not use 25% or 50% dextrose — hyperosmolar, causes sclerosis; neonates only receive 10% glucose.

## Complications

- **RDS**: chronic lung disease (bronchopulmonary dysplasia) in premature infants requiring prolonged ventilation; air leak (pneumothorax, pulmonary interstitial emphysema); intraventricular haemorrhage
- **MAS**: PPHN (mortality ~10–20% even with treatment); air trapping + pneumothorax
- **Jaundice**: kernicterus — permanent neurological damage; exchange transfusion complications
- **Neonatal sepsis**: meningitis (up to 30% of bacteraemic neonates develop meningitis); multiorgan failure; death
- **Hypoglycaemia**: permanent brain injury if severe or prolonged; seizures
- **Necrotising enterocolitis (NEC)**: bowel necrosis, predominantly in preterm infants; pneumatosis intestinalis on AXR; surgical NEC (perforation) requires laparotomy + bowel resection

## Clinical Insight

Jaundice within the first 24 hours of life is never physiological — it is haemolytic until proven otherwise. The commonest cause is Rhesus or ABO incompatibility. These infants can deteriorate from a safe bilirubin level to exchange transfusion territory within a matter of hours, so the bilirubin must be plotted on an hour-specific nomogram rather than compared to a static threshold, and repeat levels must be done frequently (every 4–6 hours in rapidly rising cases).

Grunting in a neonate is a sign of significant respiratory distress. The grunt generates intrinsic PEEP to keep the alveoli open. A neonate who is grunting at rest — not just with feeds — needs CPAP at minimum. Grunting disappears when the infant tires and can no longer generate the expiratory grunt, at which point respiratory failure and apnoea follow. The disappearance of grunting is not reassurance; it is a sign of impending collapse.

Prolonged neonatal jaundice with a raised conjugated bilirubin fraction is biliary atresia until proven otherwise. The therapeutic window for the Kasai portoenterostomy (surgical correction) is before 60 days of age — after that, outcomes are dramatically worse. A healthy-looking jaundiced baby at 3 weeks is not a reason for reassurance; a split bilirubin must be checked.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A term infant, 2 hours old, thick meconium-stained liquor at delivery. Grunting, subcostal recession, nasal flaring. SpO₂ 82% on room air.

**Step 1 — Recognise meconium aspiration syndrome:** This infant was born through thick meconium (not thin/watery — thin meconium is less clinically significant). The respiratory distress beginning at delivery in a term infant with thick meconium = MAS until proven otherwise. The key pathological processes are: airway obstruction from meconium plugs, chemical pneumonitis, surfactant inactivation, and the looming risk of PPHN.

**Step 2 — Immediate resuscitation:** ABCD neonatal resuscitation:
- Warmth and dry: prevent hypothermia (cold stress dramatically worsens respiratory distress and PPHN)
- Airway positioning: neutral neck position
- Supplemental O₂ to target SpO₂ ≥95% (term infant with hypoxia)
- If not breathing adequately → positive pressure ventilation via face mask (5 inflation breaths at 30 cmH₂O); if apnoeic/HR <60 → intubation + cardiac compressions

**Step 3 — Intubation and suction in thick meconium:** Current NLS/NRP guidance: routine intubation and suctioning of the trachea is no longer recommended for vigorous infants (good tone, HR >100, crying). If the infant is NOT vigorous (absent/depressed respirations, poor tone, HR <100) AND there is thick meconium → consider intubation + suction of the trachea with the ETT before establishing positive pressure ventilation (to remove obstructing meconium before forcing it deeper).

**Step 4 — Manage ongoing respiratory failure:**
- CPAP or intubation + ventilation depending on severity
- Obtain CXR (patchy infiltrates confirm MAS; exclude pneumothorax — a major complication of air trapping)
- Pre-ductal (right hand) vs post-ductal (foot) SpO₂: if gap >3% = right-to-left ductal shunting = PPHN developing — escalate to iNO
- Echo to assess pulmonary pressures and rule out structural cardiac disease

**Step 5 — Escalation pathway:**
- Conventional ventilation → HFOV for refractory hypoxia
- Inhaled nitric oxide for PPHN (selective pulmonary vasodilator)
- Consider surfactant (surfactant inactivation is part of MAS pathophysiology)
- ECMO at tertiary centre if all else fails

**Exam trigger:** *Term infant + thick meconium + respiratory distress at birth* → MAS. The distractor is assuming all neonatal respiratory distress is TTN (resolves by 48h) — MAS can deteriorate to PPHN and requires active management. The other trap is routinely intubating vigorous infants with meconium — current guidance is that vigorous infants should NOT have routine tracheal suctioning; only depressed infants warrant this intervention.
