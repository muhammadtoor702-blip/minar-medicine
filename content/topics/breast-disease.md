## Overview

Breast disease encompasses a spectrum from benign conditions (fibroadenoma, fibrocystic change, fat necrosis) to the most common cancer in women worldwide. One in eight women in the United Kingdom and United States will develop breast cancer in their lifetime. The clinical approach to any breast lump follows a structured protocol: **triple assessment** — clinical examination + imaging + pathology. No single component is sufficient alone; the three must be concordant before a benign diagnosis is made. Any discordance between clinical suspicion and imaging or pathology findings should prompt further investigation. Breast cancer is highly treatable when detected early — 5-year survival exceeds 90% for Stage I disease — making screening, timely referral, and MDT management the cornerstones of good practice.

## Pathophysiology

### Breast Cancer Biology

**Molecular subtypes (determined by immunohistochemistry and FISH):**

- **Luminal A (ER+/PR+, HER2−, Ki-67 low, ~40% of cases):** Best prognosis; driven by oestrogen receptor signalling; responds to endocrine therapy; low proliferative index; chemotherapy rarely adds significant benefit
- **Luminal B (ER+, HER2+ or ER+/high Ki-67, ~20%):** More proliferative than Luminal A; anti-HER2 therapy added if HER2+; endocrine therapy backbone
- **HER2-enriched (ER−, HER2+, ~15%):** Driven by HER2 (ERBB2) gene amplification on chromosome 17q12; aggressive behaviour; trastuzumab (anti-HER2 monoclonal antibody) dramatically improved prognosis
- **Triple-negative (ER−, PR−, HER2−, ~15%):** Most aggressive; no targeted therapy available (endocrine and anti-HER2 ineffective); treated with chemotherapy; enriched in BRCA1 carriers and Black women; paradoxically higher pathological complete response rate to neoadjuvant chemotherapy

**BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations:**
- **BRCA1** (chromosome 17q21): tumour suppressor; involved in DNA double-strand break repair via homologous recombination. Lifetime breast cancer risk: **65–80%**. Ovarian cancer risk: **39–44%**. BRCA1-associated cancers are predominantly triple-negative.
- **BRCA2** (chromosome 13q12): also involved in homologous recombination. Lifetime breast cancer risk: **45–85%**. Ovarian cancer risk: **11–17%**. Also increases risk in men (breast cancer) and pancreatic/prostate cancer.
- Clinically actionable: **PARP inhibitors** (olaparib, niraparib) exploit synthetic lethality — BRCA-mutated cells depend on PARP for DNA repair; PARP inhibition + loss of BRCA function = lethal DNA damage accumulation.

### Hormonal Influences

Lifetime oestrogen exposure is the dominant modifiable risk factor: early menarche, late menopause, nulliparity, delayed first pregnancy, HRT (particularly combined oestrogen-progesterone). The oestrogen receptor (ESR1 gene) drives proliferation in ER+ tumours through transcriptional activation of cyclin D1 and other cell-cycle genes. Tamoxifen is a selective oestrogen receptor modulator (SERM) — competitive partial agonist at the oestrogen receptor; in breast tissue it acts as an antagonist (blocks ER-driven proliferation); in bone and uterus it acts as a partial agonist (beneficial for bone; risk of endometrial cancer).

## Clinical Presentation

### Benign Breast Conditions

**Fibroadenoma:** The most common breast lump in women under 35. Arises from lobular stroma and epithelium. Characteristics: smooth, well-defined, mobile ("breast mouse"), rubbery, non-tender, typically 1–3 cm. Age: 15–35 years. Ultrasound: well-circumscribed, homogeneous, oval, hypoechoic. Core biopsy: benign. Management: if triple assessment benign and <3 cm, reassurance and discharge (patient option to excise if symptomatic). Giant fibroadenoma >5 cm = excision. Phyllodes tumour mimics fibroadenoma clinically but behaves differently: stroma proliferates, may be borderline or malignant; rapid growth is a warning sign; treatment = wide local excision with clear margins.

**Fibrocystic change (sclerosing adenosis, apocrine change):** Physiological exaggeration of normal cyclic breast changes; commonest in 30–50 year olds; bilateral, diffuse nodularity; cyclical breast pain (mastalgia); reassurance that risk of cancer is not increased (only atypical hyperplasia on biopsy increases risk slightly).

**Fat necrosis:** History of trauma (including seat belt injury, previous surgery, radiotherapy). Results in an irregular, firm, possibly skin-tethered lump — can be clinically and radiologically indistinguishable from cancer. Mammogram: oil cyst (radiolucent with rim calcification) = pathognomonic of fat necrosis. Core biopsy required if imaging not conclusive. Important not to over-diagnose cancer in post-traumatic or post-surgical lumps.

**Gynaecomastia:** Benign proliferation of male breast glandular tissue (not fat — hence distinguishable from lipomastia/pseudogynaecomastia by the presence of a disc of firm glandular tissue beneath the areola). Causes:
- **Physiological:** Neonatal, puberty (50–60% of adolescent males, resolves by age 18), senescence (testosterone declines, relative oestrogen excess)
- **Drug-induced (most important to identify and reverse):** Spironolactone (most common — aldosterone antagonist with anti-androgen activity), cimetidine, digoxin, anabolic steroids, marijuana, prostate cancer hormonal therapy (GnRH agonists/LHRH analogues), metoclopramide (raises prolactin)
- **Pathological:** Testicular germ cell tumour (hCG → stimulates oestrogen production — bilateral gynaecomastia in a young man = testicular tumour until proven otherwise; examine testes + serum hCG + AFP + LDH), liver cirrhosis (impaired oestrogen metabolism), hyperthyroidism, CKD

### Breast Cancer — Clinical Features

**The classic presentation:** A hard, irregular, painless lump with ill-defined margins that may be fixed to the overlying skin or deep fascia. Associated features indicate local invasion:
- **Skin tethering/dimpling:** Cooper's ligament involvement — oestrogen-responsive fibrous strands from breast parenchyma to overlying skin; pulled inward by the tumour
- **Peau d'orange:** Dermal lymphatic obstruction causing skin oedema with visible follicular depressions (like an orange peel); indicates locally advanced disease
- **Nipple retraction/deviation:** Subareolar tumour pulling the nipple inward
- **Nipple discharge (bloody):** Intraductal papilloma (most common cause of single duct bloody discharge) or ductal carcinoma in situ / invasive cancer

**Paget's disease of the nipple:** Eczema-like change of the nipple-areolar complex that does NOT respond to topical steroids — almost always indicates underlying DCIS or invasive cancer. Biopsy the nipple.

**Inflammatory breast cancer:** Rapid onset, diffuse erythema, oedema, warmth of the breast with peau d'orange — resembles mastitis clinically, but no fever, no localised abscess. Caused by dermal lymphatic permeation by tumour cells. Urgent bilateral mammogram + skin punch biopsy. Treated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy before surgery.

## Diagnosis

### Triple Assessment

**1. Clinical examination:**
- Inspect (asymmetry, skin changes, nipple abnormalities) and palpate (characteristics of the lump, lymph nodes — axillary, supraclavicular, infraclavicular)
- Assign clinical category: P1 (normal), P2 (benign), P3 (indeterminate), P4 (suspicious), P5 (malignant)

**2. Imaging:**
- **Mammography (age ≥40):** Views: mediolateral oblique (MLO) + craniocaudal (CC). Suspicious features: spiculated mass, architectural distortion, clustered microcalcifications (pleomorphic/casting type). Sensitivity 80–85%; lower in dense breasts. Assigned on a scoring system (UK BI-RADS equivalent M1–M5 or R1–R5 in the UK system).
- **Ultrasound (age <40 and all ages for assessment of palpable lesions):** Preferred for younger women (denser breast tissue reduces mammogram sensitivity); assesses node architecture, guides biopsy. Suspicious features: irregular margins, hypoechoic, taller-than-wide, posterior shadowing, internal vascularity.
- **MRI:** Not routine for diagnosis; used for: extent of disease pre-operatively (particularly lobular carcinoma, which is poorly defined on mammogram), assessment of the contralateral breast in BRCA carriers, DCIS extent, implant rupture, post-treatment residual disease.

**3. Pathology (biopsy):**
- **14G core needle biopsy (CNB):** The standard; provides tissue for histological analysis, ER/PR/HER2 status, grade. Ultrasound-guided for palpable lesions; stereotactic mammography-guided for impalpable mammographic lesions.
- **Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC):** Cells only (no tissue architecture); largely replaced by CNB in most centres; still useful for cyst aspiration and axillary node assessment.
- **Vacuum-assisted biopsy:** For microcalcifications requiring larger tissue volumes.

**Staging (TNM):**
- T1: ≤2 cm; T2: 2–5 cm; T3: >5 cm; T4: chest wall/skin involvement
- N0: no nodes; N1: mobile axillary nodes; N2: fixed axillary nodes; N3: infraclavicular/supraclavicular/internal mammary
- M0/M1: no distant metastases / distant metastases

**Staging investigations for invasive cancer:** CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (for Stage II+ and any symptoms), bone scan (for skeletal symptoms or Stage III+), PET-CT for specific indications.

## Management

### Surgical Principles

**Wide local excision (WLE) + sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB):** The standard for unifocal, early-stage invasive cancer. WLE = excision with 2 mm radial clear margins. SLNB maps the first-draining axillary lymph node(s) using radioisotope (Tc-99m nanocolloid) ± blue dye injection → the "hot and blue" sentinel nodes are removed; if negative = no further axillary surgery; if positive = ALND or axillary radiotherapy (ACOSOG Z0011 trial).

**Mastectomy:** Indications — multifocal disease, large tumour relative to breast size, patient preference, locally advanced, recurrence after prior WLE + radiotherapy, BRCA mutation (prophylactic). Mastectomy ± immediate reconstruction (implant, LD flap, TRAM/DIEP free flap).

**Axillary lymph node dissection (ALND):** Reserved for patients with extensive nodal disease (>2 positive sentinel nodes, clinical N2/N3). Associated with lymphoedema of the arm (~20%).

### Oncological Treatment

**Radiotherapy:**
- **Adjuvant whole breast radiotherapy** after WLE: mandatory — reduces local recurrence from ~25% to ~7% (Oxford meta-analysis). 3–5 weeks (40 Gy/15 fractions — standard UK hypofractionation schedule; or 26 Gy/5 fractions ultra-hypofractionation, comparable outcomes, FAST-Forward trial).
- Boost dose to tumour bed (10 Gy/5 fractions) for young women and high-grade tumours.
- Post-mastectomy radiotherapy for T3/T4 tumours, ≥4 positive nodes.

**Endocrine therapy (ER+ tumours):**
- **Premenopausal women:** Tamoxifen 20 mg daily for **10 years** (ATLAS trial: 10 years significantly reduces late recurrence vs 5 years). Ovarian suppression (GnRH agonists: goserelin) + aromatase inhibitor for high-risk premenopausal women.
- **Postmenopausal women:** Aromatase inhibitor (anastrozole, letrozole, exemestane) for 5–10 years — block peripheral conversion of androgens to oestrogens; more effective than tamoxifen in postmenopausal women; risk of osteoporosis (bone mineral density monitoring, bisphosphonates).
- **CDK4/6 inhibitors** (ribociclib, palbociclib, abemaciclib): combined with aromatase inhibitors for hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer; dramatically improved PFS (MONARCH, MONALEESA, PALOMA trials).

**Anti-HER2 therapy:**
- **Trastuzumab (Herceptin):** Monoclonal antibody against HER2 extracellular domain; 1 year of adjuvant trastuzumab reduced breast cancer mortality by 30% (HERA trial). Cardiac monitoring required (causes cardiomyopathy in ~2–4%). Combined with chemotherapy.
- **Pertuzumab:** Binds a different HER2 domain; used with trastuzumab for HER2+ disease (dual HER2 blockade).
- **T-DM1 (ado-trastuzumab emtansine):** Antibody-drug conjugate — trastuzumab linked to emtansine (microtubule inhibitor); used for residual disease after neoadjuvant therapy (KATHERINE trial).

**Chemotherapy:**
- Indicated for: triple-negative cancer, HER2+ cancer, high-risk ER+ tumours (Oncotype DX or Prosigna score >25), nodal involvement
- Regimens: AC-T (doxorubicin + cyclophosphamide × 4, then docetaxel × 4) or TC/EC-T. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (before surgery) allows assessment of pathological complete response and downsizing for breast conservation.

**PARP inhibitors:** Olaparib or talazoparib for germline BRCA-mutated HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer (OlympiAD, EMBRACA trials). Now also for high-risk early breast cancer with BRCA mutation (OlympiA trial: olaparib 1 year adjuvant after neoadjuvant chemotherapy).

## Complications

- **Surgery:** Seroma (most common after mastectomy/ALND — aspirate if symptomatic), wound infection, haematoma, lymphoedema (~5% after SLNB, ~20% after ALND), shoulder stiffness
- **Radiotherapy:** Fatigue, radiation dermatitis (acute), radiation fibrosis, lymphoedema, brachial plexopathy (rare), secondary malignancy (rare)
- **Tamoxifen:** Endometrial cancer (2–3× relative risk — postmenopausal bleeding requires urgent assessment), thromboembolic events, menopausal symptoms (hot flushes, vaginal dryness)
- **Aromatase inhibitors:** Osteoporosis, arthralgia/arthritis (30% — often dose-limiting), menopausal symptoms
- **Trastuzumab:** Cardiomyopathy (reversible in most cases — monitor LVEF every 3 months), infusion reactions
- **Chemotherapy:** Nausea, alopecia, neuropathy, myelosuppression, febrile neutropenia, amenorrhoea/premature menopause

## Clinical Insight

Triple assessment is a quality standard, not a formality. The critical rule is: if any component is discordant, investigate further. A lump that feels clinically benign but is ultrasound-indeterminate requires biopsy; a lump that is sonographically suspicious but has a benign core biopsy requires repeat biopsy or MDT discussion. The most dangerous error is premature reassurance — a false-negative core biopsy in a woman with a clinically and radiologically suspicious mass. The pathologist is sampling a small core; the entire mass is not analysed.

Inflammatory breast cancer is the trap because it looks like mastitis. A young woman with a red, oedematous breast who doesn't improve with antibiotics within 1–2 weeks must have urgent imaging and biopsy. The diagnosis is frequently delayed because clinicians (and patients) assume the response to antibiotics means it's infection. Inflammatory breast cancer doesn't respond to antibiotics. Any breast "infection" that fails to resolve promptly is inflammatory breast cancer until proven otherwise.

Genetic testing and risk reduction: a BRCA1/2 mutation is a family diagnosis, not just an individual one. Cascade testing of first-degree relatives is standard. Options for BRCA carriers include enhanced surveillance (annual MRI from age 30), chemoprevention (tamoxifen or raloxifene reduces risk by ~50%), and risk-reducing surgery (bilateral risk-reducing mastectomy reduces risk by ~90–95%; risk-reducing bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy reduces ovarian cancer risk by ~95% and breast cancer risk by 50% if done before menopause).

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 48-year-old woman, 2.5 cm hard irregular right breast mass, upper outer quadrant, skin tethering, two palpable axillary lymph nodes. Mammogram: spiculated mass + microcalcifications. Biopsy: invasive ductal carcinoma, ER+, HER2−.

**Step 1 — Stage the disease:**
- Clinical stage: T2 (2.5 cm) N1 (palpable ipsilateral axillary nodes) M? — staging CT chest/abdomen/pelvis needed
- ER+/HER2− = Luminal A or B — endocrine therapy will be core treatment

**Step 2 — MDT discussion (standard for all new breast cancer diagnoses):**
- Oncology, surgery, radiology, pathology, clinical nurse specialist, palliative care
- Core biopsy ER/PR/HER2 receptors and grade reported
- Ki-67 to classify Luminal A vs B
- Oncotype DX (Recurrence Score) for ER+/HER2−/node-negative disease to guide chemotherapy decision

**Step 3 — Surgical planning:**
- 2.5 cm tumour in a 48-year-old: wide local excision + SLNB is appropriate if tumour-to-breast ratio allows breast conservation
- Two clinically palpable axillary nodes: USS-guided core biopsy of axillary nodes — if positive, proceed to ALND (or axillary radiotherapy depending on number of positive nodes)
- Pre-operative MRI to exclude multifocal disease and assess extent

**Step 4 — Adjuvant treatment:**
- **Radiotherapy:** Whole breast radiotherapy post-WLE (mandatory for breast conservation)
- **Endocrine therapy:** ER+ → tamoxifen 20 mg daily for 10 years (pre/peri-menopausal at 48; check menopausal status — FSH/LH/oestradiol); if postmenopausal → aromatase inhibitor
- **Chemotherapy:** Depends on Oncotype DX score, nodal status, grade. If nodes positive → likely 4× AC then 4× docetaxel. Discuss with MDT.
- No trastuzumab (HER2-negative)

**Step 5 — Follow-up:**
- Annual mammogram for 5 years (ipsilateral and contralateral)
- Bone mineral density if starting aromatase inhibitor
- BRCA genetic testing should be offered given age <50 (threshold varies by centre — discuss with clinical genetics)

**Exam trigger:** *48-year-old + hard irregular breast lump + skin tethering + axillary nodes + spiculated mass on mammogram + core biopsy positive* → invasive ductal carcinoma requiring triple assessment confirmation + staging + MDT. The distractor is reassurance on the basis of no family history — 90% of breast cancers are sporadic, not hereditary; family history is not required for a malignant diagnosis.
