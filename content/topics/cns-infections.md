# CNS Infections

## Clinical Scenario

A 19-year-old university student is brought in by ambulance with a 12-hour history of severe headache, high fever, and vomiting, with progressive confusion over the past three hours. His flatmate reports he was entirely well yesterday. Temperature 39.8°C, HR 128 bpm, BP 94/58 mmHg, GCS 11. He has marked neck stiffness, a positive Kernig's sign, and a rapidly spreading non-blanching purpuric rash over his trunk and lower limbs. This is meningococcal septicaemia until proven otherwise.

## Overview

CNS infections encompass bacterial meningitis, viral meningitis/encephalitis, and cerebral abscess — conditions that range from self-limiting to rapidly fatal. The key clinical challenge is early recognition, because delays in antibiotic administration in bacterial meningitis are directly correlated with mortality and permanent neurological morbidity. A non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash in a febrile patient with neurological features is meningococcal disease until proven otherwise — treat before any other investigation.

## Bacterial Meningitis

### Pathophysiology

Bacterial meningitis is infection of the pia and arachnoid mater and the CSF within the subarachnoid space. Organisms reach the meninges through haematogenous spread (most common), direct invasion from adjacent structures (sinusitis, otitis media, mastoiditis), or following neurosurgical procedures or skull base fractures.

Bacteria trigger a massive inflammatory response in the subarachnoid space — cytokine release, neutrophil influx, and breakdown of the blood-brain barrier. This produces cerebral oedema (both vasogenic from BBB disruption and cytotoxic from cellular injury), raised ICP, and vasculitis of meningeal vessels (causing ischaemia and infarction). The combination of raised ICP, vasculitis, and inflammatory injury explains the high rates of sensorineural hearing loss, focal neurological deficits, and cognitive impairment in survivors.

**Organisms by age and context:**

| Setting | Organism |
|---------|---------|
| Neonates (<3 months) | *Group B Streptococcus*, *E. coli*, *Listeria monocytogenes* |
| Children and young adults | *Neisseria meningitidis* (most common in this group), *Streptococcus pneumoniae* |
| Adults | *S. pneumoniae* (most common overall in adults), *N. meningitidis* |
| Elderly, immunocompromised, pregnancy | *Listeria monocytogenes* — add ampicillin |
| Post-neurosurgery / penetrating trauma | *Staphylococci*, Gram-negatives |

### Clinical Presentation

The **classic triad** of bacterial meningitis — headache, fever, and neck stiffness — is present in only ~45% of patients. The absence of one element does not exclude the diagnosis.

**Symptoms**: Severe headache (often the worst ever), fever, neck stiffness, photophobia, phonophobia, nausea, vomiting, altered consciousness.

**Signs**:
- **Neck stiffness** (meningism) — resistance to passive neck flexion from meningeal irritation; the examiner's hands should encounter genuine rigidity, not just pain
- **Kernig's sign** — inability to extend the knee when the hip is flexed to 90° (hamstring spasm from meningeal irritation of lumbar nerve roots)
- **Brudzinski's sign** — passive neck flexion causes involuntary flexion of the hips and knees
- **Jolt accentuation** — worsening of headache with horizontal head rotation (2–3 Hz); sensitive (~97%) but non-specific
- **Papilloedema** — suggests raised ICP; if present, CT before LP is mandatory
- **Non-blanching rash** — petechiae or purpura; indicates meningococcal septicaemia with DIC; a haemorrhagic, stellate, non-blanching rash spreading rapidly is a medical emergency

**The glass test**: Press a glass tumbler firmly against the rash. If it does not blanch (disappear) under pressure, it is petechial/purpuric — consistent with meningococcal disease.

### Diagnosis

**Do not delay antibiotics for any investigation when bacterial meningitis is clinically suspected.** This principle is absolute.

**LP is the gold standard** for diagnosing bacterial meningitis. Perform only after:
- Excluding contraindications to LP: coagulopathy, anticoagulation, thrombocytopaenia, local skin infection, raised ICP (papilloedema, focal neurological deficit, GCS ≤12, seizure)
- If contraindications exist: CT head first, then LP if safe

**CSF interpretation:**

| Parameter | Normal | Bacterial | Viral | TB/Fungal |
|-----------|--------|---------|-------|----------|
| Appearance | Clear | Turbid/cloudy | Clear | Clear/turbid |
| White cells | <5/mm³ | Thousands (neutrophils) | Hundreds (lymphocytes) | Hundreds (lymphocytes) |
| Protein | <0.45 g/L | Very high (>1 g/L) | Mildly elevated | Elevated |
| Glucose | >2/3 serum | Very low (<2 mmol/L or <50% serum) | Normal/mildly low | Low |
| Gram stain | Negative | Positive in ~60% | Negative | AFB in TB |
| Culture | Negative | Positive (if no prior antibiotics) | Negative | Weeks for TB |

Blood cultures (2 sets) before antibiotics whenever possible without delaying treatment.

### Management

**Antibiotics must be given immediately.** If meningococcal disease is suspected in the community or pre-hospital, give **IM/IV benzylpenicillin immediately** and transfer.

**In hospital (NICE NG240):**
- **IV ceftriaxone 2g BD** — first-line for bacterial meningitis in adults
- Add **IV ampicillin 2g QDS** if Listeria is possible (age >55, immunocompromised, pregnancy)
- Add **IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg TDS** empirically until herpes encephalitis excluded (high-dose — 10 mg/kg, not the herpes simplex labialis dose)

**Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg QDS for 4 days** — start with or before first antibiotic dose. Reduces mortality and neurological sequelae (particularly hearing loss) in pneumococcal meningitis. Do not delay antibiotics to give dexamethasone first — give simultaneously.

**Supportive care**: ICU admission if GCS <12 or haemodynamically unstable. Fluid resuscitation for septicaemia. Seizure management. VTE prophylaxis when stable. Hearing test at discharge.

**Chemoprophylaxis**: Close contacts of meningococcal meningitis receive rifampicin (or ciprofloxacin single dose / ceftriaxone IM) to eradicate nasopharyngeal carriage.

## Viral Encephalitis

Inflammation of the brain parenchyma itself (vs meningitis = inflammation of the meninges). Distinguished by **altered consciousness, personality change, or focal neurological deficits** in addition to the headache and fever of meningitis.

**Herpes simplex encephalitis (HSE)**: Most common cause of sporadic encephalitis in developed world. HSV-1 causes temporal lobe encephalitis via retrograde spread along the trigeminal nerve. Presents with fever, headache, confusion, behavioural change (personality change, aggression, hypersexuality — temporal/frontal involvement), seizures (often focal temporal), and amnesia.

MRI: T2/FLAIR signal change in the temporal lobes, insular cortex, and cingulate gyrus — highly characteristic. EEG: periodic lateralising epileptiform discharges (PLEDs) over the temporal region.

**LP**: CSF shows lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, normal glucose, and RBCs (haemorrhagic nature of HSV encephalitis). **PCR for HSV DNA is diagnostic** — high sensitivity and specificity; results within 24–48 hours.

**Treatment**: IV aciclovir 10 mg/kg TDS for 14–21 days (must be renal-dose adjusted). Start empirically if encephalitis is suspected — do not wait for PCR result. High-dose aciclovir dramatically reduces mortality from ~70% untreated to ~20%.

**Other viral causes**: VZV encephalitis (reactivation in immunocompromised), EBV, CMV (in immunocompromised), enterovirus (children), Japanese encephalitis (travel), West Nile virus. Treat most with supportive care; specific antivirals for VZV and CMV.

**Autoimmune encephalitis**: Increasingly recognised. Anti-NMDAR encephalitis (most common — young women, psychiatric prodrome, movement disorders, autonomic instability, associated with ovarian teratoma) and LGI1, CASPR2, and other antibody-mediated encephalitides. CSF may show normal or mild lymphocytosis. Serum and CSF antibody testing. Treatment: immunosuppression (steroids, IVIG, rituximab), tumour removal.

## Cerebral Abscess

Collection of pus within the brain parenchyma, typically arising from direct spread from adjacent infection (sinusitis, otitis media, mastoiditis, dental infection), haematogenous spread (infective endocarditis, lung abscess), or penetrating trauma/neurosurgery. Cryptogenic in ~30%.

**Organisms**: Mixed flora (streptococci, anaerobes) from sinusitis; *Staphylococci* from endocarditis or trauma; *Toxoplasma gondii* and *Cryptococcus* in HIV (CD4 <200).

**Presentation**: Headache (most common), fever (only 50% — absence does not exclude), focal neurological deficits, seizures, raised ICP. The triad of fever + headache + focal deficit suggests cerebral abscess.

**Diagnosis**: CT or MRI with contrast — ring-enhancing lesion with central hypodensity and surrounding oedema. **MRI with DWI** differentiates abscess (restricted diffusion — bright DWI, dark ADC) from tumour or metastasis (no restricted diffusion).

**Management**: IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + metronidazole for community-acquired; add vancomycin for post-neurosurgical or traumatic) — 4–8 weeks. Surgical aspiration or excision for lesions >2.5 cm, accessible location, or deteriorating neurology. Dexamethasone for oedema with mass effect. Monitor for herniation.

## Clinical Insight

Give antibiotics before CT. Bacterial meningitis kills within hours — the organism load in CSF halves within 15 minutes of the first dose of ceftriaxone, but this is vastly outweighed by the benefit of treatment. The risk of herniation from LP in meningitis without raised ICP is extremely low. The risk of herniation from untreated meningitis is high. When LP is genuinely contraindicated, take blood cultures and CT — but do not delay antibiotics waiting for either.

Herpes encephalitis mimics acute psychiatric disease in its early stages. Personality change, aggression, bizarre behaviour, and disinhibition in a febrile patient with altered consciousness should prompt immediate LP and empirical IV aciclovir. By the time MRI shows classic temporal lobe changes, significant irreversible damage has usually occurred. The drug is safe, cheap, and renal-dose adjustable — the risk of withholding it while awaiting confirmation far exceeds the risk of giving it.

Anti-NMDAR encephalitis is now more common than herpes encephalitis as a cause of encephalitis in young women. The psychiatric prodrome is so prominent — psychosis, catatonia, emotional lability — that patients are frequently admitted to psychiatric wards before the neurological features (movement disorders, autonomic instability, reduced consciousness) become apparent. Send anti-NMDAR antibodies in the serum and CSF of any young woman with a new psychiatric presentation combined with any neurological feature.
