## Overview

Head and neck cancers encompass malignancies of the oral cavity, pharynx (nasopharynx, oropharynx, hypopharynx), larynx, salivary glands, thyroid, and paranasal sinuses. They represent approximately 3% of all UK cancers. Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) accounts for over 90% of head and neck cancers — the remainder include adenocarcinoma (salivary glands), lymphoma, melanoma, and sarcoma. There are two distinct epidemiological trends: the traditional tobacco-and-alcohol-related SCC (declining in high-income countries with falling smoking rates) and the rising epidemic of HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC, particularly in younger non-smoking adults. HPV-related oropharyngeal SCC has a substantially better prognosis than HPV-negative disease, which has transformed treatment paradigms.

## Pathophysiology

### HPV-Related Oropharyngeal SCC

**Human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly high-risk HPV-16, drives a distinct subset of oropharyngeal SCC:**
- HPV infects tonsillar crypt epithelium → persistent infection → viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 inactivate p53 and Rb tumour suppressors respectively → uncontrolled cell proliferation → SCC
- Transmission: oral-genital sexual contact; rising incidence parallels changing sexual practices
- Typically arises in the **tonsils and base of tongue** (lingual tonsil) — tonsillar crypts have invaginated epithelium particularly susceptible to HPV transformation
- Demographic: younger (40–60s), male, non-smoker, often presents with **occult primary + cervical lymphadenopathy** (primary tumour in tonsillar crypt may be very small yet nodes are large)
- **Prognosis: significantly better than HPV-negative** — 5-year OS ~80% vs 40–50% for HPV-negative; HPV-negative disease: p16 IHC negative, smoking/alcohol-related

**p16 IHC:** Surrogate marker for HPV-driven carcinogenesis (E7 inactivates Rb → loss of Rb feedback → p16 overexpression); positive p16 = HPV-related disease in oropharyngeal cancer context.

### Traditional Tobacco/Alcohol SCC

**Synergistic carcinogenesis:** Tobacco (smoke and smokeless) + alcohol act synergistically — tobacco introduces DNA-adduct-forming carcinogens (benzopyrene, nitrosamines); alcohol acts as solvent enhancing mucosal permeability + is metabolised to acetaldehyde (DNA mutagen) + impairs DNA repair. Risk is multiplicative, not additive.

**Field cancerisation:** The entire upper aerodigestive tract mucosa has been exposed to the carcinogen → multiple pre-malignant and malignant foci can develop simultaneously. Explains the 3–5% annual risk of second primary tumours in head and neck cancer survivors.

**Pre-malignant lesions:**
- **Leukoplakia** (white patch not scraped off — cannot be attributed to other cause): 5–17% malignant transformation; requires biopsy; high-risk features: non-homogeneous (speckled/nodular), floor of mouth location, dysplasia on histology
- **Erythroplakia** (red patch): Much higher malignant transformation rate (~50%) — dysplasia or frank SCC in most cases; always biopsy
- **Oral submucous fibrosis:** Betel nut chewing → submucosal fibrosis → trismus; high malignant potential

### Laryngeal Cancer

SCC of the larynx — glottic (vocal cord, most common, presents early with hoarseness), supraglottic (epiglottis, arytenoids — presents late with dysphagia), subglottic (rare). Smoking is the predominant risk factor. Glottic cancer has the best prognosis because the vocal cords have sparse lymphatics — nodal spread is late.

**Recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) involvement:** Hoarseness from tumour infiltration of the RLN is an ominous sign of advanced disease (T4) — fixed vocal cord on laryngoscopy.

## Clinical Presentation

### The Neck Lump — Differential Diagnosis

The classic "rule of 80s" for neck lumps in adults:
- 80% of neck lumps in adults >40 are neoplastic
- 80% of neoplastic neck lumps are malignant
- 80% of malignant neck lumps are metastatic (from UADT SCC or thyroid)
- 20% are primary lymphoma

**Neck lump differential by characteristics:**

| Character | Likely Diagnosis |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Young (<30), soft, fluctuant, anterior triangle | Reactive lymphadenopathy |
| Young, lateral neck, cystic, anterior to SCM | Branchial cyst (or HPV+ SCC met — radiological overlap) |
| Midline, moves on swallowing and tongue protrusion | Thyroglossal cyst |
| Midline, moves on swallowing (not tongue protrusion) | Thyroid nodule/goitre |
| Firm, non-tender, fixed, >40 years | Metastatic SCC until proven otherwise |
| Multiple, rubbery, generalised | Lymphoma |
| Pulsatile | Carotid body tumour (paraganglioma) |
| Posterior triangle, child, fever | Lymphadenopathy (viral — EBV, CMV) |

### Symptoms by Primary Site

| Site | Key Symptoms |
|------|-------------|
| Oral cavity (tongue, floor of mouth, buccal) | Ulcer/lump in mouth; pain on eating; referred otalgia (via lingual/inferior alveolar nerve → auriculotemporal = CN V₃) |
| Oropharynx (tonsil, soft palate, base of tongue) | Unilateral tonsillar enlargement; dysphagia; unilateral otalgia (via CN IX → Jacobson's nerve → tympanic plexus); neck lump (often first presentation in HPV+ disease) |
| Hypopharynx (pyriform fossa, postcricoid) | Progressive dysphagia + weight loss; referred otalgia; postcricoid carcinoma associated with Plummer-Vinson syndrome (iron deficiency, esophageal webs, female) |
| Larynx (glottic) | **Hoarseness >3 weeks** → 2-week wait referral |
| Larynx (supraglottic) | Dysphagia, odynophagia, muffled voice ("hot potato voice"), neck lump |
| Nasopharynx | Unilateral serous otitis media (Eustachian tube obstruction by tumour — always check nasopharynx in adult with new unilateral glue ear); epistaxis; neck mass (level V cervical nodes); CN palsies (skull base invasion — III, IV, V, VI) |
| Salivary gland | Painless progressive parotid/submandibular/sublingual mass; facial palsy (CN VII in parotid = malignancy until proven) |

**Referred otalgia:** Pain referred to the ear from the oropharynx/larynx via cranial nerves (CN IX, X, V₃). Ear pain in an adult with a normal ear → examine the oropharynx, hypopharynx, and larynx. This is a classic diagnostic teaching point.

## Diagnosis

### Investigations — Head and Neck Cancer Pathway

**All patients with suspected head and neck cancer → 2-week wait (2WW) referral to head and neck clinic:**
- Unexplained neck lump persisting >3 weeks in adult
- Hoarseness >3 weeks (laryngeal cancer)
- Unexplained oral ulcer/lump >3 weeks
- Unexplained oral or pharyngeal pain persisting >3 weeks
- Dysphagia

**Clinical examination:** Oral cavity inspection + bimanual palpation of floor of mouth; indirect laryngoscopy or flexible nasolaryngoscopy (FNL) — visual inspection of larynx, hypopharynx, nasopharynx, base of tongue; neck palpation with lymph node mapping.

**Flexible nasolaryngoscopy (FNL):** In-clinic; passes through nostril to visualise larynx and pharynx; identifies laryngeal lesions, vocal cord paralysis, nasopharyngeal tumours, base of tongue lesions; dynamic assessment of vocal cord movement.

**Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) ± core biopsy of neck node:** First investigation for neck lump — FNA is diagnostic in most cases. p16 IHC staining on the biopsy specimen identifies HPV-related disease. Do NOT perform excision biopsy of a neck node before excluding a primary SCC (disrupts lymphatic drainage, complicates subsequent surgery/radiotherapy).

**MRI neck:** Best soft tissue detail for primary tumour extent; identifies perineural spread; base of tongue and tonsil lesions better on MRI.

**CT chest/abdomen/pelvis:** Staging for distant metastases (lung most common) and second primary tumours.

**PET-CT:** Superior to CT alone for detecting occult primary (HPV+ neck node with no visible primary on endoscopy), regional and distant metastases. Standard staging investigation for stage III–IV disease.

**Panendoscopy (examination under anaesthetic — EUA):** Direct laryngoscopy, pharyngoscopy, oesophagoscopy, bronchoscopy under GA → systematic inspection of entire aerodigestive tract + biopsy of any suspicious lesion + identification of synchronous second primary.

**HPV testing (p16 IHC + HPV ISH):** Mandatory for all oropharyngeal SCC — determines prognosis and guides treatment de-intensification trials.

## Management

### Multidisciplinary Team (MDT)

All head and neck cancers must be discussed at a specialist head and neck MDT involving: ENT head and neck surgeon, clinical oncologist, radiologist, pathologist, speech and language therapist, dietitian, specialist nurse, and maxillofacial surgeon.

### Early Stage Disease (T1–T2, N0–N1)

**Oral cavity SCC:** Surgery is preferred — wide local excision with clear margins (5 mm); reconstruction (primary closure, local flap, free flap); elective neck dissection (selective neck dissection levels I–III) for risk of occult nodal disease (>15–20%).

**Laryngeal SCC (glottic T1–T2):** Transoral laser microsurgery (TLM) or external beam radiotherapy — equivalent oncological outcomes; choice depends on functional outcomes and patient preference. Voice quality may be better preserved with TLM for T1 lesions.

**Oropharyngeal SCC (HPV+):** Radiation-sensitive tumour — primary chemoradiotherapy (CRT) or surgery (transoral robotic surgery — TORS) for T1–T2 tonsil/base of tongue; clinical trials investigating treatment de-intensification for HPV+ disease (PATHOS, ORATOR trials).

### Advanced Stage Disease (T3–T4 or N2–N3)

**Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT):** Cisplatin-based chemotherapy + radical radiotherapy — standard of care for most stage III–IV oropharyngeal, hypopharyngeal, and unresectable laryngeal cancers. Cisplatin sensitises tumour cells to radiation.

**Radical surgery + post-op radiotherapy ± chemotherapy:**
- **Laryngectomy:** Total laryngectomy for T4 laryngeal cancer or failed larynx-preservation protocols; permanent tracheostomy; voice rehabilitation (tracheo-oesophageal voice prosthesis — most effective); swallowing rehabilitation
- **Composite resection + free flap reconstruction:** For oral cavity/oropharyngeal tumours with bone involvement; mandibular reconstruction (fibula free flap)
- **Radical neck dissection (RND):** Removal of all 5 levels of cervical lymph nodes ± SCM, IJV, CN XI; modified (spares CN XI, IJV, and/or SCM); functional/selective dissection for N0 neck

**Biological therapy:**
- **Cetuximab (anti-EGFR):** Added to radiotherapy in patients who cannot tolerate cisplatin
- **Pembrolizumab (anti-PD-1):** First-line for recurrent/metastatic HNSCC (KEYNOTE-048 trial) — particularly effective in PD-L1-positive tumours

### Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma

- Associated with EBV (especially non-keratinising and undifferentiated subtypes); endemic in Southeast Asia and North Africa
- Highly radiosensitive — primary treatment is CRT; surgery rarely indicated for primary
- Plasma EBV DNA: diagnostic marker and treatment response monitoring

## Complications

- **Post-treatment dysphagia and xerostomia:** Major late effects of head and neck radiotherapy; salivary glands in radiation field → xerostomia (dry mouth) → dental caries, swallowing difficulty, nutritional compromise; IMRT (intensity-modulated radiotherapy) spares salivary glands
- **Osteoradionecrosis (ORN):** Radiation-induced devascularisation of mandible → bone death → chronic pain, fistula; prevent with dental extraction before radiotherapy; treat with hyperbaric oxygen + surgery
- **Lymphoedema:** Facial/neck lymphoedema after surgery/radiotherapy; manual lymphatic drainage
- **Voice and swallowing dysfunction:** SLT essential pre- and post-treatment; PEG/RIG feeding for nutritional support during and after treatment
- **Hypothyroidism:** After neck radiotherapy (thyroid in field); monitor TFTs annually
- **Recurrence:** Second primary cancers in 3–5% per year due to field cancerisation; annual clinical surveillance + imaging

## Clinical Insight

The HPV epidemic in oropharyngeal cancer represents one of the most dramatic epidemiological shifts in oncology in the past 20 years. In 1990, oropharyngeal SCC was predominantly a disease of older male smokers, with a 5-year survival of ~40%. Today, approximately 80% of oropharyngeal SCCs in the UK are HPV-positive, many occurring in never-smokers in their 40s and 50s, and the 5-year survival for HPV-positive disease is ~80%. HPV vaccination (Gardasil 9 covering HPV-16 and 18) in adolescents has the potential to prevent the majority of HPV-related oropharyngeal cancers — the UK now vaccinates both boys and girls. This represents one of the clearest examples of cancer prevention through vaccination.

Referred otalgia is among the most diagnostic misleading symptoms in ENT. A patient who presents with ear pain but has a normal ear on examination — normal tympanic membrane, normal otoscopy, no signs of OE — has referred otalgia from the oropharynx or larynx until proven otherwise. The neural pathway is via CN IX (glossopharyngeal) from the tonsils/pharynx to Jacobson's nerve (tympanic branch) → tympanic plexus → perceived as ear pain. This symptom pattern in an adult warrants same-day flexible nasolaryngoscopy. Throat cancer diagnosed at the stage of referred otalgia in the absence of visible oral lesions is often T2 or early T3 — still potentially curable.

The "never do an excision biopsy of a neck node before excluding a primary UADT lesion" rule is fundamental to head and neck oncology. An excision biopsy of a metastatic neck node disrupts the lymphatic anatomy, creates scar tissue, and significantly complicates subsequent neck dissection and radiotherapy planning. The correct approach is always: FNA first → PET-CT to find primary → panendoscopy under GA → directed biopsies of likely primary sites (base of tongue, tonsil, pyriform fossa). Only if all of these fail to find a primary does management of a "neck unknown primary" proceed.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 55M, 6-week painless firm fixed 3 cm right neck lump, non-smoker, 15 units alcohol/week, normal oral cavity, HPV positive on nasopharyngeal swab.

**Diagnosis: HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC with cervical lymph node metastasis — occult primary most likely in right tonsil or base of tongue**

**Step 1 — Immediate 2WW pathway:**
- Firm fixed neck node >3 weeks in adult + HPV+ → metastatic SCC highly likely
- **Urgent 2-week wait referral to head and neck service**

**Step 2 — Investigations in head and neck clinic:**
- **FNL (flexible nasolaryngoscopy):** Inspect tonsils, base of tongue, pyriform fossae, nasopharynx, larynx — look for primary lesion; may be invisible in tonsillar crypt
- **FNA of neck node:** Confirms SCC; p16 IHC → HPV-positive disease
- **MRI neck + PET-CT:** Best modality for identifying occult tonsillar/BOT primary + staging nodal disease + distant metastases
- **EUA + panendoscopy + systematic biopsies** (tonsil biopsies bilaterally, BOT biopsy, nasopharyngeal biopsies, directed biopsies of any PET-avid site) to locate primary

**Step 3 — Staging:**
- HPV+ cN2b M0 (3 cm node, ipsilateral) = **Stage I** under AJCC 8th edition (HPV-positive oropharyngeal cancer has separate, more favourable staging system)
- Stage I HPV+ oropharyngeal cancer: 5-year OS ~85–90%

**Step 4 — MDT discussion:**
- If primary identified (tonsil): TORS (transoral robotic surgery) tonsillectomy + neck dissection OR primary CRT
- HPV+ disease: consider participation in de-intensification clinical trial (PATHOS — reduced dose radiotherapy post-TORS)
- Dental assessment + extractions before radiotherapy

**Step 5 — Patient counselling:**
- Excellent prognosis for HPV+ disease — critical message (many patients assume metastatic cancer = terminal)
- Treatment aim: **curative intent**
- Discuss sexual partner implications of HPV — HPV vaccination discussion

**Exam trigger:** *Firm painless neck node in adult + HPV positive* → HPV-positive oropharyngeal SCC with occult primary; FNA → p16 IHC; PET-CT + panendoscopy to find primary. The distractor is reassuring the patient that a "reactive node" will resolve — any firm, non-tender, fixed node >3 weeks in an adult ≥40 requires urgent ENT referral.
