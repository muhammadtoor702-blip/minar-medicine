## Overview

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common cause of preventable blindness in working-age adults in high-income countries, and the leading cause of new blindness registration in people aged 16–64 in the UK. It is a microvascular complication of diabetes, present in approximately 25% of all people with diabetes in the UK. The duration of diabetes and degree of glycaemic control are the strongest determinants of DR development and progression — after 20 years of type 1 diabetes, nearly 100% have some DR. The crucial insight is that DR is largely asymptomatic until at an advanced stage — systematic screening is the only way to detect it before significant visual loss occurs. The NHS Diabetic Eye Screening Programme (DESP) invites all people with diabetes aged ≥12 for annual retinal photography, and has been instrumental in reducing blindness from DR.

## Pathophysiology

### Microvascular Mechanisms

Chronic hyperglycaemia triggers multiple parallel pathways:

**1. Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs):** Glucose non-enzymatically glycates proteins → AGE accumulation in vessel walls → basement membrane thickening → endothelial dysfunction.

**2. Protein kinase C (PKC) activation:** Hyperglycaemia → excess diacylglycerol → PKC-β activation → VEGF upregulation → increased vascular permeability and neovascularisation.

**3. Polyol pathway:** Aldose reductase converts excess glucose to sorbitol → osmotic stress + reduced NADPH (antioxidant function) → oxidative stress.

**4. Pericyte loss:** Retinal capillary pericytes are the first cells damaged → loss of autoregulatory tone → capillary dilation → microaneurysm formation → outpouching of capillary wall. Microaneurysms are the earliest fundoscopic sign of DR.

**Progression:**
- Pericyte loss → microaneurysm → vascular leakage (hard exudates — lipid deposits; retinal oedema) → ischaemia from vessel occlusion → cotton wool spots (nerve fibre layer infarcts) → VEGF upregulation (hypoxic retina releases VEGF) → neovascularisation (proliferative DR) → fibrovascular proliferation → vitreous haemorrhage → tractional retinal detachment.

**Diabetic macular oedema (DMO):** Breakdown of inner blood-retinal barrier → fluid accumulates in the macula (the area of highest visual acuity) → macular thickening → visual loss. The leading cause of vision impairment in DR. Can occur at any stage of DR.

## Clinical Presentation

### Classification of Diabetic Retinopathy

**NHS Diabetic Eye Screening Programme (DESP) Grading:**

| Grade | Features | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **R0** (No DR) | Normal | Annual DESP |
| **R1** (Background DR) | Microaneurysms, dot/blot haemorrhages, hard exudates | Annual DESP |
| **R2** (Pre-proliferative DR) | Cotton wool spots (≥4), venous beading/looping, IRMA (intraretinal microvascular abnormalities), multiple blot haemorrhages (≥4 quadrants) | Refer to ophthalmology (urgent: 4 weeks) |
| **R3a** (Active proliferative DR) | New vessels on disc (NVD), new vessels elsewhere (NVE), pre-retinal/vitreous haemorrhage, fibrous tissue | Refer urgent same-day/within 1 week |
| **R3s** (Stable treated PDR) | Laser scars, inactive fibrous tissue, no active new vessels | 6-monthly DESP |
| **M0** (No maculopathy) | No macular features | — |
| **M1** (Maculopathy) | Hard exudates within 1 disc diameter of fovea; haemorrhages ±1 DD of fovea; any macular oedema on OCT | Refer ophthalmology |

**Key distinction in this patient:**
- Right eye: NVD + pre-retinal haemorrhage = **R3a (active proliferative DR)** → same-day/urgent referral
- Left eye: blot haemorrhages + hard exudates within 1 DD of fovea + cotton wool spots = **R2 + M1** → urgent referral within 4 weeks

### Symptoms — Why Screening Matters

- **Early DR (background, pre-proliferative):** Asymptomatic — no visual symptoms; cannot be relied upon by patients to self-detect
- **Proliferative DR:** Usually asymptomatic until vitreous haemorrhage (sudden floaters/dark spots/profound visual loss) or traction RD
- **DMO:** Gradual blurring, distortion of central vision (metamorphopsia) — may be subtle
- **By the time visual symptoms appear, DR is often advanced** — this is why systematic annual screening is non-negotiable

### Risk Factors for DR Progression

- **HbA1c:** Each 1% (11 mmol/mol) reduction in HbA1c reduces risk of microvascular complications by ~35% (UKPDS); this patient's HbA1c of 84 mmol/mol (10%) is very poorly controlled
- **Hypertension:** UK Prospective Diabetes Study — tight BP control (<150/85 vs <180/105) reduces DR progression by 34%; renin-angiotensin system (RAS) blockade has additional protective effects
- **Duration of diabetes**
- **Dyslipidaemia:** Elevated triglycerides and LDL → increased hard exudates
- **Nephropathy:** Associated with microalbuminuria → worsening DR
- **Pregnancy:** Can accelerate DR — pre-conception laser treatment indicated; regular ophthalmology during pregnancy

## Diagnosis

### Investigations

**Retinal photography (fundus photography):** NHS DESP screening method — non-mydriatic digital photography; images graded centrally by trained screeners and ophthalmologists; 7-field photography covers more retina.

**Slit-lamp biomicroscopy (dilated fundus examination):** Clinical examination by ophthalmologist; allows stereoscopic view of macula and optic disc; dynamic assessment.

**Optical coherence tomography (OCT):** Cross-sectional imaging of the retina — gold standard for diagnosing and monitoring DMO. Measures retinal thickness, identifies intraretinal/subretinal fluid, quantifies macular oedema. OCT angiography (OCTA) maps retinal vasculature without dye injection.

**Fluorescein angiography (FFA):** IV fluorescein dye → retinal angiography — identifies microaneurysms, areas of non-perfusion (capillary dropout = ischaemia), neovascularisation, and macular oedema leakage patterns (focal vs diffuse). Used to guide laser treatment and anti-VEGF delivery. Risks: nausea (~5%), allergic reaction (<0.1%).

**HbA1c, BP, renal function, lipids:** Systematic cardiovascular risk assessment; identify modifiable drivers of DR progression.

## Management

### Systemic Risk Factor Management — Mainstay of Prevention

**Glycaemic control:**
- Target HbA1c ≤48–53 mmol/mol (6.5–7%) — reduces risk and progression of DR
- Intensive glycaemic control in early disease dramatically reduces microvascular risk (DCCT in T1DM; UKPDS in T2DM)
- **Caution:** Rapid improvement in glycaemic control after prolonged poor control can precipitate "early worsening" of DR — a known phenomenon; gradual HbA1c improvement is safer

**Blood pressure:** Target <130/80 mmHg; ACE inhibitor or ARB first-line (additional renoprotective + DR-protective effect beyond BP reduction)

**Lipids:** Statin therapy (atorvastatin 40–80 mg) for all patients with diabetes and DR; fenofibrate reduces hard exudates and DMO progression (ACCORD-Eye trial) — used adjunctively

**Lifestyle:** Smoking cessation; weight loss; exercise

### Diabetic Macular Oedema — Anti-VEGF Treatment

**Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections — first-line for centre-involving DMO:**
- **Ranibizumab (Lucentis):** NICE-approved; RESTORE and RIDE/RISE trials — superior to laser for visual acuity improvement; monthly injections initially (typically 3–4 monthly loading), then PRN (as-needed) based on OCT/VA monitoring
- **Aflibercept (Eylea):** Fusion protein (VEGF trap); VIVID/VISTA trials — non-inferior to monthly ranibizumab; 2-monthly dosing after loading phase
- **Bevacizumab (Avastin):** Off-label but widely used (cheaper); similar efficacy
- **Faricimab (Vabysmo):** Bispecific antibody (anti-VEGF + anti-Ang2) — YOSEMITE/RHINE trials — allows extended dosing intervals (every 3–4 months in some patients)

**Mechanism:** VEGF drives vascular permeability → macular oedema; anti-VEGF blocks this → reduces fluid, improves VA. Mean VA gain of approximately 10–12 ETDRS letters (2 Snellen lines) at 1 year.

**Macular laser (ETDRS focal/grid laser):** No longer first-line; used adjunctively for focal leaking microaneurysms; does not improve VA but can stabilise; does not work for diffuse or centre-involving DMO.

**Intravitreal dexamethasone implant (Ozurdex):** For DMO unresponsive to anti-VEGF, pseudophakic eyes, or when patient cannot attend for monthly injections. Single implant lasts 4–6 months. Risks: IOP rise, cataract acceleration.

### Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy — Laser

**Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) laser:**
- **Mechanism:** Destroys peripheral ischaemic retina → reduces VEGF production from ischaemic tissue → regression of new vessels
- **Technique:** 1,200–1,600 burns applied to the mid-periphery and periphery of retina (sparing the macula)
- **Evidence:** DRS (Diabetic Retinopathy Study) and ETDRS trials — PRP reduces risk of severe visual loss from PDR by ~50%
- **Side effects:** Night blindness, reduced peripheral visual field, reduced contrast sensitivity, risk of inadvertent macular burn (visual loss); can worsen DMO (VEGF released at laser spots)

**Anti-VEGF for PDR:**
- Intravitreal anti-VEGF can cause rapid regression of new vessels
- PROTOCOL S trial (Protocol S, DRCR.net): Ranibizumab non-inferior to PRP for visual acuity outcomes at 2 years with fewer visual field losses; increasingly used as an alternative or supplement to PRP
- Practical limitation: requires persistent monthly or bimonthly injections; PRP effect is more durable

### Vitreous Haemorrhage and Tractional Retinal Detachment

**PPV (pars plana vitrectomy):**
- Removes vitreous blood (VH) → clears visual axis
- Removes fibrovascular proliferative membranes (tractional RD)
- Endolaser to all ischaemic retina at time of PPV
- **Indications:** Dense non-clearing VH (>3 months or if coexistent TRD); tractional RD involving or threatening macula

---

## Complications

- **Vitreous haemorrhage:** Sudden visual loss from PDR frond bleeding; spontaneous resolution in weeks-months; early PPV if non-clearing or TRD
- **Tractional retinal detachment:** Fibrovascular membrane contraction pulls retina off RPE; macular TRD = surgical emergency (PPV within 24h)
- **Rubeotic glaucoma (neovascular glaucoma):** Iris rubeosis (neovascularisation of iris) from ischaemic DR → angle neovascularisation → secondary angle-closure glaucoma; very painful; difficult to treat; PRP + anti-VEGF + glaucoma surgery
- **Anti-VEGF injection complications:** Endophthalmitis (0.05% per injection), RD, traumatic cataract, subconjunctival haemorrhage; sterile endophthalmitis from bevacizumab
- **PRP side effects:** Reduced visual field, night vision, contrast sensitivity; macular oedema worsening

## Clinical Insight

The most important intervention in diabetic retinopathy is not the laser or the injection — it is the glycaemic control that prevents the disease from developing in the first place. The DCCT study (type 1 diabetes) demonstrated a 76% reduction in risk of DR development with intensive vs conventional glycaemic control, and a 54% reduction in progression of existing DR. The UKPDS showed similar results for type 2 diabetes. This has led to the "legacy effect" or "metabolic memory" concept — early years of good glycaemic control provide lasting protection even if control deteriorates later, and vice versa.

The early worsening phenomenon following rapid glycaemic improvement is clinically important and under-recognised. When a patient with long-standing poor control (HbA1c 84 mmol/mol, as in this scenario) is started on insulin or GLP-1 agonists and HbA1c drops rapidly, DR can temporarily worsen — new haemorrhages, cotton wool spots, and even progression to PDR. The mechanism involves haemodynamic changes in the retinal circulation as glucose levels normalise rapidly. The implication is that in patients with known moderate-severe DR, glycaemic improvement should be gradual (target ~11 mmol/mol reduction per year), with ophthalmology review before and during treatment intensification.

Anti-VEGF injections have transformed the management of DMO and are one of the greatest therapeutic advances in ophthalmology in the past 20 years. Before ranibizumab, the only treatment for centre-involving DMO was macular laser, which at best stabilised vision but rarely improved it. Anti-VEGF agents consistently produce 2 Snellen lines of visual acuity improvement at 1 year in clinical trials, and many patients gain 3 or more lines. The burden of monthly clinic visits and injections is the main limitation — the drive toward extended dosing intervals (faricimab every 16 weeks in some patients) addresses this.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 48M, T2DM 12 years, HbA1c 84 mmol/mol (poor control), 3 years missed screening. Right: NVD + pre-retinal haemorrhage (R3a). Left: blot haemorrhages + hard exudates within 1 DD fovea + CWS (R2/M1). VA: 6/12 R, 6/18 L.

**Diagnoses:**
1. **Right eye: Active proliferative DR (R3a)** — NVD is the most urgent finding; risk of vitreous haemorrhage
2. **Left eye: Pre-proliferative DR + diabetic maculopathy (R2/M1)** — hard exudates near fovea indicate DMO

**Step 1 — Urgent ophthalmology referral:**
- Right eye R3a → **same-day or within 1 week urgent ophthalmology** (PDR)
- Left eye R2/M1 → **within 4 weeks**
- In practice: bilateral referral same day given clinical picture

**Step 2 — Right eye treatment:**
- **PRP laser** to right eye — ablates ischaemic peripheral retina, reduces VEGF, promotes regression of NVD
- Anti-VEGF as adjunct or alternative (PROTOCOL S data) — may need OCT to assess for concurrent macular oedema before PRP (PRP can worsen DMO)

**Step 3 — Left eye treatment:**
- **OCT macula** — confirm centre-involving DMO; if DMO present:
- **Intravitreal anti-VEGF (ranibizumab or aflibercept)** monthly × 3–4 loading injections → then PRN guided by OCT/VA
- Hard exudates within 1 DD of fovea may require focal laser to leaking microaneurysms adjunctively

**Step 4 — Systemic risk factor optimisation:**
- HbA1c 84 mmol/mol → gradually intensify diabetes management (avoid rapid reduction in PDR setting — early worsening risk)
- Endocrinology/diabetes specialist nurse referral urgently
- BP target <130/80 → ACE inhibitor
- Statin optimisation; fenofibrate addition for maculopathy
- Stop smoking (if applicable)

**Exam trigger:** *NVD on screening retinal photography* → active PDR, urgent same-day/week ophthalmology referral for PRP. *Hard exudates within 1 DD of fovea* → diabetic maculopathy, anti-VEGF treatment. Poor HbA1c must be addressed but improved gradually in presence of PDR.
