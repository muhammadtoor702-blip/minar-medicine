## Overview

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined by the KDIGO criteria as any of: a rise in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours; a rise to ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days; or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥6 hours. It is present in up to 15% of hospital inpatients and is independently associated with increased mortality, progression to CKD, and prolonged hospital stay. AKI is not merely a laboratory abnormality — it is a sign that the kidney's regulatory functions are failing, and the downstream consequences — hyperkalaemia, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary oedema — require urgent parallel management.

The diagnostic priority is to classify AKI as **pre-renal, intrinsic renal, or post-renal**, because each requires fundamentally different management. This classification also determines whether the injury is reversible and how quickly.

**KDIGO AKI staging:**

| Stage | Creatinine Criteria | Urine Output |
|-------|-------------------|--------------|
| 1 | Rise ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48h OR 1.5–1.9× baseline | <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6–12h |
| 2 | 2.0–2.9× baseline | <0.5 mL/kg/h for ≥12h |
| 3 | ≥3.0× baseline OR ≥4.0 mg/dL OR new RRT initiated | <0.3 mL/kg/h for ≥24h OR anuria ≥12h |

This patient's creatinine has risen from 1.1 to 3.8 mg/dL — a 3.5× rise, meeting Stage 3 criteria by both absolute value and proportional increase.

## Pathophysiology

**Pre-renal AKI** (~60% of cases): Reduced renal perfusion leads to reduced GFR. The kidneys themselves are structurally intact — they are simply underperfused. The kidney responds by maximally retaining sodium and water (low urine sodium, concentrated urine). If perfusion is restored promptly, the AKI is fully reversible. If not, sustained ischaemia damages tubular epithelium — transitioning to intrinsic AKI (acute tubular necrosis). The window for full reversibility closes with time.

**Intrinsic renal AKI**: Direct damage to the renal parenchyma. The most common cause is **acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** — from sustained ischaemia or nephrotoxins. The tubular epithelium sloughs into the lumen, forming muddy brown granular casts. Other intrinsic causes include:
- **Acute interstitial nephritis (AIN)**: Drug hypersensitivity (NSAIDs, PPIs, penicillins, rifampicin) or infection; characterised by eosinophilia, rash, and sterile pyuria; responds to stopping the drug ± prednisolone
- **Glomerulonephritis**: Presents with haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension; requires urgent immunological workup and nephrology referral
- **Vascular**: Renal artery or vein thrombosis, cholesterol emboli, HUS/TTP

**Post-renal AKI**: Obstruction to urine outflow raises collecting system pressure, impairing GFR. Must be bilateral (or unilateral in a single functioning kidney) to cause AKI. Most common cause is BPH in elderly men — prostatic obstruction causes chronic bladder outlet obstruction with eventual bilateral hydronephrosis. Relief of obstruction is followed by **post-obstructive diuresis** — large volumes of dilute urine (sometimes 5–10 L/day) as the kidney excretes accumulated solutes and fluid. Monitor electrolytes closely and avoid aggressive IV fluid replacement that perpetuates the diuresis.

**Why NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors are dangerous together in volume depletion:**

In hypovolaemia, the kidney depends on two autoregulatory mechanisms to maintain GFR: prostaglandin-mediated **afferent arteriolar dilation** (blocked by NSAIDs) and angiotensin II-mediated **efferent arteriolar constriction** (blocked by ACEi/ARBs). Removing both simultaneously — in a patient who is also volume-depleted and vomiting — produces a catastrophic fall in GFR. This is a classic "triple whammy" that explains this patient's presentation.

**Rhabdomyolysis:**

Muscle cell injury releases myoglobin into the circulation, which is freely filtered and directly toxic to tubular cells. Causes: trauma (crush injury), prolonged immobility (found on floor), seizures, extreme exertion, statin myopathy (particularly simvastatin + macrolide/azole), hyperthermia, alcohol, cocaine. CK >5,000 U/L with AKI is rhabdomyolysis until proven otherwise. Urine dipstick positive for blood with no RBCs on microscopy is the classic finding (myoglobin triggers the haem reaction). Management: aggressive IV fluid resuscitation to maintain urine output >200–300 mL/hour, urinary alkalinisation (sodium bicarbonate to achieve urine pH >6.5) to reduce myoglobin precipitation, and early nephrology involvement.

## Clinical Presentation

AKI is often clinically silent until advanced. Features depend on cause and severity:

- **Symptoms of uraemia** (late): fatigue, nausea, vomiting, confusion, itch, hiccups
- **Fluid overload**: peripheral oedema, pulmonary oedema, raised JVP — even in a patient who was initially volume-depleted
- **Volume depletion** (pre-renal clues): dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, postural hypotension, oliguria
- **Urological symptoms** (post-renal): poor stream, nocturia, suprapubic discomfort — but often absent even in significant obstruction; BPH can cause silent bilateral hydronephrosis
- **Systemic features suggesting intrinsic disease**: fever, rash, eosinophilia (AIN); haematuria, hypertension (GN); purpura, joint pain, nerve involvement (vasculitis)

**Always ask about:**
- New nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, aminoglycosides, contrast agents, metformin)
- Recent urological history and lower urinary tract symptoms
- Systemic illness, rash, joint pain (vasculitis, connective tissue disease)
- Risk factors for rhabdomyolysis — trauma, prolonged immobility (found collapsed), seizures, statin use, alcohol, extreme exertion

## Diagnosis

**Immediately stop all nephrotoxins** — NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs (restart when recovered and stable), metformin (risk of fatal lactic acidosis in AKI), aminoglycosides, radiocontrast.

**Bloods:** Creatinine (and trend versus baseline), urea, electrolytes, bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, FBC, CK (rhabdomyolysis), LDH, coagulation, blood cultures if sepsis suspected.

**Urine dipstick and microscopy:**

| Finding | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| Blood + protein | Glomerulonephritis |
| Leucocytes ± leucocyte casts | Infection or AIN |
| Muddy brown granular casts | ATN (pathognomonic) |
| RBC casts | Glomerulonephritis (pathognomonic) |
| WBC casts | Pyelonephritis or AIN |
| Hyaline casts | Normal or pre-renal |
| Dipstick blood with no RBCs on microscopy | Myoglobinuria (rhabdomyolysis) |

**Urine biochemistry — distinguishing pre-renal from ATN:**

| Test | Pre-renal | ATN |
|------|---------|-----|
| Urine sodium | <20 mEq/L | >40 mEq/L |
| Fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa) | <1% | >2% |
| Urine osmolality | >500 mOsm/kg | <350 mOsm/kg |

**FeNa = (Urine Na × Serum Cr) / (Serum Na × Urine Cr) × 100**

FeNa is unreliable in patients on diuretics — use fractional excretion of urea (FeUrea <35% = pre-renal) instead.

**Renal ultrasound — mandatory in all AKI:** Identifies hydronephrosis (post-renal obstruction), assesses kidney size (bilaterally small = underlying CKD), and detects bladder outflow obstruction. A bladder scan or in-and-out catheterisation is cheap, immediate, and non-invasive — do this first in any elderly man with AKI and no obvious cause. A bladder holding 800 mL is the entire diagnosis.

**Immunology if intrinsic disease suspected:** ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement C3/C4, anti-PLA2R, SPEP/UPEP, ASOT, hepatitis B/C, HIV. Do not delay obtaining these while waiting for results — if rapidly progressive GN is possible, get nephrology involved immediately.

## Management

### 1. Identify and Treat the Cause

- **Pre-renal**: IV crystalloid resuscitation (0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's) with frequent reassessment — avoid fluid overload. Stop nephrotoxins.
- **Post-renal**: Bladder catheterisation for lower urinary tract obstruction; urology referral for ureteric or upper tract obstruction (nephrostomy or stent). Monitor for post-obstructive diuresis — replace approximately half of hourly urine output with IV fluid to avoid hypotension while allowing excretion of accumulated solutes.
- **Intrinsic — AIN**: Remove the offending drug; consider prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day if no improvement within 1–2 weeks.
- **Intrinsic — GN/vasculitis**: Urgent nephrology; immunosuppression tailored to histological diagnosis.
- **Rhabdomyolysis**: IV fluids at 1–1.5 L/hour to achieve urine output >200 mL/hour; urinary alkalinisation with sodium bicarbonate.

### 2. Manage Life-Threatening Complications

**Hyperkalaemia** is the most immediately dangerous complication. ECG changes (peaked T waves → wide QRS → sine wave) indicate cardiac toxicity requiring emergency treatment:

| Step | Treatment | Mechanism | Onset |
|------|----------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | IV **calcium chloride** 10% 10 mL (preferred) OR calcium gluconate 10% 20–30 mL | Membrane stabilisation — does NOT lower K+ | Seconds |
| 2 | Insulin 10 units + 125 mL 20% glucose IV | Shifts K+ into cells | 15–30 min |
| 3 | Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg | Additional K+ cellular shift (additive to insulin) | 15–30 min |
| 4 | **Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC/Lokelma) 10 g TDS** OR patiromer | Gut potassium binder — removes K+ from body | SZC: ~1h; patiromer: 4–6h |
| 5 | Haemodialysis | Definitive K+ removal if refractory | Immediate |

Calcium chloride contains 3× more calcium per mL than calcium gluconate — it provides faster, more reliable membrane stabilisation. SZC (Lokelma) has a faster onset than patiromer (~1 hour vs 4–7 hours), making it preferable in the acute setting.

**Metabolic acidosis:** IV sodium bicarbonate if pH <7.1 or bicarbonate <15 mEq/L and symptomatic. Cautious — large volumes worsen fluid overload.

**Pulmonary oedema:** Fluid restriction, high-flow oxygen, furosemide IV if still making urine, CPAP for refractory hypoxia. If anuric and haemodynamically stable, proceed to emergent dialysis.

**Uraemia** (encephalopathy, uraemic pericarditis, platelet dysfunction with bleeding): Urgent dialysis. Uraemic pericarditis is a dialysis emergency — anticoagulation risks tamponade in this context.

### 3. Indications for Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)

- Refractory hyperkalaemia (K+ >6.5 unresponsive to medical treatment)
- Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1) not responding to bicarbonate
- Refractory pulmonary oedema
- Uraemic encephalopathy or pericarditis
- Uraemia severe enough to cause bleeding

### 4. Drug Prescribing in AKI

Adjust doses of all renally cleared drugs. Avoid NSAIDs indefinitely. Restart ACEi/ARB and metformin only when creatinine has returned to baseline and the patient is haemodynamically stable for at least 48 hours.

## Complications

- **Hyperkalaemia** — cardiac arrhythmia and arrest; the most acutely lethal complication
- **Metabolic acidosis** — cardiac depression, impaired drug metabolism
- **Pulmonary oedema** — fluid retention in oliguric/anuric patients
- **Infection** — uraemia impairs neutrophil and lymphocyte function
- **Post-AKI CKD** — even fully recovered AKI accelerates long-term renal decline; follow-up eGFR at 3 months is mandatory; approximately 30% of AKI patients do not fully recover baseline function
- **Lactic acidosis from metformin** if not stopped promptly in AKI — can be fatal

---

## Clinical Insight

Calcium chloride 10% provides 3× the elemental calcium of calcium gluconate — 272 mg/10 mL vs 93 mg/10 mL. It is the preferred membrane stabiliser in severe, ECG-confirmed hyperkalaemia where speed and reliability matter most. The trade-off is that calcium chloride is more caustic and should be given via a central or wide-bore IV line; calcium gluconate is safer peripherally. Both must be followed immediately by insulin-dextrose to actually lower the potassium — calcium treats the ECG changes without treating the electrolyte.

Metformin is renally excreted and accumulates in AKI. Accumulation inhibits hepatic mitochondrial metabolism, causing fatal lactic acidosis. The drug must be stopped at the first presentation of AKI — not after a creatinine threshold is crossed — and must not be restarted until function is fully restored and the patient is stable. This is one of the most important prescribing rules in nephrology, and one of the most commonly violated.

A bladder scan takes two minutes. In any elderly man admitted with AKI and no clear pre-renal explanation, bladder outlet obstruction from BPH is common, silent, and completely reversible with a single catheterisation. Every AKI workup must include assessment of the post-void residual before embarking on complex investigation for intrinsic disease.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 76-year-old man with hypertension and T2DM. Four days of vomiting and diarrhoea with reduced urine output. Takes ramipril, metformin, and ibuprofen. Creatinine 3.8 mg/dL (baseline 1.1 mg/dL). Oliguric, dry mucous membranes, HR 112, BP 94/58.

**Step 1 — Stage the AKI:** Creatinine rose from 1.1 to 3.8 mg/dL = 3.5× baseline. **KDIGO Stage 3** (≥3× baseline). This is severe AKI requiring urgent management.

**Step 2 — Identify the mechanism — the "triple whammy":** This presentation is a classic drug-induced AKI in the context of volume depletion:
- **Ibuprofen (NSAID)** blocks prostaglandin-mediated afferent arteriolar dilation → reduces GFR
- **Ramipril (ACEi)** blocks angiotensin II-mediated efferent arteriolar constriction → reduces GFR further
- **Volume depletion** from vomiting/diarrhoea → reduces renal perfusion pressure
All three mechanisms simultaneously = catastrophic GFR collapse. This is pre-renal AKI that may be transitioning to ATN if tubular ischaemia is established.

**Step 3 — Stop all nephrotoxins immediately (this is the first action):**
- **Stop ibuprofen** — direct nephrotoxin + afferent constriction
- **Stop ramipril** — must be withheld until creatinine recovers and the patient is haemodynamically stable
- **Stop metformin NOW** — renally excreted; accumulation in AKI causes fatal lactic acidosis. This is non-negotiable.

**Step 4 — Resuscitate:** IV Hartmann's 500 mL bolus; reassess BP, HR, and urine output. Insert urinary catheter to accurately monitor output. Target urine output >0.5 mL/kg/hour.

**Step 5 — Check potassium urgently:** AKI + ACEi = high risk of dangerous hyperkalaemia. Get an ECG — peaked T waves indicate cardiac toxicity. If K⁺ >6.0 mEq/L with ECG changes: calcium chloride → insulin-dextrose → consider dialysis.

**Step 6 — Renal ultrasound:** Mandatory to exclude obstruction. A 76-year-old man with AKI may have concurrent prostatic obstruction — check post-void residual.

**Exam trigger:** *AKI + NSAID + ACEi + volume depletion* → triple whammy; stop all three drugs immediately. The most dangerous omission is failing to stop metformin — lactic acidosis from metformin accumulation in AKI is fatal.
