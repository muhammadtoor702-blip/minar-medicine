## Overview

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined by the KDIGO criteria as any of: a rise in serum creatinine ≥26.5 µmol/L within 48 hours; a rise to ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days; or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for ≥6 hours. It is a common and serious condition — present in up to 15% of hospital inpatients — and is independently associated with increased mortality, progression to CKD, and prolonged hospital stay.

The diagnostic priority is to classify AKI as **pre-renal, intrinsic renal, or post-renal**, because each requires fundamentally different management.

## Pathophysiology

**Pre-renal AKI** (~60% of cases): Reduced renal perfusion leads to reduced GFR. The kidneys themselves are structurally normal — they are simply underperfused. The kidney responds by maximally retaining sodium and water (low urine sodium, concentrated urine). If perfusion is restored promptly, the AKI is fully reversible. If not, sustained ischaemia damages the tubular epithelium — transitioning to intrinsic AKI.

**Intrinsic renal AKI**: Direct damage to the renal parenchyma. The most common cause is **acute tubular necrosis (ATN)** — either from sustained ischaemia (the end result of untreated pre-renal AKI) or from nephrotoxins. Other intrinsic causes include acute interstitial nephritis (AIN), glomerulonephritis, and vascular causes (renal artery/vein thrombosis, HUS/TTP).

**Post-renal AKI**: Obstruction to urine outflow increases back-pressure, impairing GFR. Obstruction must be bilateral (or unilateral in a single functioning kidney) to cause AKI. The most common cause is BPH in elderly men. Relief of obstruction is followed by a post-obstructive diuresis — monitor closely.

**Why NSAIDs and ACE inhibitors are dangerous in volume depletion:**

In hypovolaemia, the kidney relies on two autoregulatory mechanisms to maintain GFR: prostaglandin-mediated afferent arteriolar dilation (countered by NSAIDs) and angiotensin II-mediated efferent arteriolar constriction (countered by ACE inhibitors/ARBs). Blocking either mechanism reduces GFR under conditions of reduced perfusion. This patient has both blocked simultaneously — a compounding insult on top of volume depletion.

## Clinical Presentation

AKI is often clinically silent until it is advanced. Features depend on the cause and severity:

- **Symptoms of uraemia** (late): fatigue, nausea, vomiting, confusion, itch, hiccups
- **Fluid overload**: peripheral oedema, pulmonary oedema, raised JVP
- **Volume depletion** (pre-renal): dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, tachycardia, postural hypotension
- **Urological symptoms** (post-renal): poor stream, nocturia, suprapubic discomfort — but often absent even in significant outflow obstruction
- **Systemic features suggesting intrinsic disease**: fever and rash (AIN), haematuria and hypertension (GN), systemic vasculitis features

**Always ask about:**
- New nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs, aminoglycosides, contrast agents, metformin)
- Recent urological history, lower urinary tract symptoms
- Systemic illness, rash, joint pain (vasculitis, connective tissue disease)
- Risk factors for rhabdomyolysis (trauma, seizures, immobility, statin use)

## Diagnosis

**Immediately stop all nephrotoxins** — NSAIDs, ACEi/ARBs (can resume when recovered and stable), metformin (risk of lactic acidosis in AKI), aminoglycosides, contrast agents.
**Bloods**: Creatinine (and trend), urea, electrolytes (hyperkalaemia is the life-threatening emergency), bicarbonate, calcium, phosphate, FBC, CK (rhabdomyolysis), LDH, coagulation.

**Urine dipstick and microscopy**:

| Finding | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| Blood + protein on dipstick | Glomerulonephritis |
| Leucocytes | Infection, AIN |
| Muddy brown granular casts | ATN (pathognomonic) |
| RBC casts | Glomerulonephritis (pathognomonic) |
| WBC casts | Pyelonephritis or AIN |
| Hyaline casts | Normal or pre-renal |

**Urine biochemistry** — distinguishing pre-renal from ATN:

| Test | Pre-renal | ATN |
|------|---------|-----|
| Urine sodium | <20 mmol/L | >40 mmol/L |
| Fractional excretion of sodium (FeNa) | <1% | >2% |
| Urine osmolality | >500 mOsm/kg | <350 mOsm/kg |

**FeNa = (Urine Na × Serum Cr) / (Serum Na × Urine Cr) × 100**

FeNa is unreliable in patients on diuretics — use fractional excretion of urea (FeUrea <35% = pre-renal) instead.

**Renal ultrasound — mandatory in all AKI**: Identifies hydronephrosis (post-renal obstruction), assesses kidney size (bilateral small kidneys = CKD), detects bladder outflow obstruction. Bladder scan (or catheterisation) should be performed in any elderly man with AKI before definitive labelling.

**Immunology if intrinsic disease suspected**: ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, anti-dsDNA, complement C3/C4, SPEP/UPEP, anti-streptolysin O, hepatitis B/C, HIV.

## Management

### 1. Identify and treat the cause immediately

- **Pre-renal**: IV fluid resuscitation with crystalloid (0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's), stop nephrotoxins. Reassess frequently — avoid fluid overload.
- **Post-renal**: Bladder catheterisation for lower urinary tract obstruction; urology referral for ureteric or upper tract obstruction (stenting or nephrostomy).
- **Intrinsic — AIN**: Remove the offending drug; consider prednisolone if no improvement within 1–2 weeks (benefit for drug-induced AIN).
- **Intrinsic — GN/vasculitis**: Urgent nephrology referral; immunosuppression (steroids ± cyclophosphamide or rituximab depending on cause).

### 2. Manage life-threatening complications

**Hyperkalaemia** is the most immediately dangerous complication. ECG changes (peaked T waves, wide QRS, sine wave) indicate cardiac toxicity and mandate emergency treatment:

| Step | Treatment | Rationale |
|------|----------|-----------|
| 1 | IV calcium gluconate 10% 10–20 mL | Membrane stabilisation — does NOT lower K+; acts within minutes |
| 2 | Insulin 10 units + 50% dextrose 50 mL IV | Shifts K+ into cells; onset 15–30 min |
| 3 | Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg | Additional K+ shift; additive to insulin |
| 4 | Calcium resonium / patiromer orally | Removes K+ via gut; slow onset (hours) |
| 5 | Haemodialysis | If refractory or severe |

**Metabolic acidosis**: IV sodium bicarbonate if pH <7.1 or bicarbonate <15 mmol/L and patient is symptomatic.

**Pulmonary oedema**: Fluid restriction, IV furosemide if oliguric but still making urine, CPAP, consider dialysis.

**Uraemia** (encephalopathy, pericarditis, platelet dysfunction, bleeding): Dialysis.

### 3. Renal replacement therapy (RRT) — indications

- Refractory hyperkalaemia (K+ >6.5 unresponsive to medical treatment)
- Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1) not responding to bicarbonate
- Refractory pulmonary oedema
- Uraemic encephalopathy or pericarditis
- Uraemia severe enough to cause bleeding

### 4. Drug prescribing

Adjust doses of all renally cleared drugs. Avoid NSAIDs indefinitely. Restart ACEi/ARB and metformin only when creatinine has returned to baseline and the patient is haemodynamically stable.

## Complications

- Hyperkalaemia — cardiac arrhythmia and arrest
- Metabolic acidosis — cardiac depression, arrhythmia
- Pulmonary oedema — fluid retention in anuric patients
- Infection — increased susceptibility due to uraemia-impaired immunity
- Post-AKI CKD — even fully recovered AKI accelerates long-term renal decline; follow-up eGFR at 3 months is mandatory
- Lactic acidosis from metformin if not stopped promptly

## Clinical Insight

Calcium gluconate stabilises the myocardium in hyperkalaemia but does not lower the potassium — it buys time. Always follow immediately with insulin-dextrose to actually shift potassium into cells. Giving calcium without the insulin-dextrose treats the ECG without treating the electrolyte.

Metformin accumulates in AKI because it is renally cleared, and accumulation causes lactic acidosis — a potentially fatal complication. Stop it at first presentation and do not restart until renal function has fully normalised and the patient is clinically stable.

A bladder scan is cheap, immediate, and non-invasive. In any elderly man admitted with AKI and no obvious pre-renal or intrinsic cause, a bladder scan or in-and-out catheterisation takes two minutes and may reveal 800 mL of retained urine — the entire diagnosis, reversible by a single catheterisation.
