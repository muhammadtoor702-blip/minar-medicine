## Overview

Obstetric emergencies encompass a cluster of life-threatening conditions unique to pregnancy and the immediate postpartum period. Their defining feature is rapid progression from initial presentation to maternal or fetal death without prompt action. Cord prolapse, placental abruption, and uterine rupture can each kill within minutes; amniotic fluid embolism may be unsurvivable regardless of intervention. The common thread is that recognition must be instantaneous, the team must be assembled simultaneously with initial management, and definitive treatment — almost always emergency caesarean section — must be achieved with the minimum possible delay. The "crash team" response in obstetrics is the equivalent of the trauma team activation: every second from decision to delivery matters.

## Pathophysiology

### Cord Prolapse

Descent of the umbilical cord through the cervical os alongside or ahead of the presenting part after membrane rupture. The cord is exposed to: (1) physical compression between the presenting part and the maternal pelvis; (2) arterial vasospasm from cold and drying. Both mechanisms reduce umbilical blood flow → fetal hypoxia → acidosis → death within minutes if unrelieved. The CTG shows variable or prolonged decelerations (cord compression) progressing to bradycardia.

**Predisposing factors:** Cord presentation (cord in front of presenting part with intact membranes), abnormal lie or presentation (transverse lie, oblique lie, breech), high presenting part (especially at amniotomy — NEVER perform ARM with a high presenting part until the head is engaged), polyhydramnios (cord floats alongside fetus), prematurity, multiparity, multiple pregnancy.

### Placenta Praevia

Placenta implanted over or adjacent to the internal cervical os. As the lower uterine segment develops in the third trimester, the attachment of the placenta shears from the os edge → painless antepartum haemorrhage. The uterus contracts with any haemorrhage → may precipitate further separation.

**Classification (RCOG):** Grade I — placental edge does not reach the os; Grade II — placental edge reaches the os; Grade III — placenta partially covers the os; Grade IV (major praevia) — placenta completely covers the os. "Minor praevia" = grades I–II, "major praevia" = grades III–IV.

**Key feature:** The uterus is NOT tender, because the haemorrhage is from the lower uterine segment pulling away from a normally contracting upper segment — there is no retroplacental clot causing uterine distension and pain (unlike abruption).

### Placental Abruption

Premature separation of a normally sited placenta from the uterine wall. Haemorrhage collects between the placenta and uterine wall (retroplacental haematoma) → (1) disrupts placental blood flow → fetal hypoxia; (2) myometrium infiltrated by blood → uterine irritability → pain, tetanic contractions (Couvelaire uterus); (3) coagulation factors consumed → DIC (particularly with severe abruption — fetal fibrin deposits trigger widespread activation).

**Risk factors:** Hypertension (hypertensive disorders of pregnancy double the risk), smoking, cocaine/amphetamine use, abdominal trauma, advanced maternal age, prior abruption (recurrence risk 10–15%), premature rupture of membranes.

**Revealed vs. concealed:** In revealed abruption, blood tracks down between membranes and uterine wall → vaginal bleeding. In concealed abruption, blood collects entirely behind the placenta → no external bleeding but severe pain + uterine hardness; the degree of haemodynamic compromise may far exceed the apparent blood loss.

### Uterine Rupture

Catastrophic failure of the uterine wall, most commonly at the site of a previous caesarean section scar. The classical scar (vertical, through upper uterine segment) is far more likely to rupture than the lower-segment transverse scar (LSCS). Risk: previous LSCS, previous uterine surgery (myomectomy), excessive oxytocin, obstructed labour, grande multiparity.

**Signs:** Sudden severe abdominal pain (may be preceded by cessation of previous labour pain as uterus decompresses), maternal collapse and haemodynamic instability, loss of fetal lie palpation (fetus extruded into abdomen), cessation of uterine contractions, CTG bradycardia or absence of fetal heart. The "scar tenderness" in the pre-rupture phase (impending rupture) may precede frank rupture.

### Amniotic Fluid Embolism (AFE)

Entry of amniotic fluid, fetal cells, and debris into the maternal circulation through disrupted uteroplacental veins (during labour, delivery, or caesarean). Triggers: complement activation + anaphylactoid response → pulmonary vasospasm → right heart failure → hypoxia. Simultaneously: procoagulant substances in amniotic fluid → massive DIC. Biphasic: (1) acute right heart failure + hypoxia; (2) haemorrhagic phase from DIC.

**Classic presentation:** Sudden dyspnoea, hypotension, and seizure during active labour or immediately post-delivery, followed by cardiovascular collapse and catastrophic haemorrhage. Mortality 20–60%. Diagnosis of exclusion after other causes are excluded.

## Clinical Presentation

### Cord Prolapse

- **Circumstance:** After spontaneous or artificial rupture of membranes
- **CTG:** Variable decelerations or prolonged deceleration/bradycardia
- **Examination:** Loop of cord palpable or visible at vulva; OR pulsatile cord felt on vaginal examination alongside presenting part
- **Emergency:** Fetal viability depends entirely on how fast the cord is decompressed

### Placenta Praevia vs. Abruption — Differential

| Feature | Placenta Praevia | Abruption |
|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| Pain | None | Severe, constant |
| Bleeding | Bright red, painless | Dark red (if revealed), may be absent |
| Uterus | Soft, non-tender | Hard, board-like, tender |
| Fetal presentation | Often abnormal (high, transverse) | Normal |
| CTG | Often normal initially | Fetal distress |
| Shock severity | Proportional to visible bleeding | May exceed visible loss (concealed) |
| DIC | Rare | Common with severe abruption |
| Diagnosis | USS | Clinical + USS to exclude praevia |

### Postpartum Haemorrhage (PPH)

**Definition:** Blood loss >500 mL after vaginal delivery or >1000 mL after CS. **Primary PPH:** within 24h of delivery. **Secondary PPH:** 24h to 12 weeks postpartum (usually infection ± retained products).

**The 4 Ts:**
- **Tone (70%):** Uterine atony — the uterus fails to contract after delivery. Risk factors: prolonged/augmented labour, high parity, overdistended uterus (twins, polyhydramnios, macrosomia), placenta praevia, magnesium infusion, general anaesthesia.
- **Trauma (20%):** Perineal tears, cervical tears, vaginal haematoma, uterine inversion, uterine rupture.
- **Tissue (10%):** Retained placenta, retained membranes, placenta accreta spectrum.
- **Thrombin (<1%):** Pre-existing or acquired coagulopathy (DIC from abruption, AFE, MH); thrombocytopenia.

## Diagnosis

**Cord prolapse:** Clinical — palpable cord on vaginal examination. If in doubt, examine vaginally in any patient with ruptured membranes and fetal heart rate abnormality.

**Praevia/abruption:** Transabdominal ultrasound (placental localisation) — do NOT perform vaginal examination in known or suspected placenta praevia without preparations for emergency CS (examination may precipitate catastrophic haemorrhage). MRI can define placenta accreta spectrum.

**Bloods in obstetric haemorrhage:** FBC (haemoglobin — may be falsely normal in acute haemorrhage before haemodilution), coagulation (PT, APTT, fibrinogen — fibrinogen <150 mg/dL = DIC in progress), U&E, crossmatch (4+ units pRBC), group and save. Kleihauer-Betke test for fetomaternal haemorrhage (Rh-negative mothers).

**CTG interpretation:** Variable decelerations with quick recovery = cord compression (cord prolapse). Prolonged deceleration (>3 min) = cord compression or abruption. Late decelerations (uniform, after peak of contraction) = placental insufficiency. Loss of variability + abnormal decelerations = severe fetal compromise.

## Management

### Cord Prolapse — Emergency Protocol

**Reduce cord compression immediately:**
1. **Manual elevation of presenting part**: Insert fingers vaginally (or hand) to push the presenting part upward, relieving pressure on the cord. Maintain continuous manual elevation until delivery — do NOT remove the hand.
2. **Position:** Knee-chest position or steep Trendelenburg (gravity keeps the presenting part away from the cord) — OR fill the maternal bladder with 500–700 mL saline (bulges the bladder, elevating the presenting part) while maintaining manual elevation.
3. **Keep cord warm and moist:** If prolapsed externally — do NOT push it back (trauma, vasospasm); cover with moist warm pad.
4. **Tocolysis (Terbutaline 0.25 mg SC or IV):** To reduce uterine contractions that force the presenting part against the cord; buys time during transfer to theatre.
5. **Call 999 if in the community** — all actions above while awaiting transfer; paramedic should maintain manual elevation throughout.
6. **Emergency CS (Category 1):** Spinal or general anaesthesia (time-critical — generally GA for speed). **Decision-to-delivery interval should be <30 minutes** (ideally <20 minutes). If CS is impossible and cervix is fully dilated: assisted vaginal delivery.

### Placenta Praevia

**Antepartum management:**
- Admit if major praevia presenting with bleeding
- IV access, crossmatch, resuscitation if haemodynamically unstable
- No vaginal examination
- Betamethasone 12 mg IM ×2 doses 24h apart (if <34 weeks — for fetal lung maturity)
- **Planned CS at 36–37 weeks** for major praevia (before labour — labour with praevia → catastrophic haemorrhage)
- **Senior obstetric + anaesthetic team + blood bank warning** for CS — praevia carries high haemorrhage risk; prepare for placenta accreta spectrum

**Placenta accreta spectrum (PAS):** Abnormal adherence of placental villi to the uterine wall (accreta) — penetration into myometrium (increta) — penetration through uterine serosa (percreta, most severe). Risk: previous CS scar + placenta praevia (anterior praevia over scar = highest risk). Management: multidisciplinary team, senior surgeons, cell salvage, hysterectomy often required. PAS accounts for the majority of peripartum hysterectomies.

### Placental Abruption

**Mild abruption:** Monitor mother + fetus; may continue pregnancy if fetal wellbeing intact and haemorrhage controlled.

**Severe abruption:**
1. IV access ×2, crossmatch 6 units, Foley catheter (urine output), bloods including coagulation + fibrinogen
2. Aggressive fluid resuscitation — **blood products (1:1:1)** if massive haemorrhage; tranexamic acid
3. **Correct DIC** if present: FFP (fibrinogen/clotting factors), cryoprecipitate (fibrinogen), platelets
4. **Emergency CS** for fetal compromise or maternal haemodynamic instability; vaginal delivery may proceed if fetal death (delivers faster, avoids surgery risk in a coagulopathic patient)
5. Oxytocin infusion post-delivery to maintain uterine tone

### PPH — Stepwise Uterotonic Ladder

**Step 1 — First-line (atony):**
- **Fundal massage** (bimanual compression)
- **Oxytocin 5–10 units IV slowly** (rapid bolus causes vasodilatation and hypotension; infuse over 30 min) then infusion 40 units over 4h

**Step 2 — Second-line:**
- **Ergometrine 0.5 mg IM/IV** (ergot alkaloid — sustained uterine contraction; contraindicated in hypertension, pre-eclampsia, cardiac disease)
- **Carboprost (prostaglandin F2α, Hemabate) 0.25 mg IM** every 15 minutes (max 8 doses); **contraindicated in asthma** (bronchospasm)
- **Misoprostol 800–1000 mcg per rectum** (prostaglandin E1)
- **Tranexamic acid 1 g IV** (antifibrinolytic — WOMAN trial: reduces death from haemorrhage; give within 3 hours of delivery)

**Step 3 — Surgical (uterine atony refractory to uterotonics):**
- **Balloon tamponade** (Bakri balloon — fills the uterine cavity with 300–500 mL saline, tamponades the placental bed)
- **B-Lynch suture** (compression suture — brace suture through the uterine wall, compresses the uterus like a brace)
- **Interventional radiology (uterine artery embolisation)** — if haemodynamically stable and radiology available; preserves uterus and fertility
- **Internal iliac artery ligation**
- **Hysterectomy** (peripartum hysterectomy) — last resort; definitive haemostasis when all else fails; performed with the family counselled about loss of fertility where possible, but in a deteriorating patient may be lifesaving without delay

## Complications

- **Cord prolapse:** Fetal death (asphyxia), cerebral palsy, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy
- **Praevia:** Peripartum hysterectomy (especially with accreta), maternal death from haemorrhage, preterm birth
- **Abruption:** DIC, fetal death, maternal death, renal failure (cortical necrosis), peripartum hysterectomy
- **PPH:** Sheehan's syndrome (pituitary necrosis from hypoperfusion → panhypopituitarism → failure to lactate, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency); ARDS; AKI; multi-organ failure; death
- **AFE:** Maternal death (20–60%), hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, DIC with catastrophic haemorrhage

## Clinical Insight

Cord prolapse management has one inviolable rule: do not remove the hand. Once the clinician's hand is inside the vagina elevating the presenting part, it must not come out until the baby is delivered. Withdrawing the hand — even momentarily — allows the presenting part to descend, recompressing the cord. The team must work around the clinician's hand: moving the patient, preparing theatre, administering anaesthesia. This requires extraordinary coordination and a team that has drilled the scenario. Simulation training for cord prolapse reduces decision-to-delivery time in real events.

The concealed abruption is the abruption that kills mothers. A patient with severe constant abdominal pain, a board-like uterus, and a shocked appearance with minimal visible vaginal bleeding has a massive concealed retroplacental haematoma. The visible blood loss seriously underestimates the true haemorrhage. Do not calculate resuscitation requirements based on what you can see — they will fall far short of what is needed.

Carboprost and ergometrine are both highly effective uterotonics but have critical contraindications that are tested repeatedly: carboprost in asthma (severe bronchospasm), ergometrine in hypertension and pre-eclampsia (severe systemic vasoconstriction worsening hypertension). In a patient with hypertension who is haemorrhaging, the second-line uterotonic should be carboprost (if no asthma) — not ergometrine.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** G2P1, 38 weeks, painless haemorrhage after membrane rupture, haemodynamically shocked (HR 124, BP 86/54), soft non-tender uterus, CTG prolonged deceleration to 70 bpm, pulsating cord palpable at the os.

**Diagnosis: Cord prolapse + haemodynamic shock from blood loss (likely placenta praevia given painless haemorrhage + soft uterus)**

**Step 1 — Cord prolapse — immediately:**
- **Do not remove hand from vagina** — manually elevate the presenting part continuously
- Call for immediate help (second midwife, obstetric registrar/consultant, anaesthetist, theatre team, neonatologist)
- Position: place patient in steep Trendelenburg or knee-chest position while maintaining elevation
- Tocolysis: **terbutaline 0.25 mg SC** to relax uterus and reduce cord compression

**Step 2 — Resuscitate simultaneously:**
- IV access ×2 large-bore
- 0.9% NaCl or blood products for haemodynamic stabilisation
- FBC, coagulation, crossmatch 6 units, Kleihauer

**Step 3 — Emergency Category 1 CS:**
- Call theatre — Category 1 (immediate threat to life)
- General anaesthesia (time-critical — faster than spinal in this emergency)
- Maintain manual elevation throughout transfer to theatre, positioning on table, and until uterine incision made
- Decision-to-delivery interval target: <30 minutes

**Step 4 — Neonatology:**
- Neonatologist and resuscitaire prepared for potentially severely compromised neonate

**Step 5 — Post-delivery:**
- Inspect placenta — if major praevia confirmed: high oxytocin infusion + prepare for PPH management
- Counsel about recurrence risk, future deliveries

**Exam trigger:** *Pulsating cord palpable at cervical os + fetal bradycardia + ruptured membranes* → cord prolapse. Immediate manual elevation of presenting part + Category 1 CS. The distractor is placing the patient in left lateral decubitus and waiting — this reduces aortocaval compression but does nothing to relieve cord compression. The hand must go in and the baby must come out.
