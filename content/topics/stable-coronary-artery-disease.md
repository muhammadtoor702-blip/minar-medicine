## Overview

Stable coronary artery disease (chronic coronary syndrome) results from fixed, non-obstructing atherosclerotic plaques that narrow one or more coronary arteries. Unlike ACS, there is no acute plaque rupture. Instead, a stable plaque limits blood flow during increased myocardial oxygen demand but permits adequate flow at rest — producing predictable, exertional symptoms that resolve with rest or nitrates.

Stable CAD represents a high cardiovascular risk state. The goals of management are twofold: **symptom relief** through anti-anginal therapy, and **mortality reduction** through aggressive secondary prevention.

## Pathophysiology

Coronary atherosclerosis is a lifelong, progressive process. Lipid infiltration, macrophage recruitment, and chronic inflammation produce an increasingly stenotic fibrous plaque. As the lumen narrows beyond 70–80%, coronary flow reserve is exhausted — resting flow is maintained, but the artery cannot vasodilate sufficiently during exercise to meet increased oxygen demand. The result is subendocardial ischaemia, producing the symptoms of stable angina.

The pathological consequences of ischaemia follow a predictable cascade:
- Metabolic changes (lactate accumulation) → diastolic dysfunction → systolic dysfunction → ECG changes → anginal pain

**Vasospastic (Prinzmetal's) angina** is a distinct entity caused by coronary artery spasm — often on angiographically normal arteries. It presents as rest angina, frequently at night or early morning, with transient ST elevation during episodes. Beta-blockers may paradoxically worsen vasospasm — calcium channel blockers are first-line.

**Microvascular angina (Cardiac Syndrome X)** — angina with a positive stress test but normal coronary angiography, due to endothelial dysfunction of small vessels. More common in women post-menopause.

## Clinical Presentation

**Typical angina requires all three features:**
1. Central chest discomfort (pressure, heaviness, tightness — not sharp or stabbing)
2. Precipitated by exertion or emotional stress
3. Relieved by rest or sublingual nitrates within 5 minutes

Atypical angina has two of three features. Non-cardiac chest pain has one or none.

Pre-test probability of significant CAD (ESC 2019) — based on age, sex, and symptom typicality — guides the investigative strategy. Low probability: no further cardiac testing. Intermediate probability: functional testing or CTCA. High probability: treat as CAD, consider direct angiography.

**Differential for exertional chest pain:**
- GORD — burning, food-related, improves with antacids
- Musculoskeletal — reproduced by palpation
- Anaemia — exertional dyspnoea, pallor
- Aortic stenosis — ejection systolic murmur radiating to carotids; always auscultate

## Diagnosis

**Resting ECG** — may show LVH, old Q waves, bundle branch block; often entirely normal.

**Non-invasive functional testing (for intermediate probability):**
- Exercise ECG (treadmill) — suitable if the patient can exercise and the resting ECG is normal. Hold beta-blockers and rate-limiting CCBs 48 hours prior unless testing anti-anginal efficacy.
- Stress echocardiography or nuclear perfusion imaging (SPECT) — if the patient cannot exercise or the baseline ECG is abnormal (LBBB, pacemaker, LVH with strain)
- CT coronary angiography (CTCA) — excellent negative predictive value; rules out significant CAD if negative; preferred for younger patients with low-intermediate probability

**High-risk features on exercise testing demanding urgent referral:**
- ST depression >2mm at low workload (<5 METs)
- ST depression involving ≥5 leads
- Failure of systolic BP to rise with exercise (flat or falling — indicates LV failure under stress)
- Sustained VT
- Duke Treadmill Score ≤−11

**Invasive coronary angiography** — gold standard; required before any revascularisation to define anatomy and guide PCI vs CABG decision.
## Management

### Secondary Prevention — Mandatory for All Patients

| Intervention | Target/Notes |
|-------------|-------------|
| Antiplatelet therapy | Aspirin 75mg indefinitely. Clopidogrel if aspirin intolerant. |
| High-intensity statin | Atorvastatin 80mg or rosuvastatin 20–40mg. LDL target <1.4 mmol/L (very high risk). |
| ACE inhibitor or ARB | All patients with LV dysfunction, diabetes, or CKD; consider in all. |
| BP control | Target <130/80 mmHg |
| Glycaemic control | HbA1c <53 mmol/mol |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation, Mediterranean diet, supervised cardiac rehabilitation, ≥150 min exercise/week |

### Anti-anginal Therapy

**Beta-blockers** are first-line. They reduce heart rate, increase diastolic filling time, and reduce myocardial oxygen demand. They also improve survival post-MI and in HFrEF.

**Calcium channel blockers:**
- Rate-limiting (diltiazem, verapamil) — negative chronotropic and inotropic effects; useful if beta-blocker is contraindicated or in combination
- Dihydropyridines (amlodipine) — predominantly vasodilatory; no negative chronotropic effect; well tolerated
- **Vasospastic angina:** CCB is the drug of choice. Beta-blockers are contraindicated — they worsen spasm by blocking beta-mediated vasodilation, leaving alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed.

**Nitrates:**
- Short-acting GTN (sublingual spray or tablet) — for acute episodes; onset within 2 minutes
- Long-acting nitrates (isosorbide mononitrate) — for prophylaxis; require an 8-hour nitrate-free interval daily to prevent tolerance
- Mechanism: systemic venous dilation → reduced preload → reduced wall stress and O₂ demand

**Third-line options:**
- **Ivabradine** — reduces heart rate via If channel inhibition in the SA node without affecting BP or contractility; useful if CCB and beta-blocker are both contraindicated
- **Ranolazine** — inhibits late sodium current; reduces intracellular calcium overload during ischaemia
- **Nicorandil** — potassium channel opener and nitrate; both preload and afterload reduction

### Revascularisation

**PCI** (percutaneous coronary intervention, stenting):
- Preferred for single or two-vessel disease
- Drug-eluting stents — DAPT for 6–12 months post-stenting
- Superior to CABG for isolated left main stem or single-vessel disease

**CABG** (coronary artery bypass grafting):
- Preferred for three-vessel CAD, especially with diabetes (FREEDOM trial) or reduced EF
- Left main stem stenosis
- Complex anatomy (high SYNTAX score)
- LIMA to LAD is the conduit of choice — >90% patency at 10 years

**ISCHEMIA trial (2020):** In stable CAD with moderate-severe ischaemia, routine early invasive strategy did not reduce major adverse cardiac events compared with optimal medical therapy alone. This has reinforced the importance of maximising medical therapy before proceeding to revascularisation in stable patients.

## Complications

- Progression to ACS — plaque destabilisation and rupture
- Heart failure — from cumulative ischaemic myocardial damage
- Arrhythmias — ventricular arrhythmias from ischaemic myocardium
- Sudden cardiac death

## Clinical Insight

Stable angina carries a real but manageable mortality risk. The ISCHEMIA trial has confirmed that aggressive medical therapy — statin, antiplatelet, ACEi, beta-blocker, BP control, lifestyle — is as effective as early revascularisation for preventing MI and death in stable patients. Revascularisation is primarily for symptom relief when medical therapy is inadequate.

GTN should relieve typical angina within 2–5 minutes. If pain persists beyond 20 minutes or occurs at rest, it is ACS until proven otherwise.

Always auscultate for aortic stenosis before attributing exertional chest symptoms to coronary disease. A patient with AS, a normal resting ECG, and exertional symptoms may be sent for a stress test that could precipitate syncope or arrhythmia if the valve pathology is missed.
