## Overview

Stable coronary artery disease (chronic coronary syndrome) results from fixed atherosclerotic plaques that progressively narrow one or more coronary arteries. Unlike ACS, there is no acute plaque rupture — instead, a stable plaque limits blood flow during increased myocardial oxygen demand while permitting adequate resting flow. The result is predictable, exertional symptoms that resolve with rest or nitrates.

Stable CAD represents a high cardiovascular risk state: these patients are at continued risk of plaque destabilisation and ACS. The goals of management are therefore twofold — **symptom relief** through anti-anginal therapy, and **mortality reduction** through aggressive secondary prevention.

---

## Pathophysiology

Coronary atherosclerosis begins in the second decade of life. LDL infiltrates the arterial intima, undergoes oxidation, and is engulfed by macrophages to form foam cells — the cellular basis of the fatty streak. Progressive inflammation, lipid accumulation, and smooth muscle cell migration produce a fibrous plaque with a necrotic core capped by a fibrous layer.

When luminal narrowing exceeds 70–80%, coronary flow reserve is exhausted. At rest, the coronary artery can still vasodilate enough to maintain adequate flow. During exercise, the artery cannot vasodilate further — flow becomes fixed and insufficient for increased myocardial oxygen demand. The consequence is **subendocardial ischaemia**.

Ischaemia follows a cascade of pathological events in this order:
- Metabolic changes (lactate accumulation, ATP depletion)
- Diastolic dysfunction (impaired relaxation)
- Systolic dysfunction
- ECG changes (ST depression)
- Anginal pain — the last event in the cascade, not the first

**Types of angina:**
- **Stable angina** — predictably induced by exertion, relieved by rest in <5 minutes or by GTN
- **Unstable angina** — angina of increasing frequency or severity, occurring at rest or with minimal exertion; equivalent to ACS (see ACS topic)
- **Decubitus angina** — precipitated by lying flat (increased venous return raises preload)
- **Vasospastic (Prinzmetal's) angina** — caused by epicardial coronary artery spasm, even on angiographically normal arteries; pain occurs at rest, often at night or early morning; ECG during episodes shows ST elevation (not depression); relieved by GTN and calcium channel blockers; can coexist with fixed stenosis

**Microvascular angina (Cardiac Syndrome X):** angina symptoms with a positive stress test but normal coronary angiography, caused by endothelial dysfunction of the coronary microvasculature. More common in post-menopausal women.

## Clinical Presentation

**Typical angina — all three features must be present:**
1. **Constricting or heavy discomfort** to the chest, jaw, neck, shoulders, or arms (not sharp, not pleuritic, not reproduced by palpation)
2. **Brought on by exertion** or emotional stress
3. **Relieved within 5 minutes** by rest or sublingual GTN

**Atypical angina:** 2 of 3 features. **Non-anginal chest pain:** 0–1 features.

Features that make angina less likely: pain that is continuous; pleuritic; or worse with swallowing; associated with palpitations, dizziness, or tingling; reproduced by palpation.

**Other precipitants:** cold weather, heavy meals, emotional stress. Associated symptoms: dyspnoea, nausea, diaphoresis.

**Always examine for:** aortic stenosis (ejection systolic murmur radiating to carotids — can mimic or coexist with angina), anaemia (pallor, flow murmur), signs of heart failure.

**Differential for exertional chest pain:**
- Aortic stenosis — ejection systolic murmur, slow-rising pulse; auscultate before any stress testing
- GORD — burning, food-related, worse at night, responds to antacids
- Musculoskeletal — reproduced by chest wall palpation
- Anaemia — exertional dyspnoea, pallor
- PE — pleuritic pain, tachycardia, risk factors

## Diagnosis

**Resting ECG** — often entirely normal in stable CAD. May show: LVH (in hypertension), old Q waves (prior MI), LBBB. ST depression or T-wave inversions at rest suggest ischaemia or prior infarction.

**Blood tests:** FBC (anaemia as a cause or exacerbating factor), U&E, TFTs (hypothyroidism accelerates coronary disease and causes hyperlipidaemia), lipids, HbA1c. Consider echo and CXR.

### NICE Investigation Algorithm for Suspected Stable Angina

**Typical angina in a patient with previously proven IHD:** Treat as stable angina. If further confirmation is required, use non-invasive testing (e.g., exercise ECG) or offer non-invasive functional imaging if there is uncertainty.

**Typical and atypical angina:** CT coronary angiography (CTCA) is first-line. Excellent negative predictive value — effectively rules out significant stenosis if negative. If CTCA is inconclusive, use functional imaging (second-line), then transcatheter angiography (third-line).

**Non-anginal chest pain:** Check ECG. If normal and no other clinical suspicion of IHD, no further investigation at this stage. If ECG shows ischaemic changes, investigate as per typical angina.

### Non-Invasive Functional Tests

- **Exercise ECG** — the patient exercises to a standardised protocol (e.g., Bruce protocol on treadmill); ECG and BP monitored throughout. Hold beta-blockers and rate-limiting CCBs 48h prior unless testing anti-anginal efficacy specifically. Requires interpretable baseline ECG.
- **Stress echocardiography** — if patient cannot exercise adequately, or baseline ECG abnormal (LBBB, paced rhythm, significant LVH). Dobutamine or exercise stress used.
- **Nuclear perfusion scintigraphy (SPECT)** — myocardial perfusion imaging at stress vs. rest; demonstrates reversible ischaemia
- **Cardiac MRI with perfusion** — increasingly used; excellent for characterisation

**High-risk features on exercise testing — demand urgent referral:**
- ST depression >2mm at low workload (<5 METs)
- ST depression involving ≥5 leads
- Failure of systolic BP to rise with exercise (flat or declining BP response — indicates LV failure under stress)
- Sustained VT during exercise
- Duke Treadmill Score ≤−11

**Invasive coronary angiography** — gold standard; required before any revascularisation to define anatomy precisely and guide the PCI vs CABG decision. Transcatheter angiography allows concurrent stenting.

## Management

### Secondary Prevention — Mandatory for All Patients

| Intervention | Target / Notes |
|-------------|---------------|
| Aspirin 75mg OD | Indefinitely; clopidogrel 75mg if genuinely aspirin-intolerant |
| High-intensity statin | Atorvastatin 40–80mg or rosuvastatin 20–40mg; LDL target <55 mg/dL in very high-risk |
| ACE inhibitor | Especially if coexisting diabetes, CKD, proteinuria, or LV dysfunction |
| BP control | Target <130/80 mmHg; beta-blockers preferred if also post-MI |
| Glycaemic control | HbA1c target according to diabetes guidelines; GLP-1 agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors reduce CV events |
| Lifestyle | Smoking cessation (most important); Mediterranean diet; ≥150 min/week aerobic exercise; weight loss; cardiac rehabilitation |

**Stop smoking**: the most impactful single intervention. Provide pharmacological support (varenicline, NRT, bupropion).

### GTN for Acute Symptom Relief

**Sublingual GTN spray or tablet** — onset of action within 2 minutes. **Advise patients:**
- Use at onset of pain
- Repeat after 5 minutes if pain has not resolved
- Call an ambulance if pain is still present 5 minutes after the second dose — this is ACS until proven otherwise

**Mechanism:** Venous dilation → reduced preload → reduced wall tension and myocardial O₂ demand; also dilates large epicardial coronary arteries. Side effects: headache, hypotension.

### Anti-Anginal Drug Therapy

**Step 1 — First-line: Beta-blocker AND/OR calcium channel blocker**

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| Bisoprolol | 5–10mg OD | β₁-selective; preferred first-line |
| Atenolol | 50mg BD | Non-selective; also used |
| Amlodipine | 5–10mg OD | Dihydropyridine; predominantly vasodilatory; no negative chronotropy; combine with beta-blocker |
| Diltiazem MR | 120–360mg/day | Non-dihydropyridine; also rate-limiting; preferred if beta-blocker contraindicated |
| Verapamil | 120–360mg/day | Non-dihydropyridine; do not combine with beta-blockers (risk of severe bradycardia/LVF) |

**Vasospastic angina:** calcium channel blockers are first-line. Avoid non-selective beta-blockers (they block beta-mediated vasodilation, leaving alpha-mediated vasoconstriction unopposed, worsening spasm). Also avoid aspirin and triptans in vasospastic angina.

**Step 2 — Add long-acting nitrate if symptoms persist:**

**Isosorbide mononitrate** — starting dose depends on formulation (typically 30–60mg OD modified-release). An 8-hour **nitrate-free interval** is mandatory to prevent tolerance. Alternative: GTN transdermal patch (remove overnight).

**Step 3 — Third-line agents:**

- **Ranolazine** — inhibits the late sodium current during ischaemia, reducing intracellular calcium overload and diastolic tension. Start at 375mg twice daily, titrate to 500–750mg BD. Caution if heart failure, weight <60kg, elderly, or prolonged QTc.
- **Ivabradine** — selective sinus node If-channel inhibitor; reduces heart rate without affecting BP or contractility. 5mg BD initially. Useful only if patient is in sinus rhythm and beta-blocker is contraindicated or insufficient. According to NICE, ivabradine in stable angina should be considered only if heart failure also present.
- **Nicorandil** — K⁺ channel activator and nitrate; reduces both preload and afterload. Start 5–10mg BD. Contraindicated in acute pulmonary oedema, severe hypotension, hypovolaemia.
- **Trimetazidine** — metabolic agent (partial fatty acid oxidation inhibitor); used in Europe.

### Revascularisation

**When to revascularise:** When optimal medical therapy is inadequate for symptom control, or when anatomy confers a survival benefit (left main or proximal LAD disease, triple-vessel disease with impaired LV function).

**PCI (percutaneous coronary intervention):**
- Preferred for single or two-vessel disease
- Drug-eluting stents reduce re-stenosis risk; DAPT (aspirin + clopidogrel) for 6–12 months post-PCI
- CT angiography helps plan approach
- **ISCHEMIA trial (2020):** In stable CAD with moderate-to-severe ischaemia on functional testing, routine early invasive strategy did NOT reduce major adverse cardiovascular events compared with optimal medical therapy over 3.5 years — reinforcing that medical therapy is equally effective for prevention in stable disease; revascularisation primarily improves symptoms

**CABG (coronary artery bypass grafting):**
- Preferred for three-vessel disease, left main stem disease, complex anatomy (high SYNTAX score), or diabetes with multivessel disease (FREEDOM trial)
- Compared with PCI: longer recovery but less frequent repeat revascularisation and better long-term symptom control in multivessel disease
- **LIMA to LAD** is the conduit of choice: internal mammary artery graft >90% patency at 10 years; saphenous vein grafts have >50% closure at 10 years (aspirin helps prevent this)
- After CABG: restart anti-anginal drugs if symptoms recur; ensure BP, diabetes, and hyperlipidaemia are optimally managed; aspirin 75mg OD indefinitely; cardiac rehabilitation; driving — 1 month rest, no DVLA notification needed for non-HGV licence

## Complications

- **Progression to ACS** — plaque destabilisation and rupture; the most important complication; risk reduced by statin and aspirin
- **Heart failure** — from cumulative ischaemic myocardial damage and repeated silent ischaemic episodes
- **Ventricular arrhythmias** — from ischaemic myocardium; SCD risk is real even in stable disease
- **Sudden cardiac death** — particularly if left main or severe three-vessel disease is undetected

## Clinical Insight

The ISCHEMIA trial has fundamentally changed how we approach stable CAD — it confirms that for most stable patients, aggressive medical therapy is as effective as early revascularisation for preventing MI and death. Revascularisation is primarily for symptom relief when medical therapy is inadequate. This does not apply to high-risk anatomy (left main, proximal triple vessel disease) where CABG clearly improves survival.

GTN should relieve typical angina within 2–5 minutes. If pain persists beyond 20 minutes, or occurs at rest, it is ACS until proven otherwise — manage accordingly.

Always auscultate for aortic stenosis before any stress testing. A patient with haemodynamically significant AS and exertional symptoms sent for an exercise ECG may develop syncope or life-threatening arrhythmia during the test if the valve pathology is unrecognised. The ESM radiating to the carotids, slow-rising pulse, and narrow pulse pressure are the clues.
