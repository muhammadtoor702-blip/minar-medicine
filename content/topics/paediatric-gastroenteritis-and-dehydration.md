## Overview

Acute gastroenteritis — infection-driven diarrhoea with or without vomiting — is one of the most common illnesses in childhood globally, accounting for approximately 1.7 billion episodes and nearly 500,000 deaths per year in children under 5, almost entirely in low- and middle-income countries. In developed countries, deaths are rare, but hospitalisation for dehydration remains common. The management principle is deceptively simple: replace what is lost. The complexity lies in assessing severity accurately, choosing the correct fluid type and route, and recognising the conditions that can masquerade as or complicate gastroenteritis — intussusception, pyloric stenosis, and malrotation with volvulus among them.

## Pathophysiology

### Mechanisms of Diarrhoea

**Osmotic diarrhoea:** Unabsorbed solutes in the gut lumen draw water by osmosis. Caused by carbohydrate malabsorption (lactase deficiency post-viral) or hyperosmolar oral rehydration solutions. Stops with fasting.

**Secretory diarrhoea:** Enterotoxins (e.g., cholera toxin, ETEC LT and ST toxins) activate cAMP/cGMP pathways in enterocytes → active secretion of Na+, Cl⁻, and water into the gut lumen, overwhelming absorptive capacity. Large-volume watery diarrhoea. Persists with fasting.

**Inflammatory/invasive diarrhoea:** Direct invasion of the intestinal epithelium (Shigella, Campylobacter, invasive E. coli) → mucosal damage + inflammatory cell infiltration → bloody diarrhoea with mucus (dysentery). Fever + systemic toxicity.

### Fluid Compartment Shifts in Dehydration

Fluid loss from diarrhoea is largely from the extracellular fluid compartment (isotonic loss in most gastroenteritis). Dehydration initially reduces intravascular volume → compensatory tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction (prolonged CRT), reduced urine output. Severe dehydration reduces tissue perfusion → lactic acidosis. In **hypernatraemic dehydration** (Na >150 mEq/L — from hypotonic fluid losses replaced with hypotonic fluids or formula), water moves from ICF to ECF, causing brain cell shrinkage; rapid correction can cause cerebral oedema from osmotic equilibration.

## Clinical Presentation

### Dehydration Assessment (NICE CG84)

Clinical assessment is more reliable than any single marker. Weight loss is the gold standard if a recent weight is available (1 kg = 1 L deficit):

| Feature | No Dehydration | Mild (<5%) | Moderate (5–10%) | Severe (>10%) |
|---------|---------------|-----------|-----------------|--------------|
| General appearance | Alert, normal | Alert, normal | Irritable | Lethargic/unconscious |
| Eyes | Normal | Normal | Sunken | Very sunken |
| Mucous membranes | Moist | Normal/slightly dry | Dry | Parched |
| Capillary refill | <2s | <2s | 2–3s | >3s or mottling |
| Skin turgor | Normal | Normal | Reduced | Very reduced (tenting) |
| Anterior fontanelle | Flat | Flat | Sunken | Very sunken |
| Heart rate | Normal | Mild tachycardia | Moderate tachycardia | Marked tachycardia |
| Systolic BP | Normal | Normal | Normal/low | Low (shock) |
| Urine output | Normal | Mildly reduced | Oliguria | Anuria |
| Weight loss | — | <5% | 5–10% | >10% |

### Differential Diagnoses — Do Not Miss

**Intussusception:** The most important differential in children aged 6–24 months. Ileocaecal intussusception (one bowel segment telescopes into another) causes: intermittent paroxysmal abdominal pain (child draws up legs, screams, then recovers completely — the "peaks and troughs" pattern), vomiting (initially reflex, later bilious if obstruction), and **redcurrant jelly stool** (blood + mucus — a late sign indicating mucosal ischaemia). A sausage-shaped mass may be palpable in the right upper quadrant. USS: **target sign** (concentric rings — the intussusceptum within the intussuscipiens). Management: pneumatic or hydrostatic air enema reduction (80% success rate); surgical if failed or peritonism.

**Pyloric stenosis:** Male infants, onset 2–8 weeks of age. Projectile non-bilious (never bilious — the obstruction is above the ampulla of Vater) vomiting after every feed, hungry immediately after vomiting. **Palpable olive-shaped mass** in the right epigastrium (pathognomonic). Biochemistry: hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis — loss of HCl in vomit + secondary hyperaldosteronism → Na⁺ retained, K⁺ and H⁺ lost in urine; paradoxical aciduria (kidney excretes H⁺ to save Na⁺ despite alkalosis). Diagnosis: USS (pyloric muscle thickness >4 mm, channel length >14 mm). Management: correct electrolytes BEFORE surgery (ramstedt pyloromyotomy is safe and definitive but should not be performed in an alkalotic infant).

**Malrotation with volvulus:** Bilious vomiting in a neonate = surgical emergency until proven otherwise. Malrotation of the midgut creates a narrow mesenteric pedicle susceptible to clockwise twisting (midgut volvulus) → SMA occlusion → ischaemia of the entire midgut within hours. Upper GI contrast study (contrast meal): abnormal position of the duodenojejunal flexure (normally to the left of L1 and above the antrum). Ladd's procedure (untwisting + widening of mesenteric root + appendicectomy).

## Diagnosis

**Gastroenteritis is a clinical diagnosis** — investigations are not required in most cases.

**When to investigate:**
- Signs of severe dehydration or shock → U&E (Na, K, bicarbonate, urea), glucose
- Bloody diarrhoea → stool culture (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, STEC E. coli O157)
- Prolonged illness (>7 days) → stool for ova, cysts, and parasites (Giardia, Cryptosporidium)
- Suspected hypernatraemic dehydration → Na (>150 mEq/L requires slow rehydration)
- Infants <3 months → blood culture + consider LP (sepsis can present as GE in young infants)

**Urinalysis:** Ketones (starvation/dehydration), specific gravity. Not specific for dehydration severity.

## Management

### Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

**First-line for mild–moderate dehydration.** Commercially prepared ORS (oral rehydration salts — Dioralyte, Pedialyte) contains Na 60–75 mEq/L, K 20 mEq/L, bicarbonate, and glucose 111–139 mg/dL. The glucose-sodium co-transporter in the small intestinal epithelium (SGLT1) is unaffected by most enteric pathogens — glucose absorption drives sodium absorption and water follows osmotically.

**Do NOT use:** Plain water (hyponatraemic), diluted squash, cola, sports drinks, or fruit juice — these have inappropriate osmolality and electrolyte content and can worsen hyponatraemia or osmotic diarrhoea.

**Protocol (NICE/ESPGHAN):**
- 5% dehydration: 50 mL/kg ORS over 4 hours + ongoing replacement (5–10 mL/kg per additional watery stool, 2 mL/kg per vomit)
- If child vomits during ORT: wait 5–10 minutes, then resume at a slower rate (5 mL every 1–2 minutes by syringe)
- **Resume normal feeds within 4 hours of starting ORT** — early refeeding (including breast milk and formula) reduces the duration of diarrhoea and does NOT worsen illness

### IV Fluid Management

**Indications for IV fluids:**
- ORT fails (persistent vomiting, clinical deterioration)
- Severe dehydration (>10% or haemodynamic compromise)
- Altered consciousness

**NICE guidance on IV rehydration in children:**
- Use **0.9% NaCl with 5% dextrose + 20 mEq/L KCl** (isotonic maintenance fluid)
- Do NOT use hypotonic fluids (0.45% NaCl, 4% dextrose/0.18% NaCl) — risk of **hospital-acquired hyponatraemic encephalopathy** (especially post-surgical and ill children)
- Fluid deficit replacement over 24 hours (add deficit to maintenance)
- For haemodynamic compromise (shock): 10–20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl IV bolus; reassess after each bolus

**Hypernatraemic dehydration (Na >150 mEq/L):** Rehydrate slowly over 48 hours (maximum rate of Na fall: 0.5 mEq/L/hour or 10–12 mEq/L/24h). Rapid correction causes brain cells to swell (cerebral oedema) as water enters previously adapted cells, causing seizures and death.

**Glucose monitoring:** Children with gastroenteritis and poor oral intake are at risk of hypoglycaemia (limited glycogen stores). Check glucose (normal 72–144 mg/dL in children) and add dextrose to IV fluids as needed.

### Antimicrobials

**Most gastroenteritis does NOT require antibiotics** — viral and most bacterial causes are self-limiting.

**Antibiotic indications in paediatric gastroenteritis:**
- Confirmed Shigella (resistance-guided: azithromycin or ciprofloxacin)
- Salmonella in high-risk children: infants <3 months, immunocompromised, sickle cell disease, bacteraemia (amoxicillin or azithromycin)
- Confirmed Giardia or Cryptosporidium in immunocompromised (metronidazole, nitazoxanide)
- STEC E. coli O157 (HUS risk): antibiotics are CONTRAINDICATED — increase risk of HUS by releasing Shiga toxin from lysed bacteria

### Rotavirus and Vaccination

Rotavirus is the most common cause of severe dehydration from gastroenteritis in young children globally. The oral rotavirus vaccines (Rotarix 2 doses at 8 and 12 weeks; RotaTeq 3 doses) reduce severe gastroenteritis hospitalisation by 80–90%. The vaccine significantly changed the epidemiology in countries with vaccine programmes.

## Complications

- **Hypernatraemic dehydration:** Brain shrinkage → bridging vein rupture → subdural haemorrhage; cerebral oedema on rapid correction → seizures, death
- **Hyponatraemic dehydration:** Rare in typical GE; caused by inappropriate hypotonic fluid administration — cerebral oedema → seizures
- **Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS):** STEC E. coli O157 gastroenteritis (bloody diarrhoea) → Shiga toxin → microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (Hb <10 g/dL, schistocytes on blood film) + thrombocytopenia (<150 × 10⁹/L) + AKI (creatinine rise) → dialysis in up to 50% of children with HUS; avoid antibiotics, NSAIDs, antidiarrhoeal agents
- **Intussusception:** Bowel ischaemia → perforation if untreated
- **Post-infectious lactose intolerance:** Transient lactase deficiency after viral enteritis; usually resolves in 4–6 weeks

## Clinical Insight

The weight-based assessment of dehydration is the most objective tool available — if you have a recent weight, use it. A 1 kg weight loss = 1 L of fluid deficit in a child, and this translates directly into your rehydration plan. Clinical signs are important but subjective; two observers can assess CRT differently. A documented weight from a recent well-child appointment provides objective ground truth.

Pyloric stenosis is always non-bilious vomiting. The obstruction is at the pylorus, proximal to the bile duct entry at the ampulla of Vater. Bilious (green or yellow) vomiting in a neonate is a surgical emergency — malrotation with volvulus until proven otherwise. The distinction between bilious and non-bilious vomiting is therefore one of the most clinically important observations in all of neonatal medicine and should be elicited from every parent of a vomiting neonate.

Redcurrant jelly stool in a distressed child aged 6–24 months with a history of intermittent colicky pain is intussusception until proven otherwise. The "currant jelly" appearance (dark blood mixed with mucus) is actually a late sign — by the time it appears, the bowel has been compromised for some time. Early intussusception has no bloody stool — just the characteristic peaks-and-troughs pain pattern of intermittent obstruction. Act on the clinical history, not on the absence of rectal bleeding.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 14-month-old girl, 48h watery diarrhoea + 3 vomits, last urinated 9h ago. HR 158, dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes and fontanelle, skin turgor reduced, CRT 3 seconds. Weight 10.2 kg (was 11 kg = 800g = ~800 mL deficit = ~7.3% dehydration).

**Step 1 — Assess dehydration severity:** Using NICE criteria:
- CRT 3 seconds (amber-to-red boundary), sunken fontanelle, dry membranes, reduced turgor = **moderate-to-severe dehydration** (~7–10%)
- No shock yet (BP not mentioned as low, still irritable not lethargic) = moderate dehydration

**Step 2 — Attempt oral rehydration:** Despite severity, ORT should be tried first if the child is not shocked:
- 50 mL/kg ORS over 4 hours = 510 mL = 128 mL/hour
- Offer by syringe or spoon, 5 mL every 2–3 minutes to minimise vomiting
- If vomiting continues and ORT fails → escalate to IV

**Step 3 — IV fluids (ORT likely to fail in this case):** CRT of 3 seconds + prolonged anuria (9h) + sunken fontanelle → this child is at the severe end of moderate dehydration and likely cannot tolerate adequate ORT:
- IV access + 10 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl bolus for haemodynamic stabilisation (reassess CRT after)
- Calculate total fluid need: 800 mL deficit + 100 mL/kg/24h maintenance (1100 mL) = 1900 mL over 24 hours
- Use 0.9% NaCl + 5% dextrose + KCl 20 mEq/L (isotonic — never hypotonic)
- Check Na: if Na >150 mEq/L, extend rehydration to 48h
- Monitor glucose: check BM (hypoglycaemia risk with poor intake)

**Step 4 — Consider differential diagnoses:**
- Is this actually intussusception? The diarrhoea history is straightforward and watery (not redcurrant jelly), with no colicky peaks-and-troughs. But if the clinical response to fluids is poor or the child deteriorates, USS abdomen is appropriate.
- Is the cause bacterial? Watery diarrhoea without blood makes STEC E. coli and Shigella less likely. Rotavirus most probable. No stool culture needed routinely.

**Step 5 — Resume feeds within 4 hours of starting ORT/IV rehydration:** Early refeeding reduces diarrhoea duration by 1 day. There is no justification for prolonged "bowel rest" — it delays recovery and causes catabolism.

**Exam trigger:** *14-month-old + 48h watery diarrhoea + HR 158 + CRT 3s + sunken fontanelle + weight loss* → moderate-severe dehydration requiring IV rehydration. Use 0.9% NaCl + 5% dextrose — NEVER give hypotonic fluids (0.45% NaCl or 4%/0.18%) to a sick child with gastroenteritis. The distractor is ordering hypotonic "maintenance" fluids, which can cause hyponatraemia and cerebral oedema in the wrong clinical context.
