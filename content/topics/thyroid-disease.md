## Overview

Thyroid disease encompasses a spectrum from hypothyroidism (deficiency of thyroid hormone) to hyperthyroidism (excess), and from benign structural disease (goitre, nodules) to malignancy. The thyroid produces T4 (thyroxine — inactive prohormone) and T3 (triiodothyronine — active form), regulated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid (HPT) axis. TSH is the most sensitive indicator of thyroid status and is the appropriate first investigation in almost all situations.

## Hyperthyroidism

### Pathophysiology

**Graves' disease** (most common cause of hyperthyroidism, ~75%): Autoimmune condition in which IgG antibodies (**TSH receptor antibodies, TRAb**) bind to and stimulate the TSH receptor on follicular cells → autonomous, TSH-independent thyroid hormone synthesis. TRAb also cross-reacts with orbital fibroblast TSH receptors → orbital inflammation, adipogenesis, glycosaminoglycan deposition → **Graves' ophthalmopathy**. The pretibial myxoedema (also called thyroid dermopathy) is a similar fibroblast-mediated process in the skin.

**Toxic multinodular goitre (TMNG)**: Autonomously functioning nodules in a previously euthyroid multinodular gland. Develop after years of TSH stimulation. Common in iodine-deficient areas and in older patients. No ophthalmopathy.

**Toxic adenoma (Plummer's disease)**: Single autonomously functioning adenoma with a somatic TSH receptor gain-of-function mutation. "Hot nodule" on radioiodine uptake scan.

**Thyroiditis** (subacute de Quervain's, postpartum, painless/silent): Inflammatory destruction releases stored thyroid hormone → transient hyperthyroidism → hypothyroid phase → recovery. Radioiodine uptake is characteristically **low** (distinguishes from Graves' where uptake is elevated — the gland is not actively synthesising, just leaking stored hormone).

**Drug-induced**: Amiodarone (contains 37% iodine by weight — causes both hypo- and hyperthyroidism). Iodinated contrast. Excess iodine supplementation.

**Jod-Basedow phenomenon**: Iodine-induced hyperthyroidism in patients with pre-existing thyroid autonomy (multinodular goitre, subclinical Graves'). When a large iodine load is given (amiodarone, IV contrast) to a gland with autonomous nodules that have escaped the normal regulatory mechanisms, the substrate for synthesis overwhelms suppression → hyperthyroidism. The same large iodine load in a **normal thyroid** causes the Wolff-Chaikoff effect (transient suppression of organification → brief hypothyroidism), from which the normal gland escapes within 1–2 weeks. Autonomous tissue cannot escape — it becomes hyperthyroid instead.

**Amiodarone-induced thyroid disease (AIT)**: Two distinct types requiring different treatment:
- **Type 1 AIT**: Excess iodine-driven synthesis in an abnormal gland (Jod-Basedow). Treat with carbimazole + potassium perchlorate (to block iodine uptake).
- **Type 2 AIT**: Destructive thyroiditis from direct amiodarone cytotoxicity. Treat with prednisolone. Thyroid uptake scan: **low uptake** (not synthesising — only leaking). Often self-limiting.

### Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms of thyroid hormone excess**: Palpitations, heat intolerance, weight loss despite increased appetite, tremor, anxiety, irritability, hyperactivity, diarrhoea, menstrual irregularity (oligomenorrhoea), proximal muscle weakness.

**Signs**: Tachycardia, AF (in 10–15%), warm moist skin, fine tremor, proximal myopathy, onycholysis, hair thinning.

**Graves'-specific features (not present in other causes):**
- **Diffuse smooth goitre** with bruit (increased vascularity)
- **Graves' ophthalmopathy**: Proptosis (exophthalmos), periorbital oedema, conjunctival injection, chemosis, lid lag, lid retraction, diplopia (restriction of extraocular muscles), corneal exposure
- **Pretibial myxoedema**: Non-pitting, indurated, orange-peel textured plaques on the shins
- **Thyroid acropachy**: Digital clubbing (rare)

### Thyroid Storm (Thyrotoxic Crisis)

Life-threatening exacerbation of hyperthyroidism — precipitated by surgery, infection, trauma, radioiodine, iodinated contrast, or withdrawal of antithyroid drugs. Burch-Wartofsky scoring system for diagnosis.

**Features**: High fever (often >40°C), severe tachycardia/AF/heart failure, agitation, confusion, vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice, coma.

**Treatment (the 6-step protocol):**
1. **PTU** or high-dose methimazole — blocks new synthesis (PTU also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion — preferred in thyroid storm)
2. **Saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI) or Lugol's iodine** — given at least 1 hour AFTER PTU (blocks release of stored thyroid hormone — the Wolff-Chaikoff effect). **Never give iodine before PTU** — iodine provides substrate for further synthesis.
3. **Propranolol** — rate control and blockade of peripheral T4→T3 conversion
4. **Hydrocortisone/dexamethasone** — reduces T4→T3 conversion; treats relative adrenal insufficiency
5. **Paracetamol** — for fever (NOT aspirin — displaces T4 from TBG → worsens thyrotoxicosis)
6. **Treat the precipitant**

### Management of Hyperthyroidism

**Antithyroid drugs (ATDs):**
- **Carbimazole** (first-line in UK) or **propylthiouracil (PTU)** — inhibit thyroid peroxidase, blocking hormone synthesis. PTU also blocks peripheral T4→T3 conversion.
- Monitor: agranulocytosis is a rare but life-threatening complication (~0.3%). Warn all patients: sore throat → stop the drug and seek an urgent FBC. Routine FBC monitoring does not reliably prevent agranulocytosis.
- **Titration regimen**: Carbimazole dose titrated to maintain euthyroidism. Remission rate after 12–18 months of treatment: ~50%.
- **Block-and-replace regimen**: High-dose carbimazole + levothyroxine replacement. Better compliance but more side effects.

**Radioactive iodine (RAI — ¹³¹I)**:
- Definitive treatment — ablates thyroid tissue over 6–12 weeks
- Most patients become hypothyroid (accept and prescribe levothyroxine)
- **Contraindications**: Pregnancy, breastfeeding, moderate-severe active Graves' ophthalmopathy (can worsen eye disease — pre-treat with steroids if used)
- Avoid pregnancy for 6 months post-RAI

**Surgery (thyroidectomy)**:
- Total thyroidectomy for large goitres, compressive symptoms, malignancy suspected, patient preference, or failed medical treatment
- Risks: hypoparathyroidism (hypocalcaemia — most common complication), recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (hoarseness), hypothyroidism (planned)

## Hypothyroidism

### Pathophysiology

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis** (most common cause in iodine-sufficient regions): Autoimmune destruction of thyroid follicular cells. Characterised by lymphocytic infiltration and anti-thyroid antibodies (**anti-TPO** most sensitive and specific, **anti-thyroglobulin**). Initially presents with transient hyperthyroidism ("Hashitoxicosis" — from follicular disruption releasing stored hormone), followed by hypothyroidism, followed in some by permanent hypothyroidism with a small atrophied gland.

**Other causes**: Post-RAI, post-thyroidectomy, iodine deficiency (most common worldwide cause), drugs (amiodarone, lithium, carbimazole, interferon-alpha), central hypothyroidism (TSH or TRH deficiency — pituitary/hypothalamic disease), congenital (cretinism — a leading cause of preventable intellectual disability).

**Sick euthyroid syndrome (non-thyroidal illness syndrome — NTIS)**: In any severe systemic illness (sepsis, major surgery, ICU admission, malnutrition, myocardial infarction), the peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 is impaired → low T3, with normal or low T4, and normal or low TSH. The body downregulates thyroid hormone activity as an adaptive response to conserve energy and reduce catabolism. The reverse T3 (rT3) — an inactive isomer — accumulates. Critically: **do not treat sick euthyroid syndrome with levothyroxine** — thyroid replacement does not improve outcomes in this context and may cause harm. The abnormal TFTs resolve when the underlying illness resolves. Distinguish from central hypothyroidism (also low TSH + low T4), which presents in a clinically different context (other pituitary deficiency signs, history of pituitary disease) and requires treatment.

### Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms of thyroid hormone deficiency**: Cold intolerance, weight gain, fatigue, constipation, dry skin, dry/coarse hair, hair loss (including lateral third of eyebrows), bradycardia, menorrhagia, depression, cognitive slowing, muscle cramps, carpal tunnel syndrome.

**Signs**: Bradycardia, dry skin, periorbital puffiness, macroglossia, coarse facial features, slow-relaxing reflexes ("hung-up reflexes" — delay in the relaxation phase of the ankle jerk), goitre (in Hashimoto's — firm, rubbery), hypothyroid coma (myxoedema coma).

**Myxoedema coma**: Decompensated hypothyroidism — hypothermia, bradycardia, hypotension, hypoventilation, hyponatraemia, depressed consciousness. Precipitated by cold exposure, infection, sedatives, surgery. Medical emergency — ICU, IV T4 or T3, IV hydrocortisone (to prevent precipitating adrenal crisis from increased metabolism). Mortality ~30–40%.

**Biochemical features of hypothyroidism**: Raised TSH (primary), low free T4, raised cholesterol, raised CK (muscle involvement), hyponatraemia (SIADH — uncommon).

### Management

**Levothyroxine (L-T4)**:
- Start 50–100 µg daily (25 µg if elderly, cardiac disease, or severe hypothyroidism — risk of precipitating angina or AF)
- Increase by 25 µg every 4–6 weeks guided by TSH
- Take on an empty stomach, 30–60 minutes before food (absorption reduced by food, calcium, iron, PPIs)
- Target TSH: 0.5–2.5 mU/L in most patients (allow higher in elderly/frail: 1–4 mU/L)
- Recheck TSH 6–8 weeks after each dose change

**Liothyronine (L-T3)**: Not routinely recommended. Some patients on adequate L-T4 with normal TSH remain symptomatic — a small subgroup benefit from combination T4 + T3 therapy. Refer to endocrinology.

## Thyroid Nodules and Cancer

**Evaluation of a thyroid nodule:**
1. **TSH first**: If low (subclinical/overt hyperthyroidism) → radioiodine uptake scan (hot nodule = functioning, almost never malignant)
2. If TSH normal or high → **ultrasound** (characterise nodule — size, echogenicity, calcification, vascularity, margins, lymph nodes)
3. **Fine needle aspiration cytology (FNAC/FNA)** — for nodules with suspicious USS features or size >1 cm. Bethesda classification of cytology (I–VI).

**Red flag features suggesting malignancy**: Male sex, age <20 or >70, rapid growth, hoarseness (RLN involvement), dysphagia, hard/fixed nodule, cervical lymphadenopathy, family history of MEN2 or thyroid cancer, prior neck irradiation.

**Thyroid cancer types:**

| Type | Frequency | Key Features | Prognosis |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| Papillary | ~80% | Best prognosis; lymphatic spread; "Orphan Annie eye" nuclei; psammoma bodies; BRAF V600E mutation | Excellent (~98% 5-yr survival) |
| Follicular | ~10% | Haematogenous spread (bone, lung); thick capsule invasion; FNA cannot distinguish from follicular adenoma | Good |
| Medullary | ~5% | Parafollicular C-cells; secretes **calcitonin**; RET proto-oncogene mutation; MEN2A/2B association; screen family | Moderate |
| Anaplastic | ~2% | Most aggressive; elderly; rapidly fatal | Very poor (median survival <6 months) |

**Medullary thyroid cancer and calcitonin**: Elevated calcitonin is the tumour marker. RET mutation testing in all medullary thyroid cancers — screen first-degree relatives. Prophylactic thyroidectomy in RET-positive relatives before cancer develops.

**Treatment**: Total thyroidectomy (most thyroid cancers) ± RAI ablation (papillary and follicular) ± TSH-suppressive levothyroxine therapy (high-dose — reduces TSH-driven tumour growth). Lifelong thyroglobulin monitoring for recurrence (papillary/follicular).
## Complications

**Hyperthyroidism**: Atrial fibrillation, heart failure, osteoporosis (accelerated bone turnover), corneal exposure and visual loss (severe Graves' ophthalmopathy), tracheal compression (large goitre), thyroid storm.

**Hypothyroidism**: Dyslipidaemia and accelerated atherosclerosis, carpal tunnel syndrome, pericardial effusion, myxoedema coma.

**Treatment complications**: Agranulocytosis (ATDs), hypoparathyroidism (post-thyroidectomy), RLN injury (post-thyroidectomy), worsening ophthalmopathy (RAI).

## Clinical Insight

TSH is the single most informative thyroid test in the vast majority of clinical situations. A normal TSH essentially excludes clinically significant primary thyroid disease. Free T4 should be checked when TSH is abnormal, not as a routine screen. An isolated free T4 without a TSH is almost uninterpretable.

In thyroid storm, the sequence matters: PTU first, then iodine — never the reverse. Giving Lugol's iodine before blocking synthesis with PTU provides a massive substrate load for continued hormone production and can catastrophically worsen thyrotoxicosis. This is a pharmacological principle with potentially fatal consequences if violated.

Amiodarone causes thyroid dysfunction in up to 15–20% of patients — both hypo- and hyperthyroidism. Amiodarone-induced hyperthyroidism (AIH) is particularly difficult to manage — it has two types (Type 1: excess iodine-stimulated synthesis in an abnormal gland; Type 2: destructive thyroiditis from direct amiodarone toxicity), which require different treatments. Thyroid function must be checked before starting amiodarone and every 6 months thereafter.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 31-year-old woman with 3 months of palpitations, heat intolerance, weight loss despite good appetite, tremor, and irregular periods. Diffuse smooth goitre, lid lag, bilateral proptosis, pretibial myxoedema. HR 108, irregularly irregular. Undetectable TSH, markedly elevated free T4.

**Step 1 — Diagnose Graves' disease:** Three features are specific to Graves' disease and appear together in this patient:
1. **Diffuse smooth goitre** (from TSH-receptor antibody stimulation)
2. **Proptosis/exophthalmos** (Graves' ophthalmopathy — autoimmune infiltration of retroorbital tissue)
3. **Pretibial myxoedema** (glycosaminoglycan deposition in skin)
This triad = **Graves' disease** until proven otherwise. Confirm with TSH-receptor antibodies (TRAb/TSHR-Ab).

**Step 2 — Address the AF immediately:** Irregular pulse at 108 bpm = atrial fibrillation, the commonest arrhythmia from hyperthyroidism. Rate control is the immediate priority:
- **Propranolol 40 mg TDS** (or atenolol) — controls both HR and thyrotoxic symptoms (tremor, anxiety, palpitations). Start now.
- Cardioversion is NOT appropriate yet — AF is almost certainly secondary to hyperthyroidism and will often resolve spontaneously once euthyroid

**Step 3 — Start antithyroid drug:** Carbimazole 20–40 mg OD (titration regimen: titrate down as TFTs normalise, or block-and-replace: fixed high dose + add levothyroxine once levels fall). Typically takes 4–8 weeks to achieve euthyroidism. Check FBC before starting — 0.1–0.3% risk of agranulocytosis; warn patient to attend urgently if they develop fever or sore throat.

**Step 4 — Definitive treatment:** Three options for Graves' disease:
- **Radioiodine (RAI)** — first-line in most adults; permanent and effective; avoid in active significant ophthalmopathy (worsens eye disease)
- **Total thyroidectomy** — first-line if large goitre, compressive symptoms, or patient preference; also preferred in active significant ophthalmopathy
- **Long-term antithyroid drugs** — if patient declines RAI/surgery; remission rate ~50% after 12–18 months

**Step 5 — Ophthalmopathy management:** Bilateral proptosis = active Graves' ophthalmopathy. Refer to ophthalmology. Severe active disease: **IV methylprednisolone** (orbital decompression in selected cases). Avoid RAI if active moderate-severe ophthalmopathy.

**Exam trigger:** *Proptosis + pretibial myxoedema + diffuse goitre + hyperthyroidism* → Graves' disease. These three features together are pathognomonic. Do not confuse with toxic multinodular goitre (no proptosis, no pretibial myxoedema).
