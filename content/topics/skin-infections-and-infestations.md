## Overview

Skin infections are among the most common reasons for acute medical presentation. They range from superficial, self-limiting conditions (impetigo, tinea, pityriasis versicolor) to life-threatening surgical emergencies (necrotising fasciitis). The clinical priority in all cases is to recognise the depth and severity of infection, identify the causative organism, and differentiate infectious from non-infectious mimics. In diabetic and immunocompromised patients, the threshold for aggressive investigation and treatment must be lower. **First Aid high-yield:** Know the classic morphology, distribution, and treatment for each infection — these are highly tested as clinical vignettes.

## Cellulitis and Erysipelas

### Pathophysiology

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the dermis and subcutaneous tissue. Erysipelas affects the upper dermis and superficial lymphatics, producing a raised, sharply demarcated edge (because the inflammatory oedema tracks along the lymphatic channels and creates a distinct boundary with normal skin).

**Causative organisms:**
- **Group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus** (*Streptococcus pyogenes*) — most common cause of both erysipelas and cellulitis
- **Staphylococcus aureus** — cellulitis, particularly when associated with a wound, abscess, or folliculitis
- **Gram-negative organisms** — in diabetics, immunosuppressed, water-related injuries (*Aeromonas* from freshwater, *Vibrio vulnificus* from saltwater/raw shellfish — **FA buzzword:** *Vibrio vulnificus* cellulitis in a cirrhotic patient who ate raw oysters = classic stem)
- **Haemophilus influenzae** — facial cellulitis in children (pre-vaccination era; rare now)

Entry points: tinea pedis, wounds, abrasions, insect bites, ulcers — identify and treat the portal of entry to prevent recurrence.

### Clinical Presentation

**Erysipelas**: Bright red, raised, hot, tender plaque with a **sharply defined advancing edge** — this sharp border is the distinguishing clinical feature. Mainly affects the face (butterfly distribution) or lower leg. Systemic features common — fever, rigors, malaise. Lymphangitis (red streaking) and regional lymphadenopathy may be present.

**Cellulitis**: Diffuse, non-raised erythema, warmth, swelling, and tenderness with a **less well-defined edge**. Lower limb most common site. Blistering and skin necrosis in severe cases.

**Severity classification (Eron classification):**

| Class | Features | Setting |
|-------|---------|---------|
| I | No systemic features; no significant comorbidity | Oral antibiotics at home |
| II | Mild-moderate systemic features OR significant comorbidity | Oral or IV antibiotics; may need admission |
| III | Severe systemic features; unstable comorbidity; involvement of special sites (face, periorbital, perineal) | IV antibiotics inpatient |
| IV | Sepsis; life-threatening | ICU; exclude necrotising fasciitis |

**Marking the edge of erythema** with a skin-safe marker pen at the time of assessment is essential to objectively document progression. Reassess at 24–48 hours.

**Differential diagnosis** — particularly important before prescribing antibiotics:
- **Deep vein thrombosis** — unilateral leg swelling, erythema; Doppler USS
- **Lipodermatosclerosis** — chronic, bilateral, inverted champagne bottle legs; no fever; chronic venous insufficiency
- **Contact dermatitis/eczema** — itchy, no fever, often bilateral
- **Gout** — acute monoarthritis, periarticular rather than skin, urate crystals
- **Erythema migrans (Lyme disease)** — expanding annular rash with central clearing after tick bite; treat immediately with doxycycline; **do not wait for serology** if classical rash
- **Eosinophilic cellulitis (Wells syndrome)** — eosinophilia, recurrent; biopsy diagnostic

### Management

**Antibiotics:**
- **Eron I/II**: Oral **flucloxacillin** 500 mg–1 g QDS (covers both streptococci and staphylococci). Clarithromycin or cefalexin if penicillin allergic.
- **Eron III/IV**: IV **benzylpenicillin** (streptococcal cellulitis) or IV **flucloxacillin** (staphylococcal/wound-related). Add IV vancomycin or linezolid if MRSA is suspected.
- Duration: 5–7 days for mild, 10–14 days for severe. Review at 48 hours.

**Treat the portal of entry**: Tinea pedis is the most common entry portal for recurrent lower leg cellulitis — always examine the interdigital spaces, and treat tinea with topical or oral antifungal.

**Limb elevation** — reduces oedema, accelerates resolution.

**Recurrent cellulitis prophylaxis**: ≥2 episodes in 12 months → consider long-term prophylactic phenoxymethylpenicillin 250 mg BD (or erythromycin if penicillin allergic) indefinitely.

## Necrotising Fasciitis

A rapidly progressive, life-threatening bacterial infection of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue, producing widespread tissue necrosis. Mortality 20–40% even with treatment.

**Types:**
- **Type I** (polymicrobial): Mixed aerobic and anaerobic organisms — diabetics, elderly, post-surgical. Gas-producing organisms → crepitus. **FA:** Fournier's gangrene = necrotising fasciitis of the perineum/scrotum
- **Type II** (monomicrobial): Group A *Streptococcus* — young, healthy patients; no portal of entry often identifiable ("strep toxic shock")

**Clinical hallmark — pain out of proportion to skin findings.** Early: erythema, swelling, warmth (appears as cellulitis). Later: dusky discolouration, blistering, skin necrosis, **crepitus** (gas in tissue — pathognomonic), loss of sensation (nerve destruction). Systemic toxicity — fever, tachycardia, hypotension.

**The "hard sign"**: Crepitus on palpation — indicates gas-forming organisms and is an absolute surgical emergency. The "soft sign" that must not be ignored: pain out of proportion to the skin appearance.

**Laboratory risk indicator for necrotising fasciitis (LRINEC score)**: CRP, WBC, Hb, Na, creatinine, glucose — score ≥6 suggests high probability; ≥8 = high risk. Useful but not sensitive enough to exclude necrotising fasciitis in high clinical suspicion.

**Management**: **Immediate surgical debridement** is the only definitive treatment — do not delay for imaging if clinical suspicion is high. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (meropenem + clindamycin + vancomycin) — adjunctive, not a substitute for surgery. ICU admission. Consider hyperbaric oxygen at specialist centres.

## Impetigo

Superficial bacterial skin infection — most common in children. 

- **Non-bullous impetigo**: *S. aureus* or *Streptococcus pyogenes* — **golden (honey-coloured) crusted lesions** on the face, particularly around the nose and mouth. Highly contagious. **FA mnemonic:** "honey-coloured crust" = impetigo
- **Bullous impetigo**: *S. aureus* only (exfoliative toxins cleave **desmoglein-1**) — fragile, clear-fluid bullae that rupture leaving a thin honey-coloured crust. Same toxin mechanism as Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS) but localised.

**Staphylococcal Scalded Skin Syndrome (SSSS)**: Generalised exfoliative toxin-mediated disease — widespread superficial skin peeling in neonates and young children. Positive Nikolsky sign. **Distinguished from TEN by** age (children vs adults), no mucosal involvement (toxin doesn't reach mucosae), and the cleavage plane (subcorneal vs. full-thickness epidermal necrosis in TEN). Treat with IV flucloxacillin.

**Management of impetigo**: Topical **fusidic acid** (first-line for localised); topical **mupirocin** (for MRSA-positive or fusidic acid-resistant). Oral **flucloxacillin** for widespread disease or systemic features. School exclusion until lesions have crusted over or 48 hours of antibiotics.

## Folliculitis

Infection of the hair follicle — usually *Staphylococcus aureus*. Presents as small, erythematous papules and pustules around follicles, often on the scalp, face, thighs, or buttocks.

**Hot tub folliculitis** (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*): Develops 1–4 days after exposure to inadequately chlorinated hot tubs or swimming pools. Pruritic papulopustular rash, predominantly on areas covered by swimwear. Usually self-limiting; ciprofloxacin for severe cases.

**Eosinophilic folliculitis**: In HIV-positive patients with CD4 <200 cells/μL — intensely pruritic papules on the face, trunk, and proximal extremities. Treat underlying HIV; topical steroids, antihistamines.

**Gram-negative folliculitis**: Sudden worsening of acne after prolonged oral antibiotic use (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Proteus*). Treat with isotretinoin, which eliminates the gram-negative bacteria by reducing sebum.

## Tinea (Dermatophytosis)

Superficial fungal infections caused by dermatophytes (*Trichophyton*, *Microsporum*, *Epidermophyton*) — invade only keratinised tissue (skin, hair, nails).

| Site | Name | Appearance |
|------|------|-----------|
| Scalp | Tinea capitis | Scaly patches + alopecia; kerion = inflammatory boggy mass (T. tonsurans most common in UK) |
| Body | Tinea corporis | Annular, scaly plaque with **active raised edge** and central clearing — "ring worm" |
| Groin | Tinea cruris | Well-defined erythema with scaly edge in groin and inner thigh; spares scrotum (helps differentiate from candidiasis) |
| Foot | Tinea pedis (athlete's foot) | Interdigital maceration, scaling, and fissuring; moccasin pattern; most common portal of entry for leg cellulitis |
| Nails | Onychomycosis | Subungual hyperkeratosis, onycholysis, yellow-brown discolouration |

**Diagnosis**: 
- Skin scrapings for mycology: **KOH preparation** — hyphae visible as branching filaments; culture for species identification
- **Wood's lamp**: Tinea capitis (Microsporum) fluoresces **blue-green** under Wood's lamp; however *T. tonsurans* (most common UK cause) does NOT fluoresce — so Wood's lamp negative does not exclude tinea capitis
- Nail clippings sent in dry paper

**Management:**
- Topical antifungals (clotrimazole, terbinafine cream) for tinea corporis, cruris, and pedis — 2–4 weeks
- **Oral terbinafine** for onychomycosis (6 weeks for fingernails, 12 weeks for toenails; check LFTs before)
- **Oral terbinafine or griseofulvin** for tinea capitis (topical ineffective — oral required; treat all household contacts)

## Pityriasis Versicolor (Tinea Versicolor)

**First Aid high-yield** — commonly tested. Superficial fungal infection caused by **Malassezia furfur** (*Malassezia globosa*) — a commensal lipophilic yeast that converts to its hyphal form in certain conditions (heat, humidity, sweating, immunosuppression, corticosteroid use, oily skin).

**Clinical features**: Hypo- or hyperpigmented (or salmon-coloured) macules with fine scale, predominantly on the **upper trunk, neck, and upper arms**. Lesions appear darker on pale skin (hyperpigmented form) and lighter on tanned or dark skin (hypopigmented form — tanning is impaired in affected areas). Fine powdery scale when lesion is scratched. **Non-inflammatory** — itch is minimal or absent.

**Diagnosis**:
- **KOH preparation**: "**Spaghetti and meatballs**" appearance — short hyphae (spaghetti) and yeast spores (meatballs). This is a pathognomonic First Aid buzzword.
- Wood's lamp: **Pale yellow-green fluorescence** (less reliable than KOH).
- Clinically: distribution, scale, and colour change pattern are characteristic.

**Management**:
- **Topical selenium sulfide 2.5%** shampoo (apply to affected areas, leave 10 min, wash off) or topical azoles (ketoconazole 2% shampoo/cream) — first-line
- **Oral itraconazole** (200 mg/day for 5–7 days) — for extensive disease or recurrence; oral fluconazole also effective
- Counsel patients: **skin colour normalisation is slow** (weeks to months after successful treatment) — the pigmentary change is not an infection residue but a post-inflammatory process

## Scabies

Infestation by *Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis* — a mite that burrows into the stratum corneum. Transmitted by prolonged skin-to-skin contact (>10 minutes of direct contact). Approximately 300 million cases worldwide per year (OHCM). Common in conditions of poverty and overcrowding.

**Clinical features**: Intense pruritus, **worse at night** (when the mite is most active), sparing the face in immunocompetent adults. **Burrows** — pathognomonic — short, tortuous, linear tracks with a visible white dot at the advancing end (the mite) — in the **web spaces of the fingers, wrists, nipples, genitalia, and periumbilical area**. Secondary eczematisation and excoriation. In infants, the face and scalp are often involved.

**First Aid buzzword**: "Burrows in web spaces of fingers, nocturnal pruritus, entire household affected" = scabies.

**Crusted (Norwegian) scabies**: Hyperkeratotic, crusted plaques covering large areas — occurs in immunocompromised patients (HIV, transplant, elderly). **Millions of mites** (vs ~10–15 in classical scabies). Highly contagious — airborne transmission possible. Public health emergency in healthcare settings.

**Diagnosis**: Clinical — burrow pattern and distribution. **Dermoscopy** (delta-wing jet sign — the mite at the end of the burrow, resembling a delta-wing aircraft). Skin scraping and microscopy confirming mites, eggs, or faeces (scybala).

**Management:**
- **Permethrin 5% cream** (first-line) — apply to the **whole body from the neck down**, wash off after 8–12 hours. Repeat after 7 days. Apply to face, scalp, and ears in elderly, immunocompromised, and infants.
- **Oral ivermectin** (alternative, unlicensed in UK) — for crusted scabies or treatment failure. Repeat after 7 days.
- Treat **all household contacts and sexual partners simultaneously** regardless of symptoms (incubation period means asymptomatic carriers exist).
- Wash all clothing, bedding, and towels at **60°C** on the treatment day.
- Pruritus may persist for **2–4 weeks after successful treatment** (hypersensitivity reaction to dead mites and their faeces) — reassure; prescribe antihistamines and mild steroid cream.

## Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

Reactivation of varicella-zoster virus (VZV) from dorsal root ganglia following primary varicella infection. Occurs when cell-mediated immunity wanes — with age, immunosuppression, or stress.

**Clinical features**: Prodromal pain (burning, hyperaesthesia) in a dermatomal distribution, typically **2–3 days before the rash**. Then vesicular eruption in a **dermatome — unilateral, does not cross the midline**. Common sites: thoracic (most common ~50% of cases), trigeminal (V1 — ophthalmic herpes zoster), and cervical.

**Herpes zoster ophthalmicus (HZO)**: V1 branch involvement — blistering on the forehead, nose, and periocular skin. **Hutchinson's sign** — vesicles on the tip or side of the nose (nasociliary branch innervates both the nasal tip and the cornea) → **indicates corneal involvement is likely**. Urgent ophthalmology referral even without visible eye lesions when Hutchinson's sign is present.

**Ramsay-Hunt syndrome**: VZV reactivation in the **geniculate ganglion** → facial nerve palsy (CNVII) + vesicles in the external auditory canal and pinna + sensorineural hearing loss (CNVIII) + vertigo. **FA buzzword:** "Painful ear vesicles + ipsilateral facial palsy" = Ramsay Hunt. Treat with high-dose antivirals (aciclovir 800 mg 5× daily for 7 days) + prednisolone.

**Post-herpetic neuralgia (PHN)**: Persistent neuropathic pain in the affected dermatome beyond 90 days after the rash heals. More common in the elderly and with severe acute zoster. Treat with: tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline), gabapentin or pregabalin, topical lidocaine patches, capsaicin cream.

**Management:**
- Antivirals (aciclovir 800 mg 5× daily, or valaciclovir 1 g TDS for 7 days) — **start within 72 hours of rash onset**; reduces duration, severity, and risk of PHN. Also indicated beyond 72 hours if new lesions still appearing, HZO, Ramsay-Hunt, or immunocompromised.
- Pain management: paracetamol, NSAIDs, neuropathic agents for severe pain.
- **Shingrix vaccine** (recombinant zoster vaccine) — recommended for adults ≥50; 97% efficacy against shingles; highly effective for preventing PHN; two doses 2–6 months apart. Preferred over Zostavax (live attenuated).

## Erythema Multiforme

A reactive mucocutaneous condition — not a primary infection, but frequently triggered by infections (especially HSV) or drugs. Important to distinguish from SJS/TEN.

**Triggers**:
- **HSV** — most common cause (~70%); erythema multiforme often recurs with each HSV reactivation
- *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* — especially in children and young adults
- Drugs (less common than in SJS/TEN)

**Clinical features**:
- **Target (iris) lesions** — the pathognomonic finding: three concentric zones — central dark area (epidermal necrosis) + pale oedematous ring + outer erythematous ring. **FA buzzword:** "Target lesions" = erythema multiforme
- Predominantly on **extensor surfaces of extremities** (hands, elbows, knees) and the face — unlike SJS which involves trunk
- **Erythema multiforme minor** — skin only, minimal or no mucosal involvement
- **Erythema multiforme major** — mucosal involvement (oral, conjunctival, genital) + skin lesions; historically confused with SJS but now considered a distinct entity

**Management**:
- Identify and treat the trigger
- For recurrent HSV-triggered EM: **prophylactic aciclovir 400 mg BD** (suppressive therapy) dramatically reduces recurrence frequency
- Supportive care for mucosal involvement; systemic steroids are controversial

## Complications

- **Necrotising fasciitis**: death (30–40% mortality even with treatment), limb loss, ICU-level morbidity, prolonged reconstruction
- **Cellulitis**: recurrent episodes (lymphatic damage predisposes to further infections), bacteraemia, sepsis, very rarely necrotising fasciitis
- **Herpes zoster**: post-herpetic neuralgia (severe chronic pain, especially in elderly); Ramsay Hunt syndrome (facial palsy, deafness, vertigo); herpes zoster ophthalmicus (corneal scarring, iritis, glaucoma, permanent vision loss); disseminated zoster in immunosuppressed
- **Crusted (Norwegian) scabies**: affects immunosuppressed — hyperkeratotic plaques, millions of mites, highly contagious, resistant to single-treatment permethrin
- **Impetigo**: post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis (rare, unlike strep pharyngitis where it's more common), cellulitis, MRSA impetigo
- **Pityriasis versicolor**: recurrence very common; pigmentary changes persist months after successful treatment; no scarring

## Clinical Insight

Tinea pedis is the most important and most overlooked risk factor for recurrent lower leg cellulitis. The fissures between the toes provide a portal of entry for streptococcal infection. Treating the tinea — with topical terbinafine or clotrimazole applied consistently to the web spaces — is as important as the antibiotic for preventing recurrence. Every patient with lower leg cellulitis should have their interdigital spaces examined.

Necrotising fasciitis in its early stages is indistinguishable from cellulitis. The single most important clinical clue is **pain disproportionate to the skin appearance**. A patient who appears to have cellulitis but is in agonising pain, has systemic toxicity, or is not responding to 24 hours of IV antibiotics should be assumed to have necrotising fasciitis until proven otherwise. The consequences of delay are irreversible. Mark the skin, photograph it, and reassess frequently.

Post-herpetic neuralgia is best prevented, not treated. Starting antivirals within 72 hours of rash onset in patients over 50 significantly reduces the risk of PHN. The Shingrix vaccine should be offered proactively to all patients over 50 before they develop shingles — a 97% reduction in disease incidence is an extraordinary public health tool.

The "spaghetti and meatballs" KOH finding of pityriasis versicolor is a First Aid classic — a young patient with hypopigmented or salmon-coloured patches on the trunk that appear after sunbathing (because the tanned skin contrasts with the untanned affected patches), with fine scale and a positive KOH. Selenium sulfide shampoo is the high-yield treatment answer.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 45-year-old man with type 2 diabetes. Four-day history of rapidly expanding erythema, warmth, and swelling on the right lower leg. A 2 cm wound on his heel from a corn he picked at. Temperature 38.6°C, HR 104, BP 118/76. Erythema has a sharp, well-defined advancing edge; extended proximally 4 cm since yesterday morning.

**Step 1 — Classify the infection: erysipelas, not just cellulitis:** The **sharp, well-defined advancing edge** is the clinical hallmark of erysipelas — tracking along superficial lymphatics in the upper dermis. This contrasts with cellulitis, which has an ill-defined edge (deeper dermal/subcutaneous spread). Both are predominantly caused by Group A beta-haemolytic streptococcus. In a diabetic patient with a heel wound, *Staphylococcus aureus* co-infection and Gram-negative organisms are additional concerns. The entry portal is the heel wound — document and treat this specifically.

**Step 2 — Assess severity: this is Eron Class III (at minimum) — admit for IV antibiotics:** Eron classification: this patient has **systemic features** (temperature 38.6°C, HR 104) and **significant comorbidity** (type 2 diabetes — impaired immunity, slow wound healing, increased risk of deep tissue infection and necrotising fasciitis). The Eron III threshold is met. Additionally, the 4 cm proximal extension in <24 hours indicates rapidly progressing infection that is unlikely to respond to oral therapy alone. Admit; start IV antibiotics.

**Step 3 — Mark the edge of erythema and monitor closely:** **Mark the advancing edge with a skin-safe pen** at the time of assessment. Document the time. This is not optional — without a marked baseline, you cannot objectively assess whether the infection is responding to antibiotics over the next 24 hours. Photograph the leg. Reassess at 24 hours: if erythema is advancing beyond the mark despite IV antibiotics, suspect necrotising fasciitis and escalate immediately.

**Step 4 — Before starting antibiotics, consider necrotising fasciitis:** The combination of: diabetic patient + rapidly spreading infection + systemic features demands active exclusion of necrotising fasciitis. Ask: Is there pain out of proportion to the appearance? Any crepitus? Any skin discolouration (dusky, grey, blistering)? Any loss of sensation at the lesion? The heel wound is a plausible entry point. In necrotising fasciitis, the skin findings initially look like severe cellulitis — the clue is extreme pain beyond what the erythema alone would explain. If any suspicion → urgent surgical review; do not delay for imaging if the patient is deteriorating.

**Step 5 — IV antibiotics and portal of entry management:** For Eron III cellulitis/erysipelas in a diabetic with a wound: **IV benzylpenicillin** (streptococcal cover) + **IV flucloxacillin** (staphylococcal cover, given the wound). Add **IV vancomycin** if MRSA is suspected (prior MRSA colonisation, healthcare exposure, no improvement on standard therapy). Duration: 10–14 days total (IV until systemically improved, then step down to oral). For the heel wound: wound care (debride, irrigate, appropriate dressings) and assess for underlying osteomyelitis — X-ray foot (gas, bony erosion), MRI if clinical concern. Treat any underlying tinea pedis (most common portal of entry for recurrent lower leg cellulitis — examine the interdigital spaces). **Limb elevation** reduces oedema and accelerates resolution.

**Exam trigger:** *Rapidly expanding lower leg erythema with sharp advancing edge + fever + diabetic patient + wound entry portal* → **erysipelas/cellulitis, Eron Class III** → admit for IV antibiotics. **Mark the erythema edge with a pen** — mandatory. Diabetes + rapid spread = actively exclude **necrotising fasciitis** (pain out of proportion, crepitus, haemodynamic instability). The distractor is managing with oral antibiotics — Eron III requires IV therapy. The other distractor is missing the tinea pedis entry portal — always examine the interdigital spaces in lower leg cellulitis, because untreated tinea causes recurrence regardless of antibiotic efficacy.
