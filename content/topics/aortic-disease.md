## Pathophysiology

### Aortic Dissection

An intimal tear allows blood to penetrate the aortic media, creating a false lumen that propagates along the vessel wall. The driving forces are the pulse pressure (aortic wall stress is highest in the first 10cm of the ascending aorta — hence 90% of dissections start here) and preexisting medial weakness.

**Causes (Pathoma):**
- **Hypertension** — most common (chronic → hyaline arteriolosclerosis of vasa vasorum → media atrophy)
- **Connective tissue disorders** — Marfan syndrome (fibrillin-1 mutation → cystic medial necrosis), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (younger patients, <40)
- Bicuspid aortic valve, coarctation
- Cocaine use (acute hypertension)
- Inflammatory (Takayasu arteritis, giant cell arteritis — rare)

**Stanford Classification (clinical relevance):**
- **Type A** — involves ascending aorta (± descending). Surgical emergency — risk of retrograde dissection → aortic regurgitation, coronary occlusion (MI), cardiac tamponade, stroke
- **Type B** — involves descending aorta only (beyond left subclavian). Usually managed medically unless complications.

**DeBakey Classification:** Type I (whole aorta), Type II (ascending only), Type III (descending only).

**Complications:**
- Retrograde dissection → pericardial tamponade → PEA arrest
- AR → acute aortic regurgitation (AR murmur + pulmonary oedema)
- Coronary occlusion → MI (most commonly RCA → inferior MI)
- Stroke (carotid involvement)
- Mesenteric ischaemia (abdominal pain)
- Renal failure (renal artery involvement)
- Spinal cord ischaemia (paraplegia — intercostal arteries occluded)

## Clinical Reasoning

**Classic presentation:** Sudden severe tearing or ripping chest or back pain, maximal at onset, radiating to the back.

**Key signs:**
- **BP differential >20 mmHg between arms** — subclavian artery involvement (present in only ~20–30%)
- **Pulse deficit** — absent or diminished pulse in one arm
- **Aortic regurgitation** — decrescendo diastolic murmur (new = ascending dissection)
- **Neurological deficits** — stroke (carotid involvement), paraplegia (intercostal artery occlusion)

**Must not miss — aortic dissection before thrombolysis in STEMI:**
- Inferior MI (from RCA occlusion by dissection) + tearing back pain = think dissection first
- Thrombolysis in aortic dissection = fatal haemorrhage

**Differential for acute severe chest pain:**
- STEMI — central, ECG changes, troponin rise (within hours)
- PE — pleuritic, dyspnoea, risk factors
- Boerhaave oesophageal rupture — post-vomiting, subcutaneous emphysema
- Mesenteric ischaemia — abdominal pain, out of proportion to examination findings

## Workup

**CXR:** Widened mediastinum (>8cm supine) — most common finding but only 60% sensitive. Aortic knob obliteration, tracheal deviation, left pleural effusion (haemothorax from leak).

**ECG:** Normal or nonspecific ST-T changes (rules out STEMI as primary cause but doesn't exclude dissection).

**Definitive imaging:**

| Test | Stability | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **CT aortography** | Stable | Best for determining extent. Reveals true/false lumen, intimal flap, branch involvement |
| **TOE** | Unstable or contrast allergy | Bedside. Highly sensitive for ascending dissection |
| **MRI aorta** | Stable, not time-critical | Best anatomical detail; not practical in acute setting |

**Inner Circle principle:** CXR first (widened mediastinum) → stable patient → CT aortography → unstable → TOE.

**Bloods:** Cross-match blood, FBC, coagulation, U&E, troponin (rule out concomitant MI, or Type A causing coronary dissection)

## Management

### Immediate — All Types

**Anti-impulse therapy** — the dual goal is to reduce heart rate AND blood pressure (reduce aortic wall stress):
1. **IV beta-blocker first** (esmolol or labetalol) — target HR <60 bpm. Beta-blockade reduces dP/dt (rate of pressure rise with each heartbeat).
2. **THEN IV vasodilator** (sodium nitroprusside or nicardipine) if BP still elevated — target systolic 100–120 mmHg.
3. **Never vasodilator alone** (reflex tachycardia → ↑ aortic wall stress → propagates dissection).

IV morphine for pain (also reduces sympathetic drive).

### Type A — Urgent Surgery

- Emergency surgical repair/replacement of ascending aorta
- Mortality 1–2% per hour untreated — operate within hours
- If AR significant → aortic valve repair/replacement included

### Type B — Medical Management (Uncomplicated)

- IV anti-impulse therapy → transition to oral (labetalol, amlodipine)
- Manage in high dependency unit
- Surveillance CTA at 48–72h, 1 month, 6 months, then annually

**TEVAR** (thoracic endovascular aortic repair) for complicated Type B: malperfusion of organs, uncontrolled hypertension, haemorrhage, rapidly expanding false lumen.

## Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA)

**Definition:** Dilatation of the abdominal aorta ≥3.0 cm (normal ~2.0 cm). Aneurysm defined as ≥1.5× normal diameter. Most are fusiform, infrarenal.

**Risk factors:** Male sex, age >65, **smoking** (strongest modifiable risk factor — Inner Circle), hypertension, atherosclerosis, family history.

**Pathoma:** Atherosclerosis → loss of elastic tissue in media → progressive dilatation → wall stress increases with radius (Laplace's law) → rupture risk rises exponentially.

**Clinical:**
- Usually **asymptomatic** (found on screening or incidental imaging)
- **Expanding AAA** — dull central or back pain, pulsatile tenderness
- **Ruptured AAA** — sudden severe abdominal or back pain + hypotension + pulsatile mass = classic triad. Retroperitoneal rupture slows haemorrhage but don't be falsely reassured.

**Screening (Inner Circle):** One-time abdominal ultrasound for all **men who have ever smoked, aged 65–75**. Single scan only — AAA screening does not benefit women or non-smokers at current evidence levels.

**Surveillance intervals (NICE/SVS):**
- 3.0–4.4 cm → USS annually
- 4.5–5.4 cm → USS every 3 months
- ≥5.5 cm → refer for repair

**Repair thresholds:**
- ≥5.5 cm (men) or ≥5.0 cm (women)
- Growth >1 cm/year
- Symptomatic (pain = expanding/impending rupture) → emergency repair

**Repair options:**
- **EVAR** (endovascular aortic repair) — preferred in suitable anatomy. Lower perioperative risk.
- **Open surgical repair** — more durable. Younger, lower-risk patients.

**Ruptured AAA** — direct to theatre (no time for CT if haemodynamically unstable). Mortality 80% overall.

## High Yield Pearls

- Aortic dissection: tearing/ripping pain maximal at onset, radiating to the back, BP asymmetry between arms
- Anti-impulse therapy: beta-blocker BEFORE vasodilator — never vasodilator alone (reflex tachycardia worsens dissection)
- Type A dissection = emergency surgery. Type B = medical management (unless complicated).
- Never thrombolyse a suspected aortic dissection presenting as inferior MI — it is fatal
- AAA screening: ONE-TIME USS in men aged 65–75 who have ever smoked. Strongest modifiable risk = smoking.
- Ruptured AAA: classic triad (pain + hypotension + pulsatile mass) — straight to theatre, no time for CT
