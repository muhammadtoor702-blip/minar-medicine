## Overview

COPD is a common, preventable, and treatable disease characterised by persistent, progressive airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. It is the third leading cause of death worldwide. The umbrella term encompasses two overlapping pathological processes — emphysema and chronic bronchitis — which coexist to varying degrees in most patients.

Cigarette smoking is the dominant cause, accounting for over 90% of cases in the developed world. Biomass fuel exposure, occupational dusts, and alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency are important alternative causes globally.

---

## Pathophysiology

Inhaled irritants trigger a sustained innate and adaptive immune response in the airways and lung parenchyma. Neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T-cells drive chronic inflammation, releasing proteases (particularly neutrophil elastase) that exceed the lung's antiprotease capacity — disrupting the normal protease-antiprotease balance and destroying alveolar walls.

**Emphysema** results from destruction of alveolar walls and loss of elastic recoil. Enlarged, fused air spaces increase dead space and reduce the surface area for gas exchange. During expiration, small airways collapse (dynamic hyperinflation) because the supporting elastic recoil is lost — producing air trapping and the characteristic barrel chest. Patients hyperventilate to maintain oxygenation — the "pink puffer" phenotype.

**Chronic bronchitis** is defined clinically: productive cough for at least three months per year for at least two consecutive years. Mucus gland hypertrophy (Reid index >50%) and goblet cell hyperplasia produce excessive mucus, promoting bacterial colonisation and recurrent infection. Mucus plugging causes ventilation-perfusion mismatch, hypoxaemia, and hypercapnia — the "blue bloater" phenotype.

Most patients have mixed pathology. Chronic hypoxia triggers pulmonary vasoconstriction, eventually producing pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular strain — **cor pulmonale**.

**Alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency:** An autosomal recessive condition caused by mutations in the SERPINA1 gene. AAT is the principal inhibitor of neutrophil elastase in the lung. The most clinically significant allele is Z (Glu342Lys). ZZ homozygotes have serum AAT levels <15% of normal, causing severe early-onset **panacinar emphysema** (lower lobe predominant), as well as hepatic disease from misfolded AAT protein accumulating in hepatocytes. AAT deficiency should be suspected when COPD presents in never-smokers, age <40, or with lower-lobe predominant emphysema. Confirm with serum AAT level and phenotyping/genotyping. **AAT augmentation therapy** (weekly IV infusions of human pooled AAT) is available for severe deficiency with evidence of emphysema — slows CT-measured lung density loss. Refer to specialist centre.

## Clinical Presentation

Progressive exertional dyspnoea is the cardinal symptom, often developing insidiously over years. Chronic productive cough, wheeze, and recurrent respiratory infections complete the picture. Many patients attribute their breathlessness to aging or deconditioning and present late.

**Signs of chronic disease:** Barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing (which maintains positive airway pressure, preventing dynamic airway collapse), use of accessory muscles, hyperresonant percussion, reduced breath sounds, and expiratory wheeze.

**Signs of cor pulmonale:** Peripheral oedema, raised JVP, loud P2, tricuspid regurgitation murmur. ECG shows P pulmonale, RBBB, and right axis deviation.

**Acute exacerbation** is defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variation, requiring a change in treatment. The most common triggers are respiratory tract infections — viral URTIs first, followed by bacterial superinfection (*Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*). Always consider PE as a precipitant, particularly if there is no infectious prodrome.

**Distinguishing COPD from asthma:**

| Feature | COPD | Asthma |
|---------|------|--------|
| Age | Typically >40, smoker | Any age, often childhood onset |
| Airflow obstruction | Fixed (irreversible) | Variable (fully reversible) |
| Atopy | Absent | Common |
| DLCO | Reduced (emphysema destroys alveoli) | Normal or elevated |
| Sputum | Chronic, purulent | Minimal, mucoid |
| Nocturnal symptoms | Not typical | Classic — worst at 2–4am |

**Digital clubbing is not caused by COPD.** If a patient with apparent COPD has finger clubbing, this demands urgent investigation for lung cancer, bronchiectasis, or another underlying diagnosis. Clubbing in COPD should prompt urgent CT thorax.

## Diagnosis

**Spirometry is required to diagnose COPD.** Clinical features alone are insufficient.
Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.7 confirms obstructive ventilatory defect. FEV1 % predicted determines GOLD severity:

| GOLD Grade | FEV1 % Predicted | Severity |
|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| GOLD 1 | ≥80% | Mild |
| GOLD 2 | 50–79% | Moderate |
| GOLD 3 | 30–49% | Severe |
| GOLD 4 | <30% | Very severe |

**DLCO (transfer factor)** is reduced in emphysema — reflecting loss of alveolar surface area — and helps distinguish from asthma where DLCO is preserved. In severe emphysema, DLCO can be <40% predicted.

**BODE index** — a composite four-point prognostic score superior to FEV1 alone for predicting mortality:

| Component | Score 0 | Score 1 | Score 2 | Score 3 |
|-----------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| **B**MI | >21 | ≤21 | — | — |
| **O**bstruction (FEV1%) | ≥65% | 50–64% | 36–49% | ≤35% |
| **D**yspnoea (MRC grade) | 0–1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| **E**xercise (6MWT metres) | ≥350m | 250–349m | 150–249m | <150m |

BODE score 0–2: good prognosis. **BODE score 7–10: approximately 80% 4-year mortality** — these patients should be counselled and considered for lung transplantation assessment.

**Acute exacerbation investigations:**
- ABG — essential. Identifies type 2 respiratory failure (↓PaO₂, ↑PaCO₂, ↑HCO₃). Rising PaCO₂ in an acutely breathless patient = impending ventilatory failure.
- CXR — exclude pneumothorax and consolidation
- FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures if septic
- Sputum culture if purulent
- ECG — right heart strain, arrhythmia

## Management

### Acute Exacerbation

**Controlled oxygen therapy is the most critical initial step.** The target SpO₂ in known or suspected CO₂ retainers is **88–92%**, not the standard 94–98%. Administering high-flow oxygen abolishes hypoxic drive in patients dependent on hypoxaemia to breathe, causing worsening hypercapnia, CO₂ narcosis, and respiratory arrest. Use a 24% Venturi mask (blue) and titrate upward cautiously, rechecking ABG at 30–60 minutes after any oxygen change.

- **Nebulised salbutamol** 2.5–5mg and **ipratropium bromide** 0.5mg — back-to-back in severe exacerbations; continue 4-hourly; drive nebulisers with compressed air (not oxygen) in CO₂ retainers
- **Oral prednisolone** 30–40mg for 5 days (IV hydrocortisone 100mg if unable to swallow). Steroids reduce inflammation, shorten recovery, and reduce treatment failure — do not extend beyond 5 days without good reason.
- **Antibiotics** — indicated if sputum is purulent (colour change from baseline), consolidation present, or patient is systemically unwell. Amoxicillin 500mg TDS, doxycycline 200mg loading then 100mg OD, or clarithromycin 500mg BD as first-line options. Choice guided by local resistance patterns and previous sputum cultures.
- **Non-invasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP)** — indicated if pH <7.35 with PaCO₂ >45 mmHg despite 1 hour of initial medical therapy. Reduces intubation rate and mortality (evidence grade A). Deliver via full-face mask, with IPAP typically 12–20 cmH₂O and EPAP 4–6 cmH₂O. Recheck ABG at 1 hour.
- **Intubation and invasive ventilation** — if NIV fails or is contraindicated, with careful discussion of goals of care

### Stable COPD — Stepwise Management

**Smoking cessation is the only intervention that slows the decline in lung function.** Nothing else comes close. Offer pharmacotherapy (varenicline — most effective; nicotine replacement therapy; bupropion) plus behavioural support at every opportunity. Even late in disease course, cessation slows FEV1 decline and reduces exacerbation risk.

**Bronchodilator therapy (inhaled):**
- LAMA (tiotropium, aclidinium, umeclidinium) or LABA (salmeterol, formoterol, indacaterol) — first-line monotherapy for breathlessness
- LAMA + LABA — for patients symptomatic on monotherapy; combination superior to either alone
- Add ICS — only if frequent exacerbations (≥2/year) and blood eosinophil count >300 cells/µL. **ICS does not reduce mortality in COPD** and significantly increases pneumonia risk — do not use routinely as first-line add-on.

**Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT):**
- Indicated when PaO₂ <55 mmHg (<7.3 kPa) on air at rest when clinically stable, confirmed on **two separate occasions at least three weeks apart**
- Or PaO₂ 55–60 mmHg (7.3–8.0 kPa) with cor pulmonale, secondary polycythaemia (haematocrit >55%), or nocturnal desaturation
- Minimum **15 hours per day** including during sleep — below this threshold, mortality benefit is not demonstrated
- Proven mortality benefit — alongside smoking cessation, one of only two interventions that extend life in COPD

**Azithromycin prophylaxis:** In patients with ≥2 exacerbations per year despite optimal inhaled therapy, **azithromycin 250mg OD** or 500mg three times weekly reduces exacerbation frequency by approximately 27% (MACRO trial). Before starting: check QTc (avoid if prolonged — risk of torsades), baseline hearing test (macrolide-related sensorineural hearing loss), and arrange sputum culture for NTM (non-tuberculous mycobacteria — azithromycin monotherapy promotes resistance). Monitor annually.

**Mucolytics:** Carbocisteine 375mg BD-TDS or N-acetylcysteine for patients with chronic productive cough — NICE recommends considering in patients with troublesome cough; may reduce exacerbation frequency. Not routinely recommended without productive cough.

**Pulmonary rehabilitation** — supervised exercise and education programme (minimum 6 weeks); improves exercise capacity, dyspnoea scores, and quality of life in all GOLD grades. Arrange after every hospitalisation — reduces readmission. One of the most effective interventions in COPD management.

**Vaccinations:** Annual influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine (one-off), COVID-19 vaccination — all reduce exacerbation-related mortality.

**Surgical and interventional options:**
- **Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS):** For upper-lobe predominant emphysema with poor exercise capacity despite pulmonary rehabilitation (NETT trial: LVRS improves survival in this specific subgroup). Removes the most diseased lung tissue, allowing more normal lung to expand and reducing hyperinflation.
- **Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (Zephyr valves):** Endobronchial one-way valves placed in the most diseased lobes; causes targeted lobar collapse and reduces hyperinflation. Requires complete lobar fissures and absence of collateral ventilation (determined by Chartis assessment). Reduces exacerbations and improves FEV1 in selected patients.
- **Bullectomy:** For a single large bulla (>1/3 of hemithorax) compressing surrounding normal lung tissue; can produce dramatic improvement in breathlessness.
- **Lung transplantation:** Single or bilateral lung transplant for very severe disease (GOLD 4) with rapid decline, poor prognosis despite maximal therapy, or AAT deficiency. Refer to specialist centre early — waiting list times are long.

**Indications for specialist referral:**
- Uncertain diagnosis (never-smoker, age <40, clinical-spirometry mismatch)
- GOLD 3–4 or rapid decline
- Frequent exacerbations (≥3/year) despite optimal therapy
- Suspected AAT deficiency
- Consideration for surgical/interventional options or transplant
- Complex management decisions (ICS use, azithromycin, NIV at home)

## Complications

- Acute-on-chronic respiratory failure — type 2, requiring NIV
- Cor pulmonale — right heart failure from pulmonary hypertension; secondary polycythaemia from chronic hypoxaemia
- Spontaneous pneumothorax — from bullae rupture; higher risk with large bullae and GOLD 3–4
- Lung cancer — markedly elevated risk in smokers with COPD; any new or changing symptom warrants CT chest
- Depression and anxiety — highly prevalent, under-treated, worsen prognosis and adherence; screen at every visit

## Clinical Insight

Digital clubbing is not caused by COPD. If a patient with apparent COPD has finger clubbing, this demands investigation for lung cancer, bronchiectasis, or another underlying diagnosis. Clubbing in COPD should prompt urgent CT thorax.

The oxygen target of 88–92% is perhaps the most important number in acute respiratory medicine. Giving uncontrolled high-flow oxygen to a hypercapnic COPD patient is one of the most common and preventable causes of iatrogenic harm in emergency medicine. The risk is real, the mechanism is well established, and Venturi masks exist precisely to prevent it. The 24% blue Venturi mask is the correct starting point for any COPD patient in respiratory distress — do not reach for the non-rebreather mask.

LABA should not be used without ICS in asthma — but in COPD, LABA monotherapy is standard. This distinction matters both clinically and in examinations. The same drug (salmeterol) in a different disease has a completely different risk profile. Prescribing salmeterol without ICS to an asthmatic is dangerous; prescribing it without ICS to a COPD patient is standard practice.
