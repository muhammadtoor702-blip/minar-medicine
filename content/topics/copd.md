# COPD

## Clinical Scenario

A 66-year-old ex-smoker with a 45 pack-year history presents with three days of worsening breathlessness, increased sputum that has turned green, and wheeze. He normally walks 200 metres before stopping. On examination his RR is 26, SpO₂ is 87% on air, and he is using accessory muscles. He has a barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing, hyperresonant percussion bilaterally, and a prolonged expiratory phase with audible wheeze.

## Overview

COPD is a common, preventable, and treatable disease characterised by persistent, progressive airflow limitation that is not fully reversible. It is the third leading cause of death worldwide. The umbrella term encompasses two overlapping pathological processes — emphysema and chronic bronchitis — which coexist to varying degrees in most patients.

Cigarette smoking is the dominant cause, accounting for over 90% of cases in the developed world. Biomass fuel exposure, occupational dusts, and alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency are important alternative causes globally.

## Pathophysiology

Inhaled irritants trigger a sustained innate and adaptive immune response in the airways and lung parenchyma. Neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T-cells drive chronic inflammation, releasing proteases (particularly neutrophil elastase) that exceed the lung's antiprotease capacity — disrupting the normal protease-antiprotease balance and destroying alveolar walls.

**Emphysema** results from destruction of alveolar walls and loss of elastic recoil. Enlarged, fused air spaces increase dead space and reduce the surface area for gas exchange. During expiration, small airways collapse (dynamic hyperinflation) because the supporting elastic recoil is lost — producing air trapping and the characteristic barrel chest. Patients hyperventilate to maintain oxygenation — the "pink puffer" phenotype.

**Chronic bronchitis** is defined clinically: productive cough for at least three months per year for at least two consecutive years. Mucus gland hypertrophy (Reid index >50%) and goblet cell hyperplasia produce excessive mucus, promoting bacterial colonisation and recurrent infection. Mucus plugging causes ventilation-perfusion mismatch, hypoxaemia, and hypercapnia — the "blue bloater" phenotype.

Most patients have mixed pathology. Chronic hypoxia triggers pulmonary vasoconstriction, eventually producing pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular strain — **cor pulmonale**.

## Clinical Presentation

Progressive exertional dyspnoea is the cardinal symptom, often developing insidiously over years. Chronic productive cough, wheeze, and recurrent respiratory infections complete the picture. Many patients attribute their breathlessness to aging or deconditioning and present late.

**Signs of chronic disease:** Barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing (which maintains positive airway pressure, preventing dynamic airway collapse), use of accessory muscles, hyperresonant percussion, reduced breath sounds, and expiratory wheeze.

**Signs of cor pulmonale:** Peripheral oedema, raised JVP, loud P2, tricuspid regurgitation murmur. ECG shows P pulmonale, RBBB, and right axis deviation.

**Acute exacerbation** is defined as an acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variation, requiring a change in treatment. The most common triggers are respiratory tract infections — viral URTIs first, followed by bacterial superinfection (*Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*). Always consider PE as a precipitant, particularly if there is no infectious prodrome.

**Distinguishing COPD from asthma:**

| Feature | COPD | Asthma |
|---------|------|--------|
| Age | Typically >40, smoker | Any age, often childhood onset |
| Airflow obstruction | Fixed (irreversible) | Variable (fully reversible) |
| Atopy | Absent | Common |
| DLCO | Reduced (emphysema destroys alveoli) | Normal or elevated |
| Sputum | Chronic, purulent | Minimal, mucoid |

## Diagnosis

**Spirometry is required to diagnose COPD.** Clinical features alone are insufficient.

Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.7 confirms obstructive ventilatory defect. FEV1 % predicted determines GOLD severity:

| GOLD Grade | FEV1 % Predicted | Severity |
|-----------|-----------------|---------|
| GOLD 1 | ≥80% | Mild |
| GOLD 2 | 50–79% | Moderate |
| GOLD 3 | 30–49% | Severe |
| GOLD 4 | <30% | Very severe |

**DLCO (transfer factor)** is reduced in emphysema — reflecting loss of alveolar surface area — and helps distinguish from asthma where DLCO is preserved.

**Acute exacerbation investigations:**
- ABG — essential. Identifies type 2 respiratory failure (↓PaO₂, ↑PaCO₂, ↑HCO₃). Rising PaCO₂ in an acutely breathless patient = impending ventilatory failure.
- CXR — exclude pneumothorax and consolidation
- FBC, CRP, U&E, blood cultures if septic
- Sputum culture if purulent
- ECG — right heart strain, arrhythmia

## Management

### Acute Exacerbation

**Controlled oxygen therapy is the most critical initial step.** The target SpO₂ in known or suspected CO₂ retainers is **88–92%**, not the standard 94–98%. Administering high-flow oxygen abolishes hypoxic drive in patients dependent on hypoxaemia to breathe, causing worsening hypercapnia, CO₂ narcosis, and respiratory arrest. Use a 24–28% Venturi mask and titrate to target.

- **Nebulised salbutamol** (2.5–5mg) and **ipratropium bromide** (0.5mg) — back-to-back in severe exacerbations; continue 4-hourly
- **Oral prednisolone** 30–40mg for 5 days (IV hydrocortisone if unable to swallow). Steroids reduce inflammation, shorten recovery, and reduce treatment failure — but do not prolong duration beyond 5 days.
- **Antibiotics** — indicated if sputum is purulent, consolidation present, or patient systemically unwell. Amoxicillin, doxycycline, or clarithromycin as first-line options. Choice guided by local resistance patterns.
- **Non-invasive ventilation (NIV/BiPAP)** — indicated if pH <7.35 with PaCO₂ >6 kPa despite 1 hour of initial medical therapy. Reduces intubation rate and mortality (evidence grade A). Deliver via full-face mask, with IPAP typically 12–20 cmH₂O and EPAP 4–6 cmH₂O.
- **Intubation and invasive ventilation** — if NIV fails or is contraindicated, with careful discussion of goals of care

### Stable COPD — Stepwise Management

**Smoking cessation is the only intervention that slows the decline in lung function.** Nothing else comes close. Offer pharmacotherapy (varenicline, nicotine replacement) plus behavioural support at every opportunity.

**Bronchodilator therapy (inhaled):**
- LAMA (tiotropium) or LABA — first-line monotherapy for breathlessness
- LAMA + LABA — for patients symptomatic on monotherapy
- Add ICS — only if frequent exacerbations (≥2/year) and blood eosinophil count >300 cells/µL. ICS does not reduce mortality in COPD and increases pneumonia risk — do not use routinely.

**Long-term oxygen therapy (LTOT):**
- Indicated when PaO₂ <7.3 kPa (55 mmHg) on air at rest, confirmed on two occasions at least three weeks apart when the patient is stable
- Or PaO₂ 7.3–8.0 kPa with cor pulmonale, polycythaemia, or nocturnal desaturation
- Minimum **15 hours per day** including during sleep
- Mortality benefit is proven — alongside smoking cessation, one of only two interventions that improve survival in COPD

**Pulmonary rehabilitation** — supervised exercise and education programme; improves exercise capacity, dyspnoea scores, and quality of life in all GOLD grades. Arrange after every hospitalisation.

**Vaccinations:** Annual influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccine (one-off), and now COVID-19 vaccination — all reduce exacerbation-related mortality.

## Complications

- Acute-on-chronic respiratory failure — type 2, requiring NIV
- Cor pulmonale — right heart failure from pulmonary hypertension
- Spontaneous pneumothorax — from bullae rupture
- Secondary polycythaemia — from chronic hypoxaemia
- Lung cancer — markedly elevated risk; any new or changing symptom warrants CT chest
- Depression and anxiety — highly prevalent, under-treated, worsen prognosis

## Clinical Insight

Digital clubbing is not caused by COPD. If a patient with apparent COPD has finger clubbing, this demands investigation for lung cancer, bronchiectasis, or another underlying diagnosis. Clubbing in COPD should prompt urgent CT thorax.

The oxygen target of 88–92% is perhaps the most important number in acute respiratory medicine. Giving uncontrolled high-flow oxygen to a hypercapnic COPD patient is one of the most common and preventable causes of iatrogenic harm in emergency medicine. The risk is real, the mechanism is well established, and Venturi masks exist precisely to prevent it.

LABA should not be used without ICS in asthma — but in COPD, LABA monotherapy is standard. This distinction matters both clinically and in examinations. The same drug in a different disease has a different risk profile.
