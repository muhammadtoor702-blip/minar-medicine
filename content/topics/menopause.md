## Overview

Menopause is defined as the permanent cessation of menstruation resulting from the loss of ovarian follicular activity, confirmed retrospectively after 12 consecutive months of amenorrhoea. The average age in the UK is 51 years. The perimenopause (menopausal transition) begins on average 4 years before the final menstrual period and is characterised by hormonal fluctuation, irregular periods, and the emergence of menopausal symptoms. Approximately 80% of women experience vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes and night sweats), and 25–30% describe these as severe and significantly affecting quality of life. Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), occurring before age 40, affects 1% of women and carries additional health implications beyond symptom management.

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) — the administration of exogenous oestrogen (with progestogen in women with an intact uterus) — remains the most effective treatment for menopausal symptoms and has been controversial since the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) trial in 2002 reported increased breast cancer risk. Subsequent reanalysis, longer follow-up, and newer formulations have substantially revised the risk-benefit picture. NICE 2015 and the 2023 NICE update conclude that HRT is appropriate for most women with bothersome menopausal symptoms, and that the risks have been overestimated and overcommunicated.

## Pathophysiology

### Hormonal Changes in Menopause

**Follicular depletion:** Women are born with approximately 1–2 million primordial follicles; by puberty ~300,000 remain; menopause occurs when the pool falls below a critical threshold (~1,000 follicles). Oocyte atresia and ovulation progressively reduce the follicular pool throughout reproductive life.

**Hormonal sequence:**
1. Declining inhibin B (from granulosa cells) → FSH rises (earliest detectable marker)
2. Intermittent anovulatory cycles → irregular periods, oestrogen fluctuation
3. Eventually follicles stop responding to FSH → oestradiol falls markedly
4. Loss of oestrogen negative feedback → FSH and LH markedly elevated (FSH >30 IU/L = postmenopausal range)
5. Androgen production from adrenal glands and stroma continues → testosterone (reduced but present); converted peripherally to oestrone

### Consequences of Oestrogen Deficiency

**Vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes/night sweats):**
Narrowing of the thermoregulatory neutral zone in the hypothalamus — small perturbations in core temperature trigger inappropriate heat dissipation responses (peripheral vasodilation, sweating). Oestrogen withdrawal reduces serotonin and noradrenaline activity in hypothalamic thermoregulatory centres. SSRI/SNRIs partially effective via this mechanism.

**Urogenital atrophy (genitourinary syndrome of menopause — GSM):**
Oestrogen receptors throughout the lower urogenital tract; oestrogen deficiency → vaginal epithelial thinning, loss of rugae, reduced lubrication, reduced glycogen → loss of Lactobacillus → increased vaginal pH → dyspareunia, vulval pruritus, recurrent UTIs, urgency. Unlike vasomotor symptoms (which often improve with time), GSM worsens progressively without treatment.

**Bone health:**
Oestrogen inhibits osteoclast activity. Postmenopausal oestrogen deficiency → uncoupled bone remodelling → net bone resorption → osteoporosis. Bone loss accelerates most rapidly in the first 3–5 years postmenopause (up to 3–5% per year). Fracture risk (hip, vertebral, Colles' wrist) increases significantly.

**Cardiovascular risk:**
Premenopausal women have lower cardiovascular risk than age-matched men — oestrogen protective effects on lipid profile (raises HDL, lowers LDL), vasomotor tone, endothelial function. After menopause, cardiovascular risk equalises and eventually exceeds that of men.

**Cognitive effects:**
Oestrogen modulates neuronal plasticity, synaptic transmission, and cerebral blood flow. Vasomotor symptoms disrupt sleep → cognitive symptoms (brain fog, memory complaints). The relationship between HRT and dementia risk is complex and unresolved.

### Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI)

Ovarian failure before age 40. Causes: idiopathic (~90%), autoimmune (Turner syndrome mosaic, associated with Addison's, autoimmune thyroiditis), iatrogenic (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, oophorectomy), genetic (FMR1 premutation — fragile X carrier, Turner syndrome 45,XO).

**Clinical importance of POI:**
- Reduced life expectancy (cardiovascular disease, osteoporosis) if untreated
- HRT until at least age 51 (natural menopause age) is essential to replace physiological oestrogen — this is not "treatment" of symptoms but replacement to the normal level for that age
- Fertility implications: spontaneous ovulation in 5–10%; cryopreservation before treatment if diagnosed while planning fertility

## Clinical Presentation

### Menopausal Symptoms

**Vasomotor:**
- Hot flushes: sudden sensation of heat spreading from chest to face and neck, flushing, diaphoresis, followed by chills; may last 1–5 minutes; 8–10× daily is severe
- Night sweats: nocturnal version → sleep disruption → fatigue, poor concentration, mood disturbance

**Psychological:**
- Low mood, irritability, anxiety, poor concentration ("brain fog")
- Distinguishing menopausal mood symptoms from depression is clinically important — low mood in perimenopausal women is often attributable to hormonal fluctuation + sleep deprivation + life context, not primary depressive disorder

**Urogenital:**
- Vaginal dryness, irritation, dyspareunia (superficial — at introitus, from atrophy)
- Urinary urgency, frequency, recurrent UTIs (reduced urethral and bladder epithelial thickness)
- Reduced libido (multifactorial: dyspareunia, hormonal, psychological)

**Musculoskeletal:**
- Joint pains, reduced muscle mass (oestrogen/testosterone deficiency)

### Perimenopause vs Menopause vs POI

| | Perimenopause | Menopause | POI |
|--|--------------|-----------|-----|
| Definition | Transition period — irregular periods | 12 months amenorrhoea | <40 years; 4+ months amenorrhoea + FSH >25 IU/L × 2 |
| Hormone profile | FSH rising; oestradiol fluctuating | FSH >30 IU/L; oestradiol low | FSH >25 IU/L × 2 (4-week interval); oestradiol low |
| Diagnosis | Clinical | Clinical/hormonal | Hormonal (2 × FSH >25 IU/L) |

## Diagnosis

### When to Measure FSH

In women ≥45 years presenting with typical menopausal symptoms, **diagnosis is clinical** — FSH is NOT required. Symptoms + age + menstrual pattern are sufficient.

**FSH indications:**
- Age <45 years (to rule out other causes of irregular periods)
- Age 40–45 with atypical history
- Suspected POI (under 40)
- After hysterectomy (no periods to monitor)

**FSH interpretation:** A single elevated FSH is not diagnostic of POI (wide variation in POI); **two FSH measurements >25 IU/L at least 4 weeks apart** required for POI diagnosis.

### Investigations at Menopause/POI

- **Thyroid function:** Hypothyroidism mimics many menopausal symptoms (fatigue, weight gain, mood changes)
- **FBC:** Anaemia (heavy irregular periods in perimenopause)
- **Bone density (DEXA scan):** At diagnosis of POI; and for postmenopausal women with additional osteoporosis risk factors
- **Karyotype + FMR1 premutation testing:** In all women with POI (to exclude Turner syndrome and fragile X carrier status)
- **Autoimmune screen:** Anti-ovarian antibodies, adrenal antibodies (Addison's co-association)

## Management

### HRT — Principles and Prescribing

**Indication:** Bothersome menopausal symptoms (vasomotor, psychological, urogenital) — most effective treatment available; also prevents bone loss.

**Types:**

**1. Oestrogen-only HRT:**
- For women post-hysterectomy (no uterus → no progestogen needed)
- Systemic: oral or transdermal (patch/gel/spray)

**2. Combined HRT (oestrogen + progestogen):**
- For women with intact uterus — progestogen protects endometrium from oestrogen-driven hyperplasia/cancer
- **Sequential (cyclical):** Oestrogen continuously + progestogen for 12–14 days/month → monthly withdrawal bleed; suitable for perimenopausal women (still having periods)
- **Continuous combined:** Oestrogen + progestogen continuously → no bleed; suitable after 12+ months amenorrhoea (postmenopausal); breakthrough bleeding common in first 3–6 months

**Progestogen options:**
- Micronised progesterone (Utrogestan): natural progesterone; lower breast cancer risk; lower cardiovascular risk; preferred agent
- Synthetic progestogens (norethindrone, medroxyprogesterone acetate, levonorgestrel): older; associated with higher VTE and breast cancer risk in WHI
- **LNG-IUS (Mirena):** Local progestogen endometrial protection + provides contraception; can be combined with systemic oestrogen

**3. LNG-IUS + systemic oestrogen:** Excellent option for perimenopasual women — IUS provides both endometrial protection and contraception

**4. Local oestrogen (vaginal):** Vagifem tablets, Estring, Ovestin cream — low-dose topical oestrogen for GSM only; minimal systemic absorption; safe with or without systemic HRT; safe in women with previous breast cancer (insufficient systemic absorption to affect recurrence risk — NICE 2019)

**5. Tibolone:** Synthetic steroid with oestrogenic + progestogenic + androgenic activity; continuous (no bleed); helps libido; NOT recommended in breast cancer history

### Routes — Transdermal vs Oral

**Transdermal oestrogen (patch, gel, spray) is preferred over oral for:**
- VTE risk factors — transdermal bypasses hepatic first-pass effect → does NOT increase clotting factor synthesis → **no increased VTE risk** (unlike oral oestrogen which doubles VTE risk vs no HRT)
- Migraine with aura — avoids oestrogen peaks of oral dosing
- Hypertriglyceridaemia — oral oestrogen raises triglycerides
- **NICE guidance: offer transdermal oestrogen as first choice for most women**

### WHI Trial — Understanding the Evidence

The 2002 WHI trial (Rossouw et al., JAMA) reported increased breast cancer, cardiovascular disease, VTE, and stroke with HRT. This caused widespread HRT discontinuation. However:
- WHI used oral conjugated equine oestrogen + medroxyprogesterone acetate (synthetic progestogen) — formulations no longer recommended
- Mean age at enrolment was 63 — not the typical 50-year-old seeking symptom relief
- Absolute risks were small — e.g., 8 extra breast cancers per 10,000 woman-years
- The oestrogen-only arm (post-hysterectomy women) showed **reduced** breast cancer risk
- **Millns et al. (2020) and CAMS data:** Transdermal oestrogen + micronised progesterone does not increase VTE or breast cancer risk significantly

**Breast cancer risk in context:**
- Combined HRT (oestrogen + synthetic progestogen): small increased risk (comparable to drinking 1–2 units alcohol/day; less than obesity)
- Oestrogen-only HRT: no significant increase or slight reduction
- Transdermal oestrogen + micronised progesterone: lowest risk of all regimens
- Risk returns to baseline within a few years of stopping HRT
- **NICE 2015:** Benefits of HRT for symptom control outweigh risks for most healthy women under 60

### Testosterone

Female androgen deficiency syndrome → low libido, fatigue, low mood. NICE 2015 recommends considering testosterone supplementation for women with reduced libido not responding to HRT. Unlicensed for women in UK (use male preparations at ~1/10 dose — fingertip amount of gel). Effective in RCTs (APHRODITE study).

### Non-Hormonal Options

For women who cannot or choose not to take HRT:
- **SSRIs/SNRIs (venlafaxine, paroxetine, citalopram):** Second-line for vasomotor symptoms — 40–60% reduction in flush frequency
- **Clonidine:** Alpha-2 agonist; modest effect on flushes; side effects limit use
- **Gabapentin:** Modest benefit for flushes; sedating
- **Fezolinetant (Veoza):** NK3 receptor antagonist — newly licensed 2024; directly targets hypothalamic thermoregulatory pathway; significant reduction in flushes; first non-hormonal mechanism-specific treatment
- **Vaginal lubricants/moisturisers:** For GSM without systemic HRT
- **CBT:** Evidence-based for psychological symptoms and vasomotor symptoms

### Duration of HRT

There is no arbitrary time limit on HRT use. The old "5-year rule" was based on WHI data and is no longer recommended. NICE guidance states women should take HRT for as long as benefits outweigh risks, reviewed annually. Many women continue beyond 5 years; the decision is individualised.

## Complications

- **Endometrial cancer:** Oestrogen-only HRT without progestogen in women with intact uterus → endometrial hyperplasia → cancer. **Never prescribe oestrogen-only to a woman with a uterus.**
- **Breast cancer:** Small increased risk with combined HRT (synthetic progestogen); minimal with transdermal oestrogen + micronised progesterone
- **VTE:** Oral oestrogen only (not transdermal); relative risk ×2 baseline; absolute risk still low in healthy women
- **Stroke:** Oral oestrogen associated with modest increased ischaemic stroke risk; not seen with transdermal
- **Irregular bleeding:** Common in first 3–6 months of HRT; any unexpected bleeding after 6 months on HRT or in postmenopausal women → **endometrial pipelle biopsy ± TVUS to exclude endometrial pathology**

## Clinical Insight

The WHI trial is one of the most consequential misinterpretations in modern medicine. The headlines in 2002 — "HRT causes breast cancer and heart disease" — led to a generation of women suffering severe menopausal symptoms unnecessarily. The reality is that the WHI was a study of older women (average age 63) using outdated formulations (oral conjugated equine oestrogen + synthetic progestogen). Its findings are not applicable to the healthy 50-year-old woman presenting with debilitating hot flushes. The absolute risks are small; the quality-of-life benefits are large; and modern transdermal oestrogen with micronised progesterone has a substantially better safety profile than the original WHI formulations.

The clinical consultation about HRT often requires explicit risk communication. Rather than relative risk ("HRT doubles your VTE risk"), absolute risk numbers are more meaningful: "Of 1,000 women your age not on HRT, about 2 per year will get a DVT. On oral HRT, this rises to about 4. On a patch, the risk is essentially unchanged at 2." This reframing — absolute not relative risk — is what allows women to make genuinely informed decisions rather than being paralysed by headline-level statistics.

Genitourinary syndrome of menopause is systematically under-treated. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, which often improve after a few years, GSM worsens progressively throughout life and, untreated, significantly affects sexual function, quality of life, and relationship wellbeing. Local vaginal oestrogen is safe, effective, and appropriate even for women who cannot or choose not to take systemic HRT — and is appropriate even after breast cancer treatment (with oncology discussion). The barrier to treatment is embarrassment; the barrier to prescribing is often the same.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 49F, 18 months irregular periods, 6 months amenorrhoea, 8–10 hot flushes/day, sleep disruption, low mood, reduced libido, vaginal dryness/dyspareunia. No VTE/breast cancer history. BMI 26. Worried about HRT causing cancer.

**Diagnosis:** Menopause (12+ months amenorrhoea not quite reached, but 6 months with typical symptoms — likely perimenopausal/menopausal). No FSH needed at this age with these symptoms.

**Step 1 — Symptom assessment:**
- Vasomotor (8–10 flushes/day = severe) + sleep disruption + mood + sexual symptoms
- All significantly affecting quality of life → strong indication for HRT

**Step 2 — Contraindication screen:**
- No VTE history, no breast cancer, non-smoker, BMI 26, BP (check) → no contraindications to HRT

**Step 3 — Offer HRT:**
- **Transdermal oestrogen (patch or gel) — preferred** (no VTE risk; avoids oral oestrogen issues)
- **Intact uterus → combined HRT required** (progestogen for endometrial protection)
- **Sequential combined** (oestrogen continuously + progestogen 12–14 days/cycle) — appropriate as she may not yet be fully postmenopausal; expects a monthly bleed
- Progestogen: **micronised progesterone (Utrogestan 200 mg days 1–14 of cycle)** — lowest risk formulation
- Alternatively: LNG-IUS (endometrial protection + contraception) + transdermal oestrogen

**Step 4 — Address cancer concerns:**
- Explain WHI context: older women, different formulations
- Absolute breast cancer risk with modern transdermal + micronised progesterone: minimal; comparable to 2 units alcohol/day
- Benefits: symptom relief, bone protection, cardiovascular benefits when started in early menopause (timing hypothesis)

**Step 5 — Libido/GSM:**
- Vaginal oestrogen (Vagifem/Ovestin) for dyspareunia — can be used alongside systemic HRT
- Consider testosterone if libido remains low after systemic HRT is optimised

**Step 6 — Review at 3 months:**
- Assess symptom response; side effects (breast tenderness, bloating)
- Check BP; any unexpected bleeding → pipelle biopsy
- Annual review thereafter

**Exam trigger:** *Menopausal symptoms + fear of HRT* → explain WHI context; offer transdermal oestrogen + micronised progesterone (safest combined HRT regimen); no time limit on use. The distractor is refusing HRT based on the WHI headlines and sending her away with lifestyle advice alone — inadequate for severe vasomotor symptoms affecting daily function.
