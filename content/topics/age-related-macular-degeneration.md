## Overview

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of irreversible severe visual loss in people over 50 in high-income countries, accounting for approximately 50% of all registrations of severe visual impairment in the UK. The macula — the central 6 mm of the retina responsible for high-acuity central vision — is preferentially affected, resulting in loss of reading vision, face recognition, and fine detail tasks, while peripheral vision is typically preserved. AMD is classified as **dry** (non-neovascular, ~90%) and **wet** (neovascular, ~10%) — the dry form progresses slowly and has no proven treatment beyond nutritional supplementation, while the wet form causes rapid severe visual loss but is now highly treatable with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections. Timely recognition of the wet AMD conversion phenotype (metamorphopsia, sudden vision loss) and urgent anti-VEGF initiation can preserve and even restore central vision.

## Pathophysiology

### The Aging Macula

The retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) performs multiple critical functions for photoreceptors: phagocytosis of shed outer segment discs, transport of nutrients and waste, visual cycle regeneration. With age, this function deteriorates:

**Drusen formation:** RPE dysfunction → incomplete processing of photoreceptor outer segment byproducts → **drusen** (deposits of lipid-containing extracellular material) accumulate between the RPE and Bruch's membrane (the basement membrane separating the RPE from the choriocapillaris). Drusen are the hallmark of early AMD.

**Hard drusen:** Small, well-defined — common in normal aging, minimal AMD risk.
**Soft drusen:** Larger (>63 μm), indistinct margins, confluent — significant AMD risk marker; indicate RPE/Bruch's membrane dysfunction.

**Risk factors:** Age (strongest — doubles per decade after 50); genetics (complement factor H polymorphism CFH Y402H — common variant in up to 50% of AMD risk); smoking (2–4× increased risk — most important modifiable factor); white ethnicity; family history; cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, hypercholesterolaemia).

### Dry AMD — Geographic Atrophy (GA)

Progressive RPE atrophy → loss of overlying photoreceptors → expanding areas of **geographic atrophy** (GA) — well-demarcated area of RPE loss. GA expands at approximately 1–2 mm² per year. When GA involves the foveal centre (the central 200 μm containing exclusively cone photoreceptors), profound central visual loss results.

**Treatment:** No proven pharmacological treatment historically. **Pegcetacoplan (Syfovre) and avacincaptad pegol (Izervay):** Complement inhibitors (anti-C3 and anti-C5 respectively) — first ever drugs for GA; intravitreal injections; OAKS/DERBY and GATHER trials show ~20–35% reduction in GA growth rate; regulatory approval 2023–2024; benefit modest, not visual acuity improvement but reduced progression rate.

### Wet AMD — Choroidal Neovascularisation (CNV)

Soft drusen + RPE dysfunction → chronic local complement activation + oxidative stress → upregulation of VEGF in RPE and Bruch's membrane → **choroidal neovascularisation (CNV)**: new vessels from the choriocapillaris grow through Bruch's membrane and under the RPE and/or neurosensory retina.

These new vessels are **abnormal** — lack proper tight junctions → **fluid leakage** (intra/subretinal fluid, sub-RPE fluid) → **macular oedema → rapid photoreceptor damage → central visual loss in days to weeks**.

Two OCT classifications:
- **Type 1 CNV (under RPE):** Sub-RPE fluid; often less acute
- **Type 2 CNV (above RPE, subretinal):** Subretinal fluid + associated haemorrhage; most commonly seen in classical wet AMD
- **Type 3 CNV (retinal angiomatous proliferation — RAP):** Retinal-based neovascularisation; deep retinal haemorrhages

**VEGF in wet AMD:** VEGF-A is the principal mediator of CNV vessel growth and permeability. Anti-VEGF therapy targets this pathway.

## Clinical Presentation

### Dry AMD Symptoms (Gradual)

- **Blurring of central vision** — gradual over years
- **Difficulty reading fine print** — initial complaint in early AMD
- **Decreased contrast sensitivity**
- **Reduced colour vision** (central photoreceptor loss)
- **Central scotoma** in advanced GA — dense blank area in central vision; patients typically retain enough peripheral vision to navigate and avoid falls
- **No metamorphopsia** (no distortion) unless conversion to wet

### Wet AMD Symptoms (Rapid) — Red Flags

- **Metamorphopsia:** Distortion of straight lines — lines appear wavy, bent, or broken. Caused by subretinal fluid lifting photoreceptors → spatial distortion of the retinal mosaic. **The hallmark symptom of wet AMD — must be acted upon URGENTLY**
- **Sudden deterioration of central visual acuity**
- **Central scotoma** (blank spot in central vision) — may appear as sudden central blur
- **Micropsia** (objects appear smaller)

**Amsler grid:** A grid of parallel horizontal and vertical lines with a central fixation dot. Used to screen for metamorphopsia and scotoma:
- Normal: lines straight, grid complete, fixation dot visible
- Wet AMD: wavy/distorted lines, gaps in the grid
- **Recommended for patients with drusen: daily self-monitoring at home** (one eye covered at a time); report any new distortion immediately to ophthalmologist

### AMD Classification (Beckman Initiative 2013)

| Stage | Features |
|-------|---------|
| Normal | No drusen, no pigmentary changes |
| Early AMD | Small/medium drusen (<125 μm) ± pigmentary changes; no vision loss |
| Intermediate AMD | Large drusen (≥125 μm) and/or pigmentary changes; possible subtle vision loss |
| Late AMD | GA (dry late) OR neovascular AMD (wet late); significant vision loss |

## Diagnosis

### Investigations

**Amsler grid:** Bedside screening; identifies metamorphopsia; does not diagnose AMD but indicates functional macular disturbance warranting OCT.

**OCT (optical coherence tomography):** Gold standard for wet AMD diagnosis and monitoring.
- **Subretinal fluid (SRF):** Fluid between photoreceptors and RPE — indicates active wet AMD
- **Intraretinal fluid (IRF):** Fluid within retinal layers — associated with worse prognosis; usually indicates more aggressive disease
- **Sub-RPE fluid (PED — pigment epithelial detachment):** Elevation of RPE from Bruch's membrane
- **CNV membrane:** Hyperreflective lesion below the RPE/above RPE depending on type
- OCT-A (angiography): Maps CNV without dye injection — identifies neovascular lesion before fluid appears

**Fluorescein angiography (FFA) ± ICG angiography:**
- FFA: Maps leakage from CNV
- ICG (indocyanine green) angiography: Better visualises choroidal vessels; needed for type 1 CNV and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV)
- Less used in the anti-VEGF era (OCT-A largely replaced for CNV detection)

**Fundus photography:** Documents drusen, atrophy, and baseline macular appearance.

## Management

### Wet AMD — Anti-VEGF (First-Line, NICE-Approved)

**Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections:** Transformative treatment — before ranibizumab (2006), wet AMD caused rapid, irreversible central visual loss. Anti-VEGF has changed wet AMD from a diagnosis of inevitable blindness to a manageable condition.

**Anti-VEGF agents:**
- **Ranibizumab (Lucentis):** Fab fragment of anti-VEGF monoclonal antibody; MARINA and ANCHOR trials (2006) — landmark; 95% of patients maintained vision at 2 years; 1/3 gained ≥3 lines. NICE-approved.
- **Bevacizumab (Avastin):** Full antibody; off-label but CATT trial showed non-inferiority; significantly cheaper; widely used globally.
- **Aflibercept (Eylea):** Fusion protein (VEGF trap); VIEW 1&2 trials — non-inferior to ranibizumab; 2-monthly dosing after loading phase; NICE-approved.
- **Brolucizumab (Beovu):** Small single-chain antibody; HAWK/HARRIER trials — q12 weeks dosing possible; concerns about retinal vasculitis/occlusive side effects.
- **Faricimab (Vabysmo):** Bispecific (anti-VEGF + anti-Ang-2); TENAYA/LUCERNE trials — extended dosing intervals (q16 weeks in some); newest agent; NICE approved 2023.

**Treatment protocols:**
- **Loading phase:** Monthly injections × 3 (standard) or × 3 (aflibercept q8 after 3 monthly)
- **Treat-and-extend (TAE):** After loading, extend intervals by 2 weeks if retina is dry on OCT at each visit; reduce interval if fluid recurs; maintains most patients at reduced burden
- **PRN (as-needed):** Monthly monitoring OCT/VA; inject only if fluid present; higher visual acuity burden from monitoring

**Monitoring:** At each visit — VA measurement + OCT (decision to inject based on presence of fluid/activity). Monthly visits required on most protocols. OCT-guided treatment.

### Dry AMD — Prevention and Nutritional Supplementation

**AREDS2 (Age-Related Eye Disease Study 2) supplementation:**
- **Vitamin C 500 mg + Vitamin E 400 IU + Lutein 10 mg + Zeaxanthin 2 mg + Zinc 80 mg + Copper 2 mg** daily
- AREDS2 trial: Reduces progression from intermediate to advanced AMD by ~25% in patients with intermediate AMD (bilateral large drusen or advanced AMD in one eye)
- **Does NOT benefit early AMD; does NOT benefit normal eyes; does NOT reverse existing damage**
- Prescribe only to those with intermediate AMD or advanced AMD in one eye

**Smoking cessation:** Strongest modifiable risk factor; smoking cessation at any age reduces risk of progression.

**Healthy diet:** Antioxidant-rich diet (leafy green vegetables, fish), Mediterranean diet associated with lower AMD risk; omega-3 fatty acids.

**UV protection:** Sunglasses with UV400 protection — long-term UVA/UVB exposure implicated in AMD pathogenesis.

### Emerging Treatments for Dry AMD (Geographic Atrophy)

**Pegcetacoplan (Syfovre) and avacincaptad pegol (Izervay):**
- Complement inhibitors (anti-C3 and anti-C5 respectively)
- Intravitreal injection monthly or every 2 months
- First FDA-approved (USA 2023) and in regulatory review (UK/EU)
- Reduce GA growth rate by ~20–35% but do not improve visual acuity
- Represents first disease-modifying treatment for geographic atrophy

## Complications

- **Bilateral wet AMD:** Fellow eye risk ~12% per year when one eye has wet AMD — daily Amsler grid self-monitoring + prompt presentation with new symptoms; anti-VEGF started promptly
- **End-stage "disciform" scar:** Inactive CNV with fibrous scar replacing the central macula → permanent central visual loss; anti-VEGF cannot reverse fibrosis, only prevents further fluid/haemorrhage
- **Submacular haemorrhage:** Large subretinal bleed from CNV rupture → photoreceptor toxicity from blood → can cause sudden profound visual loss; requires urgent vitreoretinal assessment; submacular tissue plasminogen activator injection + gas tamponade in some cases
- **Low vision rehabilitation:** Central scotoma from GA or disciform scar → eccentric viewing (using peripheral retina), magnifiers, electronic aids; RNIB (Royal National Institute of Blind People) referral; emotional support for significant disability
- **Anti-VEGF injection risks:** Endophthalmitis (0.05% per injection), RD, traumatic cataract, transient IOP spike, brolucizumab retinal vasculitis (uncommon but serious)

## Clinical Insight

The most important sentence in AMD management is: "Any new distortion or sudden change in central vision must be assessed urgently." AMD is one of the few conditions in medicine where a 1–2 week delay in treatment can be the difference between preserving 6/12 vision and losing it permanently. The subretinal fluid from an active CNV lesion causes progressive photoreceptor damage — anti-VEGF works by drying the retina, not by reversing established photoreceptor death. Fluid present for longer = more photoreceptors irreversibly destroyed before treatment begins.

The transformation in AMD prognosis since 2006 represents one of the most dramatic improvements in outcomes of any chronic disease. Before anti-VEGF, wet AMD caused legal blindness in most patients within 2 years of onset. The MARINA trial demonstrated that 95% of patients maintained their vision at 2 years with monthly ranibizumab injections. The challenge now is not efficacy but treatment burden — monthly hospital visits for injections is difficult for elderly patients, and extensions of treatment intervals (faricimab to 16 weeks) aim to reduce this without sacrificing vision outcomes.

The distinction between dry AMD (geographic atrophy) and wet AMD (neovascular) is clinically critical because they require completely different management. GA = no injections, AREDS2 supplements, lifestyle. Wet AMD = urgent anti-VEGF, monthly visits, OCT-guided treatment. A patient with years of stable drusen (dry AMD) who suddenly develops metamorphopsia has converted to wet AMD — this conversion changes management urgency from routine annual review to same-day urgent ophthalmology. Every patient with intermediate AMD should be testing their Amsler grid daily (one eye covered at a time) for exactly this reason.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 78F, 2-week history of metamorphopsia + visual blurring right eye, background of known bilateral drusen (stable × 5 years). VA 6/24 right, 6/9 left. Amsler: metamorphopsia right. OCT: subretinal fluid + CNV right macula.

**Diagnosis: Neovascular (wet) AMD, right eye. Dry AMD (bilateral drusen), left eye (intermediate).**

**Step 1 — Urgency:**
- Wet AMD with active CNV → **urgent same-day ophthalmology referral or AMD fast-track pathway** (most NHS trusts have AMD urgent fast-track: seen within 2–3 working days of referral, same-week first injection)

**Step 2 — Investigations (in AMD clinic):**
- OCT both eyes: confirm active CNV type, quantify fluid volume
- OCT angiography or FFA: map CNV lesion extent
- VA and baseline documentation

**Step 3 — Treatment (right eye):**
- **Intravitreal anti-VEGF loading:** Aflibercept 2 mg or ranibizumab 0.5 mg monthly × 3 injections
- Reassess at 4 weeks: VA + OCT; if dry (no fluid) → extend interval; if persistent fluid → continue monthly
- **Treat-and-extend protocol:** Extend interval by 2 weeks at each visit if retina is dry; reduce by 2 weeks if fluid recurs; aim for personalised stable minimum injection frequency

**Step 4 — Prevention (left eye):**
- Intermediate AMD (large drusen) → **AREDS2 supplementation** (reduces risk of progression to advanced AMD by ~25%)
- **Daily Amsler grid monitoring** — self-check both eyes daily; report any new distortion immediately (urgent pathway activation)
- Annual OCT left eye monitoring (or more frequently if high-risk features)

**Step 5 — Lifestyle:**
- **Stop smoking** if applicable (strongest modifiable risk factor)
- Antioxidant-rich diet; UV-protective sunglasses
- Low vision services referral if VA remains reduced after treatment

**Exam trigger:** *Sudden metamorphopsia in patient with known drusen* → wet AMD conversion — same-day/urgent ophthalmology referral for anti-VEGF. AREDS2 supplements for the fellow eye with intermediate AMD. The distractor is continuing routine annual monitoring — any new distortion is an urgent presentation requiring same-week ophthalmology assessment.
