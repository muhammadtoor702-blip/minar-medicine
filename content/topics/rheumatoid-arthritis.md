## Overview

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic, immune-mediated inflammatory arthritis characterised by synovial inflammation, progressive joint destruction, and significant extra-articular manifestations. It is the most common inflammatory arthritis, affecting ~1% of the population with a 3:1 female predominance and peak onset in the fourth to fifth decades. Without effective treatment, RA causes progressive disability, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality. Early diagnosis and aggressive treatment — targeting disease remission rather than symptom control — fundamentally alters the disease course.

## Pathophysiology

The central event in RA is **synovial inflammation**. In genetically susceptible individuals (HLA-DRB1*04 — the "shared epitope" — is the strongest genetic risk factor), an environmental trigger (most likely cigarette smoking, which drives citrullination of self-proteins in the lung) initiates an autoimmune response.

Antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells and macrophages) present citrullinated peptides to CD4+ T helper cells in the synovium → Th1 and Th17 activation → TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6, IL-17 release → synovial fibroblast activation → **pannus formation**: an aggressive, invasive layer of proliferating synovial tissue that invades and destroys cartilage and bone at the joint margins.

**Anti-CCP (anti-citrullinated protein antibody)** is highly specific for RA (~95%) and can be present years before clinical disease — reflecting the underlying pathological process. Rheumatoid factor (RF) is an IgM antibody against the Fc region of IgG — sensitive (~70–80%) but non-specific (also positive in Sjögren's, SLE, infections, elderly normals).

Key cytokines and their therapeutic targets:
- **TNF-α**: Adalimumab, etanercept, infliximab, certolizumab, golimumab
- **IL-6**: Tocilizumab, sarilumab (block IL-6 receptor)
- **IL-1**: Anakinra (rarely used now)
- **T-cell co-stimulation**: Abatacept (CTLA4-Ig — blocks CD80/86 on APCs)
- **B-cells**: Rituximab (anti-CD20)
- **JAK-STAT signalling**: Baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib (JAK inhibitors — oral, target intracellular signalling downstream of multiple cytokine receptors)

## Clinical Presentation

### Articular Features

The distribution is the key diagnostic clue: **symmetrical, small joint, peripheral polyarthritis** — MCPs, PIPs, wrists. DIP joints are characteristically spared (distinguishes from osteoarthritis and psoriatic arthritis). Feet (MTPs) are frequently involved and often underexamined. The thumb Z-deformity, swan-neck deformity (PIP hyperextension, DIP flexion), and boutonnière deformity (PIP flexion, DIP hyperextension) are late changes from tendon and ligament damage.

**Morning stiffness >1 hour** is the hallmark of inflammatory arthritis. It reflects overnight accumulation of inflammatory exudate in the joint space. Stiffness that lasts <30 minutes suggests a mechanical (degenerative) rather than inflammatory process.

**Joints never directly involved in RA**: DIP joints, lumbar spine (the cervical spine — specifically C1-C2 — can be affected, causing atlantoaxial subluxation).

### Extra-articular Features

RA is a systemic disease — extra-articular manifestations reflect the same underlying vasculitic and inflammatory process:

| System | Manifestation |
|--------|--------------|
| Skin | Rheumatoid nodules (subcutaneous, over pressure points — olecranon, sacrum; in RF-positive disease); vasculitic ulcers |
| Lung | Interstitial lung disease (most common cause of non-articular death in RA); pleural effusion; rheumatoid nodules in lung; Caplan syndrome (nodules + pneumoconiosis) |
| Eyes | Scleritis (painful, deep inflammation — sight-threatening); episcleritis (superficial, milder); keratoconjunctivitis sicca (secondary Sjögren's) |
| Heart | Accelerated atherosclerosis (RA doubles cardiovascular risk); pericarditis |
| Haematological | Normocytic anaemia (ACD); thrombocytosis in active disease; Felty's syndrome (RA + splenomegaly + neutropaenia) |
| Neurological | Peripheral entrapment neuropathy (carpal tunnel — most common); cervical myelopathy (C1-C2 subluxation) |

**Cardiovascular risk** is the most important extra-articular consideration. RA confers a cardiovascular risk equivalent to diabetes. Aggressive lipid management, blood pressure control, smoking cessation, and low-dose aspirin in appropriate patients are as important as immunosuppressive therapy.

## Diagnosis

RA is a **clinical diagnosis** supported by serology and imaging. The **2010 ACR/EULAR classification criteria** are used in clinical trials; in practice, the diagnosis is made on clinical grounds.

**Serology**: RF and anti-CCP. A patient with symmetrical polyarthritis who is anti-CCP positive has RA until proven otherwise. RF alone is insufficient — it is too non-specific.

**Inflammatory markers**: CRP and ESR reflect disease activity. Normal inflammatory markers do not exclude RA in early disease.

**X-rays**: Early RA — periarticular osteopaenia, soft tissue swelling. Established disease — joint space narrowing (cartilage loss), marginal erosions (pathognomonic — at the "bare area" of the joint where pannus invades unprotected bone). Plain X-rays of hands and feet at baseline in all patients.

**Ultrasound and MRI**: More sensitive than X-ray for detecting synovitis and early erosions. Ultrasound-detected subclinical synovitis predicts progression. Used to confirm inflammatory arthritis when diagnosis is uncertain and to monitor treatment response.

**Differential diagnosis of symmetrical polyarthritis**:
- Seronegative RA
- Psoriatic arthritis (look for psoriasis, nail changes, DIP involvement)
- Reactive arthritis (preceding infection — urogenital or GI)
- Viral arthritis (parvovirus B19, hepatitis B, rubella, COVID-19 — usually self-limiting)
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (check ANA, complement, anti-dsDNA)
- Early connective tissue disease
- Crystal arthritis (gout, pseudogout — usually oligoarticular and episodic)
## Management

### The Treat-to-Target (T2T) Principle

The single most important conceptual shift in RA management over the past 20 years. The target is **clinical remission** (or at minimum low disease activity) as measured by composite scores — DAS28, CDAI, or SDAI. Disease activity is formally assessed at every visit; treatment is escalated if the target is not met within 3–6 months. This approach — borrowed from managing blood pressure and diabetes — dramatically reduces radiographic progression and disability compared with symptom-based dosing.

**DAS28** (Disease Activity Score using 28 joints): Incorporates tender joint count, swollen joint count, patient global assessment, and CRP/ESR. DAS28 <2.6 = remission; 2.6–3.2 = low activity; 3.2–5.1 = moderate; >5.1 = high.

### Pharmacological Management (NICE NG100)

**Step 1 — Conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs):**

**Methotrexate** is the anchor drug of RA treatment — started early, often maintained long-term, and used as a combination partner with biologics. Folate antagonist with anti-inflammatory properties (adenosine-mediated at rheumatological doses). Weekly oral or subcutaneous dosing (7.5–25 mg/week). **Always co-prescribe folic acid 5 mg once weekly** (on a different day) — reduces GI and mucosal toxicity without reducing efficacy. Monitor FBC and LFTs monthly for 6 months, then 3-monthly. Teratogenic — reliable contraception mandatory; stop 3 months before conception.

Combination csDMARD therapy (methotrexate + hydroxychloroquine + sulfasalazine) is superior to monotherapy in early active RA.

**Step 2 — Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) or JAK inhibitors:**

NICE criteria: Active disease (DAS28 >5.1) after 2 failed csDMARDs including methotrexate at adequate dose and duration.

First-line biologic: **Anti-TNF** (adalimumab, etanercept — most commonly used; subcutaneous; combined with methotrexate for greater efficacy and to prevent immunogenicity).

Alternatives: Tocilizumab (IL-6 receptor — useful when CRP/ESR are needed for monitoring and in patients with anaemia of chronic disease), abatacept, rituximab (particularly in seronegative RA or where anti-TNF is contraindicated — e.g., history of lymphoma).

**JAK inhibitors** (baricitinib, tofacitinib, upadacitinib): Oral, rapid onset (days). MHRA/FDA cardiovascular and VTE safety signals have restricted use to patients who have failed biologics or in whom biologics are contraindicated. Avoid in patients >65, smokers, or those with cardiovascular risk factors.

**Before starting any biologic or JAK inhibitor**:
- Screen for latent TB (IGRA or Mantoux + CXR) — anti-TNF reactivates TB
- Screen for hepatitis B and C, HIV
- Live vaccines contraindicated during treatment
- Withhold during serious infection

**Step 3 — Refractory disease:**

Switch biologic class. Rituximab as second or later line. Consider biosimilars (equivalent efficacy, substantially lower cost).

### Symptom Management (Adjuncts)

**NSAIDs**: Symptomatic relief while awaiting DMARD effect (onset 6–12 weeks for methotrexate). Use lowest effective dose for shortest duration — GI, renal, and cardiovascular risks. Add PPI cover in all patients on regular NSAIDs.

**Corticosteroids**: Bridging therapy during DMARD initiation or escalation. Intra-articular corticosteroid injection for flare in a single joint. Avoid long-term systemic steroids (osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, HPA suppression). If long-term steroids are unavoidable: bone protection with calcium/vitamin D and bisphosphonate.

**Physiotherapy**: Joint protection techniques, hydrotherapy, exercise programmes. Occupational therapy for functional aids and workplace adaptations.

**Surgery**: Synovectomy, joint replacement (hip/knee most commonly), wrist arthrodesis. Cervical spine X-ray or MRI before any general anaesthetic — to exclude atlantoaxial subluxation (intubation risk).

## Complications

- Progressive joint destruction and disability without effective DMARD therapy
- Atlantoaxial subluxation — cervical myelopathy from C1-C2 instability
- Interstitial lung disease — screen with PFTs and HRCT in established RA
- Cardiovascular disease — leading cause of excess mortality
- Drug toxicity: methotrexate hepatotoxicity/pneumonitis; biologic-associated infection and malignancy (particularly lymphoma with anti-TNF — though the absolute risk increase is modest)
- Secondary osteoporosis from chronic inflammation and steroid use
- Felty's syndrome — severe splenomegaly and neutropaenia in longstanding seropositive RA

## Clinical Insight

The window of opportunity in RA is real and time-limited. Synovial inflammation in the first weeks to months of disease is more reversible than established pannus. Studies consistently show that patients treated within 12 weeks of symptom onset achieve better long-term outcomes — less radiographic damage, higher rates of remission, better functional scores — than those treated at 6 or 12 months. Referral to rheumatology within 3 weeks of a suspected inflammatory arthritis presentation is the NICE standard. The GP's role is not to confirm the diagnosis — it is to refer quickly.

Anti-CCP positivity in a patient with arthritis — even with a normal CRP, even with only a few months of symptoms — is a strong predictor of erosive RA and demands urgent rheumatology review. Anti-CCP antibodies predate clinical disease by years and mark a fundamentally different prognosis from seronegative arthritis.

Methotrexate without folic acid is a missed opportunity. Folic acid co-prescription reduces nausea, mouth ulcers, and LFT abnormalities without reducing methotrexate's anti-inflammatory effect. The most common reason patients stop methotrexate is GI intolerance — much of which is preventable. If a patient on methotrexate is struggling with side effects, ask about folic acid compliance before switching drugs.
