## Overview

Otitis media encompasses a spectrum of middle ear inflammatory conditions ranging from acute otitis media (AOM) — a common, largely self-limiting bacterial or viral infection — to chronic otitis media with effusion (OME, "glue ear") and the potentially serious complications of acute mastoiditis and cholesteatoma. AOM is one of the most common childhood infections, responsible for more antibiotic prescriptions than almost any other condition in primary care. The challenge is that most episodes resolve without antibiotics, yet a minority develop complications that require urgent intervention. Accurate otoscopy, clinical criteria-based prescribing, and recognition of complications define best practice.

---

## Pathophysiology

### Anatomy and Aetiology

The middle ear communicates with the nasopharynx via the Eustachian tube — in children, this tube is shorter, more horizontal, and more compliant than in adults, facilitating ascending infection from the upper respiratory tract. Viral URTI causes mucosal oedema → Eustachian tube dysfunction → negative middle ear pressure → effusion → secondary bacterial superinfection.

**Bacterial causes (AOM):**
- *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (~35%) — most common cause of severe AOM; associated with complications
- *Haemophilus influenzae* (~25%) — non-typable; often associated with bilateral disease and OME
- *Moraxella catarrhalis* (~15%) — most likely to resolve spontaneously
- Viruses (RSV, rhinovirus, influenza) — viral AOM does NOT benefit from antibiotics

**Pathophysiology of complications:**
- **Mastoiditis:** Infection spreads into mastoid air cells via the aditus ad antrum (connection between middle ear and mastoid). Periosteal elevation → subperiosteal abscess → post-auricular fluctuant swelling + ear protrusion forward and downward.
- **Cholesteatoma:** Accumulation of desquamating stratified squamous epithelium in the middle ear/mastoid. Congenital (uncommon) or acquired — from a retraction pocket in the pars flaccida of the tympanic membrane, usually from chronic Eustachian tube dysfunction. Enzymes produced by the squamous epithelium erode bone (ossicles, tegmen, canal wall, mastoid, lateral semicircular canal, facial nerve canal).

### Otitis Media with Effusion (Glue Ear)

Persistent middle ear effusion WITHOUT acute infection. The most common cause of acquired hearing loss in children (15–40 dB conductive loss). Usually follows AOM but can occur without acute infection. Bilateral OME with significant hearing loss at a critical developmental period → speech and language delay.

## Clinical Presentation

### Acute Otitis Media — Diagnostic Criteria

The diagnosis of AOM requires all three:
1. **Acute onset** of symptoms
2. **Middle ear effusion** (bulging TM, impaired TM mobility on pneumatic otoscopy, air-fluid level, otorrhoea from TM perforation)
3. **Signs of middle ear inflammation** (distinct erythema of TM OR distinct otalgia)

**Otoscopy in AOM:** Bulging tympanic membrane (most important feature — indicates AOM rather than OME), erythema, loss of light reflex, loss of normal TM landmarks (handle of malleus, short process). Spontaneous perforation → purulent otorrhoea → sudden relief of pain.

**Severity classification (NICE):**
- **Mild-moderate:** <39°C, mild pain, systemically well
- **Severe:** ≥39°C, severe pain, vomiting, systemically unwell

**Features warranting no-antibiotic strategy:**
- Age ≥2 years, mild-moderate AOM, systemically well, no bilateral disease, no perforation

**Features warranting immediate antibiotics:**
- Age <2 with bilateral AOM
- Systemically unwell
- Immunocompromised
- Otorrhoea (perforated AOM)
- Complications suspected

### Otitis Media with Effusion (OME)

- Hearing loss (parent notices child watching TV loudly, not responding to speech, teacher concerns)
- No fever, no acute pain
- Otoscopy: dull, grey, retracted TM; absent light reflex; visible fluid level or air bubbles; poor TM mobility on pneumatic otoscopy
- Can present as "glue ear" — TM appears amber/yellow from thick viscous fluid

### Acute Mastoiditis — Red Flags

- Post-auricular erythema, swelling, and tenderness
- Ear displaced forward and downward (displaced pinna) — classic sign
- Loss of post-auricular sulcus
- Persisting or worsening AOM despite 48–72h antibiotics
- Fluctuant post-auricular swelling (subperiosteal abscess)
- Facial nerve palsy, meningism, cerebellar signs (intracranial extension)

### Cholesteatoma

- **Painless, persistent, foul-smelling otorrhoea** — the classic presentation
- Progressive conductive hearing loss (ossicular erosion)
- Deep "attic" perforation in the pars flaccida (superoanterior TM) on otoscopy
- Keratin debris (white, pearly material) visible in perforation
- Vertigo or facial palsy (bone erosion into labyrinth/facial canal)

## Diagnosis

### Investigations

**AOM:** Clinical diagnosis based on otoscopy — no investigations required in uncomplicated cases.

**Tympanometry:** Flat (type B) tympanogram = fluid in middle ear (OME or AOM with effusion); type C = negative middle ear pressure (Eustachian tube dysfunction); normal type A = no effusion. Useful in OME diagnosis and monitoring.

**Audiometry (pure tone audiogram):** In children with OME or recurrent AOM — assesses degree of conductive hearing loss. Referral threshold: bilateral OME with hearing loss ≥25 dB for ≥3 months at critical speech/language development period.

**CT temporal bones:** For suspected acute mastoiditis — identifies extent of mastoid disease, subperiosteal abscess, and any intracranial complications. Essential before mastoid surgery.

**MRI brain:** If intracranial complication suspected (meningitis, lateral sinus thrombosis, cerebral abscess, subdural empyema).

## Management

### Acute Otitis Media

**Analgesia first:** Paracetamol ± ibuprofen for all patients — regardless of antibiotic decision.

**No immediate antibiotics (watchful waiting):**
- Most AOM resolves spontaneously in 3–7 days
- Meta-analyses show antibiotics reduce pain duration by ~1 day, fever by ~0.5 days, risk of contralateral AOM — but increase diarrhoea and rash
- NICE recommends: delayed ("safety-net") prescription or no prescription for most children ≥2 years with mild-moderate unilateral AOM
- Advise parents: if no improvement or worsening at 72h → start antibiotics or seek review

**Immediate antibiotics:**
- **Amoxicillin 40–45 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses × 5–7 days** — first-line
- Penicillin allergy: clarithromycin or erythromycin
- Amoxicillin failure (no improvement at 48–72h): **co-amoxiclav** (adds beta-lactamase coverage for H. influenzae and M. catarrhalis)
- Severe systemic illness or complications: IV ceftriaxone, ENT referral

### Glue Ear (OME)

**Watchful waiting (first 3 months):** 60% of cases resolve spontaneously within 3 months. Advise parents on hearing support strategies (facing child when speaking, reducing background noise).

**Indications for referral/intervention:**
- Bilateral OME with hearing loss ≥25 dB for ≥3 months
- Speech/language delay attributable to hearing loss
- Developmental delay, learning difficulties, Down syndrome (higher risk of severe OME)
- Structural TM damage (retraction pocket threatening to form cholesteatoma)

**Grommets (ventilation tubes):** Small plastic tubes inserted through TM under GA; equalise middle ear pressure; immediately restore hearing; average extrusion at 9–15 months. First-line surgical intervention for persistent bilateral OME with hearing loss affecting development. Adenoidectomy at the same time reduces recurrence.

**Hearing aids:** Alternative to surgery in children unfit for GA or parental preference.

### Acute Mastoiditis

- **Immediate IV antibiotics:** IV cefuroxime or ceftriaxone (mastoiditis is a surgical emergency)
- **ENT urgent review:** Same-day
- **CT temporal bones:** Assess for subperiosteal abscess and intracranial complications
- **Myringotomy + grommet:** Drain middle ear; send pus for culture
- **Cortical mastoidectomy:** If subperiosteal abscess, failure to respond to IV antibiotics, or intracranial complication — surgical drainage of mastoid

### Cholesteatoma

- **Surgical excision — mastoidectomy** (canal wall up or canal wall down): The only curative treatment. Removes all squamous epithelium from middle ear and mastoid.
- **Regular surveillance:** Residual cholesteatoma recurs in up to 30% → second-look surgery or MRI surveillance
- Non-EP-diffusion-weighted MRI (DW-MRI): Can detect residual/recurrent cholesteatoma without need for open surgery

## Complications

- **Acute mastoiditis:** Most common complication of AOM (1–2 per 10,000 AOM cases) — now rare with widespread antibiotic use
- **Tympanic membrane perforation:** Central perforation in AOM usually heals spontaneously; marginal/attic perforations risk cholesteatoma formation
- **Ossicular erosion (cholesteatoma):** Progressive conductive hearing loss; requires reconstruction (ossiculoplasty)
- **Labyrinthitis/sensorineural hearing loss:** Toxic or suppurative — from cholesteatoma erosion into labyrinth or spread of infection
- **Facial nerve palsy:** Erosion of the bony facial nerve canal by cholesteatoma → House-Brackmann grading; urgent surgical decompression
- **Intracranial complications (rare but life-threatening):** Meningitis, subdural empyema, cerebral abscess, lateral/sigmoid sinus thrombosis, epidural abscess — require IV antibiotics ± neurosurgical intervention
- **Hearing loss + language delay:** From recurrent AOM or persistent OME — most important long-term consequence of untreated OME in young children

## Clinical Insight

The decision to prescribe or withhold antibiotics in AOM is one of the most discussed topics in evidence-based primary care — and one where patient/parent expectations frequently conflict with evidence. The critical clinical teaching is that **bulging of the tympanic membrane is the single most reliable predictor of bacterial AOM and benefit from antibiotics** — more so than erythema, which can be caused by crying. A red but non-bulging TM in a well child is not AOM. A bulging TM in a feverish infant with otalgia is, and warrants a prescription.

Cholesteatoma is the classic diagnosis that is missed because "ear infections" are so common that the specific pattern — chronic painless offensive otorrhoea + progressive hearing loss + attic perforation — is not connected to a separate diagnostic category. Any patient with persistent unilateral otorrhoea, hearing loss, or an attic/marginal TM perforation must have cholesteatoma excluded with formal ENT review. The consequences of delayed diagnosis (intracranial complications, labyrinthine fistula, facial nerve palsy) are severe and largely preventable with early surgical referral.

The Eustachian tube is the key anatomical explanation for why children have AOM far more commonly than adults. The child's tube is ~1.8 cm long, runs at 10° from horizontal (adult: ~3.5 cm, 45°), and is more compliant — the combination means that Eustachian tube dysfunction from URTI is far more likely to result in middle ear fluid accumulation and infection in children under 5. The higher attack rate in children attending daycare (12–15 episodes per year) versus home-raised children reflects viral exposure frequency.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 3-year-old, 2-day history of right otalgia, fever 38.9°C, 3rd episode in 6 months. Otoscopy: red, bulging right TM, loss of light reflex and landmarks.

**Diagnosis: Acute otitis media — moderate-severe (bulging TM + fever >38.5°C)**

**Step 1 — Immediate antibiotic indication:**
- Bulging TM + fever ≥38.5°C → qualifies for immediate antibiotics (NICE criteria: bilateral AOM, OR severe AOM with fever ≥39°C, OR age <2 with bilateral AOM, OR systemic illness)
- This patient: fever 38.9°C approaches threshold; third episode in 6 months; consider immediate antibiotics
- **Amoxicillin 40 mg/kg/day in 3 divided doses × 5 days**
- **Paracetamol/ibuprofen for analgesia** — prescribe regardless of antibiotic decision

**Step 2 — Safety-net advice:**
- Return if not improving or worsening at 48–72h
- Watch for: post-auricular swelling/redness (mastoiditis), facial asymmetry, neck stiffness, increasing systemically unwell → same-day A&E review

**Step 3 — Recurrent AOM (3 episodes in 6 months):**
- Meets threshold for recurrent AOM (≥3 episodes in 6 months or ≥4 in 12 months)
- **Refer to ENT** for:
  - Audiological assessment (tympanometry + PTA)
  - Consider grommets (ventilation tubes) — reduces future episodes and treats any concurrent OME
  - Adenoidectomy assessment — enlarged adenoids perpetuate Eustachian tube dysfunction

**Step 4 — Long-term:**
- Speech and language development monitoring — recurrent AOM episodes → periods of conductive hearing loss → potential language delay
- Reassure that most children "grow out" of recurrent AOM as Eustachian tube matures (length and angle)

**Exam trigger:** *Bulging TM + fever in a child with recurrent AOM* → immediate amoxicillin; refer to ENT for recurrent AOM criteria. The distractor is withholding antibiotics (appropriate for mild unilateral AOM in well child ≥2 years, but this patient has fever 38.9°C + bulging TM + third episode).
