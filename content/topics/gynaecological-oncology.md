## Overview

Gynaecological cancers — endometrial, cervical, ovarian, and vulval — present differently, have distinct risk factor profiles, and require specific investigations and management pathways. What unites them is the critical importance of **early recognition**: postmenopausal bleeding, abnormal cervical cytology, and persistent bloating all have defined investigation pathways that must not be delayed.

Ovarian cancer is the most lethal because it presents late; endometrial cancer is the most common in developed countries but is often caught early because postmenopausal bleeding brings women to attention. Understanding the risk factor profile of each cancer explains why certain patients need expedited investigation and why prevention strategies (HPV vaccination, cervical screening, aspirin, OCP) are so impactful.

## Pathophysiology

**Endometrial cancer**: The vast majority (85%) are **Type I** — endometrioid adenocarcinoma, oestrogen-driven. Prolonged, unopposed oestrogen exposure drives endometrial hyperplasia → atypical hyperplasia → invasive adenocarcinoma. The mechanism: oestrogen promotes endometrial cell proliferation; without the anti-proliferative effect of progesterone (which normally causes secretory differentiation and monthly shedding), the endometrium accumulates genetic damage.

**Type II** endometrial cancers (serous, clear cell) are oestrogen-independent, arise on an atrophic background, carry *TP53* mutations, and behave more aggressively — often diagnosed at a later stage.

**Cervical cancer**: Over 99% are caused by high-risk HPV — types 16 (squamous cell, the majority) and 18 (adenocarcinoma) account for >70%. HPV integrates into the host genome → disrupts *E6* (degrades p53 — removes apoptosis brake) and *E7* (binds and inactivates Rb — removes cell cycle brake) → unchecked cellular proliferation → CIN 1 → CIN 2 → CIN 3 → invasive cancer. This progression takes **years to decades**, which is exactly why cervical screening can interrupt the process.

**Ovarian cancer**: High-grade serous ovarian cancer (the most common and most lethal subtype) is now thought to originate predominantly from the fimbriated end of the fallopian tube. BRCA1/2 mutations impair homologous recombination DNA repair → accumulated genomic instability → malignant transformation. The lack of early symptoms reflects the peritoneal cavity's ability to accommodate significant tumour burden before causing noticeable symptoms.

## Clinical Presentation

**Endometrial cancer**: **Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) is endometrial cancer until proven otherwise**. This is the single most important sentence in gynaecological oncology. Even one episode of light PMB warrants urgent investigation. In premenopausal women, intermenstrual bleeding, heavy irregular periods, or discharge can indicate endometrial pathology. The uterus may be enlarged on examination.

Risk factors for endometrial cancer — think **unopposed oestrogen**:

| Risk Factor | Mechanism |
|-------------|-----------|
| Obesity | Peripheral aromatisation of androgens to oestrogen in adipose tissue |
| Nulliparity | Fewer progesterone-dominant luteal phases across a lifetime |
| Late menopause | More years of oestrogen exposure |
| Unopposed exogenous oestrogen (HRT without progestogen) | Direct stimulation without opposition |
| Tamoxifen use | Partial ER agonist on endometrium (antagonist in breast) |
| PCOS | Chronic anovulation → continuous oestrogen, no progesterone |
| Diabetes + hypertension | Associated with obesity; insulin also promotes endometrial proliferation |
| Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) | MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2 mutations — endometrial cancer is the second most common Lynch-associated cancer after colorectal |

Protective factors: **combined OCP** (progestogen component), **levonorgestrel IUS** (local progestogen), smoking (reduces oestrogen through anti-oestrogenic mechanisms — not a recommendation).

**Cervical cancer**: Often asymptomatic in early stages (detected by screening). When symptomatic: post-coital bleeding (classic), intermenstrual bleeding, offensive vaginal discharge, pelvic pain (advanced disease), haematuria/rectal bleeding (invasion of bladder/rectum in advanced disease).

**Ovarian cancer — "the silent killer"**: Vague, non-specific symptoms that are easily dismissed:
- Persistent bloating (most common symptom, often attributed to IBS for months)
- Pelvic or abdominal pain
- Early satiety, feeling full quickly
- Urinary frequency or urgency
- Weight loss, fatigue (advanced disease)
- Abdominal distension (ascites)

**Vulval cancer**: Pruritus (often longstanding and attributed to skin conditions), ulcer or mass on the vulva, bleeding, inguinal lymphadenopathy. May arise in the context of longstanding lichen sclerosus (older women, HPV-negative) or lichen planus, or in younger women with VIN on an HPV-related background.

## Diagnosis

**Endometrial cancer work-up**:
- **Transvaginal USS (TVS)**: Endometrial thickness. Normal postmenopausal endometrium: ≤4 mm. >4 mm requires further investigation with endometrial biopsy. (In premenopausal women, thickness varies with cycle — less useful.)
- **Pipelle biopsy**: Outpatient endometrial sampling, 90% sensitivity for endometrial cancer. Simple, quick, performed in clinic without anaesthesia.
- **Hysteroscopy + directed biopsy**: If pipelle inconclusive or unsatisfactory, or polyp suspected. Gold standard for visualisation of uterine cavity.
- **MRI pelvis**: Staging — assesses myometrial invasion depth (Stage I vs. II vs. III), cervical involvement, lymphadenopathy.

**FIGO staging of endometrial cancer:**

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Confined to uterus (IA: <50% myometrial invasion; IB: ≥50%) |
| II | Cervical stromal invasion |
| III | Adnexal/vaginal/parametrial/pelvic or para-aortic nodal involvement |
| IV | Bladder/rectal mucosa involved, or distant metastases |

**Cervical cancer work-up**:
- **Liquid-based cytology (LBC)** / HPV primary testing (UK now uses HPV-first screening)
- **Colposcopy**: Indicated for HPV positive with abnormal cytology, or abnormal smear
- **Punch biopsy** at colposcopy: Histological diagnosis
- **LLETZ** (Large Loop Excision of the Transformation Zone): Both diagnostic and therapeutic for CIN

**FIGO staging of cervical cancer** (clinical staging):

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Confined to cervix |
| IB1 | Clinically visible ≤2 cm OR microscopic >5 mm |
| IB2 | 2–4 cm |
| IB3 | >4 cm |
| IIA | Upper two-thirds vagina, no parametria |
| IIB | Parametrial involvement |
| IIIA/B | Lower vagina / pelvic wall involvement |
| IIIC | Pelvic or para-aortic lymph nodes |
| IVA/B | Bladder/rectum OR distant metastases |

**Ovarian cancer work-up**:
- **CA-125**: Elevated in >80% of epithelial ovarian cancers. However, also raised in: endometriosis, PID, fibroids, pregnancy, liver disease, peritoneal irritation. Non-specific as a screening tool.
- **Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI)**: RMI = CA-125 × USS score × menopausal status. USS score based on number of malignant features (multilocularity, solid areas, bilateral, ascites, metastases). RMI ≥200 = refer urgently to a cancer centre.
- **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis**: Staging, extent of peritoneal disease.
- **CA19-9, CEA**: For mucinous tumours.
- **BRCA1/2 testing**: For all high-grade serous ovarian cancers; if positive, cascade testing for first-degree relatives.

## Management

**Endometrial cancer**:
- **Stage I**: Total hysterectomy + bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (TH+BSO) ± pelvic lymph node dissection or sentinel lymph node biopsy. Laparoscopic or robotic approach preferred.
- **Stage II**: TH+BSO + pelvic lymph node dissection; adjuvant radiotherapy (vaginal vault brachytherapy ± external beam radiotherapy).
- **Stage III–IV**: TH+BSO + lymph node dissection + chemotherapy (carboplatin/paclitaxel) ± radiotherapy.
- **Unfit for surgery** (obesity, diabetes, cardiorespiratory disease): radiotherapy ± progestogen therapy (medroxyprogesterone acetate — used in early well-differentiated disease in women wishing to preserve fertility or unfit for surgery).

**Cervical cancer**:
- **CIN**: LLETZ (removes transformation zone with a heated wire loop under local anaesthesia). If margins involved: repeat LLETZ or close surveillance.
- **Stage IA1** (microscopic): LLETZ or simple hysterectomy.
- **Stage IB1–IIA1** (small, operable): **Radical hysterectomy** (Wertheim's) + pelvic lymph node dissection OR concurrent **chemoradiation** (cisplatin 40 mg/m² weekly + external beam RT + brachytherapy). Both are equivalent in outcome; surgery avoids radiation side effects in younger women.
- **Stage IIB+** (parametrial involvement/advanced): **Concurrent chemoradiation** is standard — surgery not feasible once parametria are involved.
- **HPV vaccination (Gardasil-9)**: Protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 — covering ~90% of HPV-related cancers. Given at age 12–13 in the UK (school-based programme).

**Ovarian cancer**:
- **Surgical staging and cytoreductive surgery ("debulking")**: Goal is complete cytoreduction (no visible residual disease) — the most important prognostic factor. Surgery includes TH+BSO + omentectomy + peritoneal biopsies + lymph node sampling ± bowel resection.
- **Chemotherapy**: Carboplatin + paclitaxel × 6 cycles (standard first-line). Carboplatin can be given intraperitoneally for maximal tumour exposure.
- **BRCA-positive patients**: Eligible for maintenance therapy with **olaparib** (PARP inhibitor) after response to platinum — SOLO-1 trial showed 36-month PFS benefit. PARP inhibitors exploit synthetic lethality in BRCA-deficient cells.
- **CA-125 monitoring**: Used to monitor treatment response and detect recurrence; serial values more useful than single readings.
- **Prognosis**: Stage I: 5-year survival ~90%; Stage III: ~30%; Stage IV: ~15%. The late-presentation problem.

**Vulval cancer**:
- **Wide local excision** with 1–2 cm margins
- **Inguinofemoral lymph node dissection**: Bilateral if midline tumour or bilateral disease; ipsilateral if lateral tumour >2 cm from midline. Associated with significant morbidity (lymphoedema, wound breakdown, infection).
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy**: Now standard for T1/T2 tumours without clinically involved nodes — reduces morbidity vs. full lymphadenectomy.
- **Radiotherapy**: For positive or close margins, or node-positive disease.
- **VIN (Vulval Intraepithelial Neoplasia)**: Topical **imiquimod** (immune modulator, stimulates local T-cell response against HPV-infected cells) or surgical excision.

## Complications

**Surgical complications**: Haemorrhage, infection, VTE (high-risk surgery — use LMWH prophylaxis), bowel and bladder injury, ureteric injury (especially during radical hysterectomy), lymphoedema (after lymph node dissection), nerve injury (genitofemoral nerve, obturator nerve), lymphocyst formation.

**Radiotherapy complications**: Acute — bowel and bladder inflammation (diarrhoea, cystitis, tenesmus). Late — radiation proctitis, haemorrhagic cystitis, vaginal stenosis, fistula (vesicovaginal, rectovaginal), secondary malignancy, bowel adhesions.

**Chemotherapy complications**:
- Carboplatin: myelosuppression, nephrotoxicity, hypersensitivity reactions (common with subsequent cycles)
- Paclitaxel: peripheral neuropathy (dose-limiting), alopecia, hypersensitivity reactions
- Cisplatin: nephrotoxicity (ensure adequate hydration), peripheral neuropathy, nausea/vomiting (severe), ototoxicity

## Clinical Insight

The woman in this scenario has **a very high probability of endometrial cancer**. Her risk factor profile is almost textbook: postmenopausal, obese (BMI 36 → peripheral oestrogen production), nulliparous (fewer progesterone-dominant cycles), diabetic (insulin resistance promotes endometrial proliferation), and — critically — **3 years of unopposed oestrogen therapy**. Oestrogen without progestogen opposition for this duration is a well-established cause of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.

The investigation pathway is clear: urgent **TVS** first (if endometrial thickness >4 mm → **pipelle biopsy** in clinic). Do not dismiss her bleeding as atrophic — any PMB requires exclusion of endometrial cancer.

The classic teaching point: HRT for menopausal symptoms should use **combined oestrogen + progestogen** in women with an intact uterus. Unopposed oestrogen is only appropriate after hysterectomy. Her GP prescribing unopposed oestrogen for 3 years in a woman with a uterus is the red flag in this history.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**Working through the case**: 56-year-old postmenopausal woman, 5 months of vaginal bleeding, BMI 36, nulliparous, T2DM, unopposed oestrogen HRT for 3 years, slightly enlarged uterus on examination.

The key constellation is: **postmenopausal bleeding + unopposed oestrogen exposure + multiple metabolic risk factors**. This is endometrial cancer until proven otherwise.

Investigations:
- TVS: assess endometrial thickness — if >4 mm, proceed to biopsy
- Outpatient pipelle biopsy (no anaesthesia required)
- If pipelle insufficient: hysteroscopy + directed biopsy
- MRI pelvis once histology confirms malignancy: assess depth of myometrial invasion and lymph node status for staging
- CT CAP: assess for distant metastases

Management (assuming Stage I endometrioid):
- TH+BSO (total hysterectomy + bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy), preferably laparoscopic
- Sentinel lymph node biopsy or pelvic lymph node dissection
- Adjuvant vaginal vault brachytherapy if high-grade or deep myometrial invasion (Stage IB, Grade 3)

Stop the unopposed oestrogen immediately.

**Exam trigger:** The classic presentation is a postmenopausal woman with vaginal bleeding, obesity, nulliparity, diabetes, and/or a history of unopposed oestrogen or tamoxifen use — the answer is endometrial cancer requiring urgent TVS and pipelle biopsy. The most common distractor is attributing postmenopausal bleeding to atrophic vaginitis or HRT-related bleeding without histological exclusion of cancer; even in a woman on HRT, PMB must always be investigated rather than assumed to be benign.
