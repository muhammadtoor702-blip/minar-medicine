## Overview

Rhinitis (inflammation of the nasal mucosa) and rhinosinusitis (inflammation of the nasal mucosa and paranasal sinuses) are among the most prevalent conditions in medicine. Allergic rhinitis affects approximately 25% of the UK population; chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS) affects 10–15% and is one of the most common chronic conditions in adults. The clinical distinction between acute rhinosinusitis (usually viral — self-limiting), allergic rhinitis (immune-mediated), and CRS with or without nasal polyps (CRSwNP vs CRSsNP) is important because management differs substantially. Untreated CRS significantly impairs quality of life, sleep, and productivity. Nasal polyps complicate approximately 20–30% of CRS and are strongly associated with asthma and aspirin sensitivity (Samter's triad).

## Pathophysiology

### Allergic Rhinitis — Type I Hypersensitivity

**Sensitisation phase:** Allergen (pollen, house dust mite, cat dander, mould) → dendritic cell processing → Th2 lymphocyte activation → IL-4/IL-13-driven B-cell class switching → IgE production → IgE binds to mast cells and basophils in nasal mucosa.

**Reaction phase (on re-exposure):**
- **Early phase (immediate, 0–30 min):** Allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells → degranulation → histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes → vasodilation + vascular permeability (rhinorrhoea, nasal obstruction) + neural stimulation (sneezing, pruritus)
- **Late phase (4–8h):** Cytokine-driven eosinophil and basophil recruitment → persistent nasal obstruction + hyposmia

**ARIA (Allergic Rhinitis and its Impact on Asthma) classification:**
- **Intermittent:** <4 days/week OR <4 consecutive weeks
- **Persistent:** >4 days/week AND >4 consecutive weeks
- Severity: **Mild** (does not impair sleep/daily activities) vs **Moderate-Severe** (sleep disturbance, impaired activities, troublesome symptoms)

**Seasonal vs perennial:**
- Seasonal: pollen (tree pollen Feb–May; grass pollen May–Aug; weed/mould Aug–Oct in UK)
- Perennial: house dust mite (Dermatophagoides spp.), animal dander — symptoms year-round; worse in winter (more time indoors)

### Chronic Rhinosinusitis

**European Position Paper on Rhinosinusitis (EPOS 2020) definition:** ≥2 nasal symptoms (nasal blockage, nasal discharge, facial pain/pressure, smell reduction) for ≥12 weeks + endoscopic OR radiological evidence of disease.

**CRS without polyps (CRSsNP):** Predominantly neutrophilic/Th1 pattern; associated with prior infection, anatomical variation, odontogenic infection; responds well to topical steroids + saline irrigation.

**CRS with nasal polyps (CRSwNP):** Predominantly eosinophilic/Th2 type 2 inflammatory pattern; elevated IL-4, IL-5, IL-13; eosinophilic mucosal disease. Associated with atopy, asthma, aspirin sensitivity.

**Samter's Triad (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease — AERD):** Nasal polyps + asthma + sensitivity to aspirin/NSAIDs (inhibition of COX-1 → shunting of arachidonic acid to lipoxygenase pathway → excessive leukotriene production). Salicylate avoidance + leukotriene receptor antagonist (montelukast) + intranasal steroids + aspirin desensitisation in specialist centres.

### Acute Rhinosinusitis

**Viral (most common — 98%):** Rhinovirus, coronavirus, influenza; URTI → mucosal oedema → ostial obstruction → secondary sinus involvement; resolves within 10 days without antibiotics.

**Bacterial superinfection (2–5% of viral rhinosinusitis):**
- *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*
- Clinical features suggesting bacterial (PODS criteria):
  - **P**urulent discharge from nose (unilateral)
  - **O**nset of severe symptoms from the beginning (not a cold that got worse)
  - **D**ouble-sickening (initial improvement then deterioration after day 5–7)
  - **S**ingle-sided facial pain

### Rhinitis Medicamentosa

Rebound nasal congestion from prolonged use of topical decongestant sprays (oxymetazoline, xylometazoline — alpha-2 agonists). Vascular rebound after drug wears off → worse congestion → more spray use → addiction cycle. Minimum 3 months of topical decongestants can cause rhinitis medicamentosa. Treatment: withdrawal of decongestant (may require tapering with intranasal steroid).

## Clinical Presentation

### Symptoms

| Symptom | Allergic Rhinitis | Acute Viral RS | Acute Bacterial RS | CRS |
|---------|------------------|---------------|-------------------|-----|
| Nasal blockage | ✓ (bilateral) | ✓ | ✓ (unilateral) | ✓ |
| Watery rhinorrhoea | ✓✓✓ | ✓ | — | ± |
| Purulent discharge | — | ± (late) | ✓✓ | ✓ (CRSsNP) |
| Sneezing | ✓✓✓ | ✓ | — | ± |
| Itching (eyes, nose, palate) | ✓✓✓ | — | — | — |
| Facial pain/pressure | — | ± | ✓✓ | ± |
| Hyposmia/anosmia | ± | — | ± | ✓✓ (CRSwNP) |
| Duration | Seasonal/perennial | <10 days | >10 days | >12 weeks |

### Nasal Endoscopy Findings

- **Allergic rhinitis:** Pale, oedematous, bluish-grey mucosa; clear watery secretions; inferior turbinate hypertrophy
- **CRSwNP:** Smooth, pale, insensate polyps (grape-like) prolapsing from middle meatus; may fill nasal cavity; bilateral
- **CRSsNP:** Mucopurulent discharge from middle meatus; oedematous mucosa without polyps
- **Unilateral polyp:** Always requires investigation to exclude inverted papilloma or malignancy (not benign bilateral CRS polyps)

### Red Flags — Refer Urgently to ENT

- **Unilateral symptoms** (nasal obstruction, discharge, polyp, crusting) — exclude malignancy
- Epistaxis without obvious cause
- Cacosmia (perception of foul smell — crusting, atrophic rhinitis, malignancy)
- Periorbital cellulitis, proptosis, diplopia (orbital complication of sinusitis)
- Meningism, altered GCS, frontal swelling (intracranial complication — Pott's puffy tumour — frontal osteomyelitis with subperiosteal abscess)
- Facial paraesthesia or palsy
- Cervical lymphadenopathy

### Complications of Acute Sinusitis

**Orbital complications (most common — from ethmoid sinusitis):**
- Pre-septal (periorbital) cellulitis → lid erythema and oedema anterior to orbital septum; no proptosis
- Orbital cellulitis → proptosis + chemosis + restricted painful eye movement + reduced vision
- Subperiosteal abscess (between periorbita and orbital wall) → medial displacement of globe

**Intracranial complications (rare, life-threatening — from frontal sinus):**
- Meningitis, subdural/epidural empyema, cerebral abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis, Pott's puffy tumour

## Diagnosis

### Investigations

**Skin prick testing (SPT) / Specific IgE (RAST):** For suspected allergic rhinitis — identifies specific allergens to guide avoidance advice and immunotherapy targeting. SPT: immediate wheal and flare response to allergen intradermal injection; panels include dust mite, cat, grass, tree pollens, moulds.

**Anterior rhinoscopy/nasal endoscopy:** Flexible nasal endoscopy (in ENT clinic) allows direct visualisation of middle meatus, polyps, purulent discharge, and posterior choanae. Essential for CRS assessment.

**CT sinuses (non-contrast):** **Standard imaging for CRS prior to surgery** — demonstrates mucosal thickening, fluid levels, polyp extent, anatomical variants (Haller cells, concha bullosa, deviated septum), and bony involvement. Not required for uncomplicated acute rhinosinusitis.

**MRI sinuses:** For suspected intracranial extension, soft tissue detail, malignancy, or encephalocele. Better soft tissue differentiation than CT.

**Smell testing (Sniffin' Sticks, University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test):** Objective quantification of anosmia/hyposmia — useful in CRS, post-COVID anosmia, neurological disease.

**Nasal cytology/culture:** In refractory CRS — identify Staphylococcal biofilm, atypical organisms, eosinophilic disease pattern.

## Management

### Allergic Rhinitis

**Step 1 — Allergen avoidance:**
- House dust mite: mattress/pillow covers, hot wash bedding, reduce humidity, remove carpets
- Pollen: sunglasses outdoors, shower after outdoor exposure, keep windows closed during high pollen counts
- Pets: remove animal from home (most effective); HEPA air filtration

**Step 2 — Intranasal corticosteroids (INCS):**
- **First-line pharmacotherapy** for moderate-severe persistent allergic rhinitis
- Mometasone (Nasonex), fluticasone (Flixonase), beclomethasone — all effective
- Onset of action: 3–7 days for full effect — patients must use regularly, not PRN
- Superior to oral antihistamines for nasal blockage; equally effective for rhinorrhoea/sneezing
- Safe for long-term use; minimal systemic absorption with modern preparations

**Step 3 — Antihistamines:**
- Non-sedating second-generation H1 antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine): Excellent for rhinorrhoea, sneezing, itching; less effective for nasal blockage
- Sedating antihistamines (chlorphenamine): Avoid for regular use; impair driving
- **Combined INCS + oral antihistamine** for moderate-severe disease

**Step 4 — Additional options:**
- Sodium cromoglicate nasal spray: Mast cell stabiliser; suitable for children; less effective than INCS
- Leukotriene receptor antagonist (montelukast): Useful when allergic rhinitis + asthma coexist; moderate evidence
- Intranasal decongestants: Short-term (≤5 days) only — rhinitis medicamentosa risk

**Step 5 — Allergen immunotherapy (desensitisation):**
- Subcutaneous immunotherapy (SCIT) or sublingual immunotherapy (SLIT) — modifies underlying immune response
- Indications: severe allergic rhinitis inadequately controlled by pharmacotherapy; identified specific allergens
- Benefits persist for years after completing treatment course (3 years)
- SLIT (e.g., grass pollen sublingual tablets) available OTC in UK; SCIT must be administered in specialist setting (anaphylaxis risk)

### Acute Rhinosinusitis

**Viral (no antibiotics):** Analgesics, saline nasal irrigation, intranasal decongestant short-term. Most resolve in 10 days.

**Bacterial (if PODS criteria or >10 days no improvement):**
- **Amoxicillin 500 mg TID × 5 days** — first-line
- Doxycycline if penicillin allergy
- Co-amoxiclav if first-line fails

**Orbital complication:** IV ceftriaxone + metronidazole; CT orbits; ophthalmology review; consider surgical drainage if orbital abscess.

### Chronic Rhinosinusitis

**Medical management (6–12 weeks trial before surgery):**
- **High-volume saline nasal irrigation** (Neti pot, SinuRinse): Clears debris, reduces mucosal oedema, improves mucociliary clearance — first-line and adjunct throughout treatment
- **Intranasal corticosteroids (INCS):** First-line pharmacotherapy; mometasone or fluticasone spray; drops (betamethasone or fluticasone drops) superior for polyps (better delivery to middle meatus)
- **Short course oral corticosteroids** (prednisolone 40–50 mg × 2 weeks): For CRSwNP — dramatically reduces polyp size; effect temporary; useful as "polyp reduction" before surgery
- **Long-term macrolide antibiotics** (clarithromycin 250 mg daily × 3 months): For CRSsNP — anti-inflammatory (not anti-microbial) mechanism; reduces neutrophilic inflammation
- **Biological therapy:** For severe uncontrolled CRSwNP
  - **Dupilumab (Dupixent):** Anti-IL-4Rα (blocks IL-4 and IL-13 signalling) — dramatically reduces polyp size, improves smell, reduces OCS need; NICER-approved for refractory CRSwNP
  - **Mepolizumab (anti-IL-5):** Reduces eosinophil-driven inflammation

**Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS):**
- Indications: CRS inadequately controlled by medical treatment (typically after 6–12 weeks of INCS + saline); orbital/intracranial complication
- Technique: Endoscopic widening of sinus ostia + removal of disease + polyp debulking; preserves mucosa; allows topical medication to reach sinuses post-operatively
- Outcomes: 80–90% patient satisfaction; polyps recur in ~30% — ongoing INCS essential post-operatively
- Complications: bleeding, orbital injury (CSF leak, optic nerve injury — rare), CSF rhinorrhoea

### Rhinitis Medicamentosa

Gradual withdrawal of nasal decongestant (may require tapering over 3–6 weeks) with initiation of INCS to manage rebound congestion during withdrawal period.

## Complications

- **CRS impact on quality of life:** Comparable to congestive heart failure and COPD on standardised QoL measures — sleep disruption, cognitive impairment, reduced work productivity
- **Nasal polyposis extension:** Large polyps → anosmia, complete nasal obstruction; rarely cause widening of nasal bridge (hypertelorism in severe disease)
- **Asthma-rhinitis comorbidity:** Unified airway disease — CRSwNP and asthma coexist in ~50%; poorly controlled rhinitis worsens asthma control (nasal breathing → filter/humidification of inspired air; post-nasal drip triggers lower airway inflammation)
- **Orbital complications of acute sinusitis:** Blindness from orbital apex syndrome (optic nerve compression) — emergency surgical drainage
- **Post-COVID anosmia:** Prolonged olfactory dysfunction after SARS-CoV-2 — separate entity from CRS; olfactory nerve direct viral invasion; management: olfactory rehabilitation (smell training with 4 scents TID); most recover by 12 months

## Clinical Insight

The diagnosis of chronic rhinosinusitis requires both symptoms AND objective evidence — it cannot be made on symptoms alone, and the "runny nose for years" complaint needs endoscopy or CT to confirm. This matters because the management differs substantially from allergic rhinitis or vasomotor rhinitis. The key endoscopic finding in CRS is mucopurulent discharge from the middle meatus (the opening draining the maxillary, anterior ethmoid, and frontal sinuses via the ostiomeatal complex). A pale polyp prolapsing from the middle meatus in a patient with bilateral disease, anosmia, and asthma is CRSwNP — a Th2 eosinophilic disease that responds to dupilumab.

Rhinitis medicamentosa is one of the most satisfying diagnoses in ENT because it is entirely iatrogenic and completely reversible. Any patient presenting with refractory nasal obstruction should be asked specifically about decongestant nasal spray use — the question needs to be direct ("do you use a nasal spray you can buy at the pharmacy?") because patients do not volunteer this information. The mechanism (tachyphylaxis to the vasoconstrictor effect followed by reactive vasodilation) is classic pharmacology. The solution is abrupt or gradual decongestant withdrawal with bridging intranasal steroid.

Sinusitis as a cause of periorbital oedema in a child is an emergency that must not be sent home with antibiotics alone. The pattern to recognise: child with URTI + increasing unilateral periorbital swelling + fever → urgent CT orbits to stage the orbital complication (Chandler classification). Pre-septal cellulitis → IV antibiotics; subperiosteal/orbital abscess → surgical drainage. Visual acuity and eye movement must be tested in all periorbital swelling — deteriorating vision or ophthalmoplegia → same-day theatre.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 34F, 10-week history of nasal blockage + purulent discharge + facial pressure + anosmia, preceded by URTI, daily decongestant spray for 6 weeks, background of allergic rhinitis, bilateral nasal polyps on endoscopy.

**Diagnosis:**
1. **Chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP)** — 10 weeks of symptoms + endoscopic polyps + purulent discharge
2. **Rhinitis medicamentosa** — 6 weeks of daily decongestant nasal spray use

**Step 1 — Address rhinitis medicamentosa:**
- Advise to STOP oxymetazoline spray immediately (or gradual weaning)
- Start **intranasal fluticasone drops (Flixonase Nasule)** — drops superior to spray for middle meatus delivery in polyp disease; manage rebound congestion during withdrawal

**Step 2 — CRSwNP medical management:**
- High-volume saline nasal irrigation (SinuRinse) twice daily — clears mucus, helps INCS penetration
- **Short course oral prednisolone 40 mg × 14 days with taper** — shrinks polyps, restores smell, allows INCS access
- Continue INCS drops long-term post-steroid course
- Allergy testing: SPT or RAST — background allergic rhinitis should be optimally treated (INCS spray + antihistamine)

**Step 3 — If inadequate response after 12 weeks of medical treatment:**
- CT sinuses — assess sinus anatomy, polyp extent, mucosal disease
- **Refer to ENT for FESS** — opens sinus drainage, removes polyps, allows post-op topical steroid access
- Check aspirin sensitivity (AERD/Samter's triad screen — asthma history? aspirin/NSAID intolerance?)

**Step 4 — If severe/refractory CRSwNP or AERD:**
- **Dupilumab** — NICE-approved for severe uncontrolled CRSwNP; refer to tertiary ENT/immunology centre

**Exam trigger:** *CRSwNP + long-term decongestant use* → diagnose rhinitis medicamentosa and address it; start intranasal steroid drops + oral steroid burst for polyps; FESS if medical failure. The distractor is giving repeated antibiotics for "sinusitis" without addressing the steroid treatment or rhinitis medicamentosa.
