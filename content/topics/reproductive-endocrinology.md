## Overview

Reproductive endocrinology encompasses disorders of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis, from polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS — the most common endocrine condition in women of reproductive age) to menopause and male hypogonadism. These conditions share the common thread of disrupted sex hormone physiology, with downstream effects on fertility, metabolic health, bone density, and quality of life.

---

## Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

### Pathophysiology

PCOS is a complex, heterogeneous disorder defined by hyperandrogenism, ovulatory dysfunction, and polycystic ovarian morphology — present in ~10% of women of reproductive age.

**Central mechanism**: Insulin resistance (present in ~70–80% of PCOS patients regardless of BMI) → compensatory hyperinsulinaemia → insulin amplifies LH-driven ovarian androgen production (theca cell stimulation) → elevated androgens → inhibit follicular maturation → oligo/anovulation → reduced progesterone → unopposed oestrogen → irregular periods.

Simultaneously, hyperinsulinaemia reduces hepatic production of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) → more free (bioavailable) androgen despite total testosterone being only mildly elevated. **Free androgen index** (total testosterone / SHBG × 100) reflects this better than total testosterone alone.

**Hypothalamic dysregulation**: Increased GnRH pulse frequency favours LH over FSH secretion → elevated LH:FSH ratio. FSH is relatively low → granulosa cell stimulation is inadequate for dominant follicle selection → multiple small antral follicles arrest ("polycystic" appearance).

**Genetic basis**: PCOS runs in families (~50% of sisters affected). Multiple susceptibility loci identified (FSHR, LHCGR, INSR).

### Clinical Presentation

**Rotterdam criteria** (2003 — diagnosis requires any 2 of 3):
1. Oligo- or anovulation (cycles >35 days or <8 cycles/year)
2. Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, male-pattern alopecia; or elevated free testosterone / free androgen index)
3. Polycystic ovarian morphology on USS (≥20 follicles per ovary on modern probes, or ovarian volume >10 mL)

Exclude other causes of hyperandrogenism before diagnosing PCOS: congenital adrenal hyperplasia (17-OHP), Cushing's syndrome (clinical features, UFC), androgen-secreting tumour (testosterone >144 ng/dL — investigate urgently for tumour), hyperprolactinaemia, thyroid dysfunction.

**Symptoms:**
- **Menstrual irregularity** — oligomenorrhoea, amenorrhoea, or (less commonly) regular cycles with anovulation
- **Hyperandrogenism** — hirsutism (Ferriman-Gallwey score; predominantly chin, upper lip, abdomen, inner thigh), acne, alopecia (male-pattern hair thinning)
- **Infertility** — anovulatory infertility is the most common cause of female infertility
- **Obesity** — ~50–60% (but 20–30% are lean; lean PCOS still has metabolic risks)
- **Metabolic syndrome** — insulin resistance, impaired fasting glucose, dyslipidaemia, hypertension
- Psychological: anxiety, depression, reduced quality of life

**Acanthosis nigricans** — velvety, hyperpigmented skin in axillae, neck, groin — marker of severe insulin resistance.

**Long-term risks:**
- Type 2 diabetes (4× increased lifetime risk)
- Cardiovascular disease (elevated risk, though absolute excess mortality is debated)
- Endometrial cancer (from chronic unopposed oestrogen in anovulation — 3× increased risk)
- Obstructive sleep apnoea

### Diagnosis

**Biochemistry**: LH, FSH, total testosterone, SHBG, free androgen index, DHEAS, 17-OHP (early morning, follicular phase), prolactin, TFTs, fasting glucose/HbA1c, fasting lipids.

**USS pelvis**: Transvaginal preferred (more accurate than transabdominal). Polycystic morphology defined per Rotterdam criteria. Do not perform ultrasound in adolescents within 8 years of menarche (normal appearance may be misinterpreted).

**OGTT**: If any features of metabolic syndrome, BMI >25, or strong family history of T2DM.

### Management

Management is symptom- and goal-directed. There is no single treatment that addresses all manifestations.

**Lifestyle modification (cornerstone):**
Weight loss of 5–10% improves insulin resistance, reduces androgens, restores menstrual cyclicity, and improves ovulation rates — without any pharmacological intervention. Even in lean PCOS, structured physical activity improves metabolic parameters.

**Menstrual regulation and endometrial protection:**
- Combined OCP: suppresses LH (reduces ovarian androgen production), raises SHBG, provides cycle control, and reduces endometrial hyperplasia risk from prolonged amenorrhoea
- If OCP is not appropriate: progestogen (medroxyprogesterone acetate or norethisterone) for 12–14 days every 3 months minimum to induce withdrawal bleed and protect the endometrium

**Hirsutism and acne:**
- **Combined OCP** (co-cyprindiol/Dianette, or OCP with anti-androgenic progestogen) — most effective hormonal treatment
- **Spironolactone** 50–200 mg/day — anti-androgen; highly effective; requires reliable contraception (teratogenic in male foetuses)
- Eflornithine cream (Vaniqa) — topical; inhibits facial hair growth
- Laser hair removal, electrolysis

**Ovulation induction for fertility:**
1. **Weight loss first** — restores ovulation in many anovulatory women with PCOS and obesity
2. **Letrozole** (aromatase inhibitor) — first-line for ovulation induction (superior to clomifene — LETROZOLE trial); oral, days 2–6 of cycle
3. **Clomifene citrate** — oral anti-oestrogen; second-line; risk of multiple pregnancy
4. **Metformin** — improves insulin sensitivity and ovulation; particularly useful in PCOS with insulin resistance; often combined with letrozole
5. **Gonadotrophin injections** — if oral agents fail; close monitoring required
6. **Laparoscopic ovarian drilling** — unipolar diathermy to the ovarian surface; restores ovulation in ~70%; mechanism uncertain (reduces androgen and LH)
7. **IVF** — if all above fail

**Metabolic management:**
- **Metformin** — reduces insulin resistance, lowers androgen levels, improves menstrual regularity, reduces T2DM risk. Consider in all PCOS patients with impaired fasting glucose or metabolic syndrome.
- Statin if dyslipidaemia meets treatment criteria
- Annual HbA1c and lipid monitoring

## Menopause

**Definition**: Cessation of menstruation for 12 consecutive months due to ovarian follicle depletion and loss of oestrogen production. Mean age ~51 years in the UK (range 45–55). Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) — menopause before age 40.

**Peri-menopause**: The transitional phase preceding the final period — irregular cycles, rising FSH, declining oestrogen, and emerging symptoms.

**Clinical features (from oestrogen withdrawal):**
- **Vasomotor symptoms**: Hot flushes (spontaneous feelings of intense heat, flushing, and sweating) — most common symptom; affect ~75%; can be severely disabling
- **Urogenital atrophy**: Vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency and frequency (genitourinary syndrome of menopause)
- **Mood changes**: Low mood, anxiety, irritability, poor concentration, sleep disturbance
- **Bone loss**: Accelerated in the first 5 years post-menopause — osteoporosis risk
- **Cardiovascular risk**: Oestrogen is cardioprotective; post-menopausal women lose this protection

**Diagnosis**: Clinical in women >45 with typical symptoms and irregular/absent periods. FSH >30 IU/L (taken twice, ≥6 weeks apart) in women under 45.

### Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT)

HRT replaces oestrogen (± progesterone in women with a uterus to prevent endometrial hyperplasia) and is the most effective treatment for menopausal symptoms.

**Benefits:**
- Most effective treatment for hot flushes and night sweats (reduces by 75%)
- Prevents and treats urogenital atrophy
- Prevents bone loss and reduces osteoporotic fracture risk
- May reduce cardiovascular risk if started early (within 10 years of menopause or before age 60) — the "timing hypothesis"
- May reduce colorectal cancer risk

**Risks (context-dependent):**
- **Breast cancer risk**: Combined HRT (oestrogen + progestogen) increases breast cancer risk after 5 years of use; oestrogen-only HRT (for women post-hysterectomy) carries lower or no increased risk. Absolute risk increase is small for most women.
- **VTE risk**: Oral oestrogen increases VTE risk. **Transdermal oestrogen does not** — avoid oral oestrogen in women with VTE risk factors.
- **Stroke**: Small increased risk with oral oestrogen; transdermal oestrogen does not increase stroke risk.

**HRT formulations:**
- Oestradiol (transdermal — patch, gel, or spray) + micronised progesterone (Utrogestan) — preferred combination; lowest risk profile
- Tibolone — tissue-selective steroid with oestrogenic, progestogenic, and androgenic activity
- Local vaginal oestrogen (cream, pessary, ring) — for urogenital symptoms only; very low systemic absorption

**Contraindications to systemic HRT**: Oestrogen-sensitive cancers (breast, endometrial), unexplained vaginal bleeding, active liver disease, VTE on anticoagulation (use transdermal).

**Duration**: NICE NG23 (2023): No mandatory time limit — the benefits and risks should be reviewed annually with individual patients. For women with severe symptoms, HRT can be continued indefinitely with informed shared decision-making.

**Non-hormonal alternatives (for women who cannot take HRT):**
- SSRIs/SNRIs (paroxetine, venlafaxine) — reduce hot flush frequency by 50–60%
- Clonidine (alpha-agonist) — modest efficacy
- Gabapentin — effective for vasomotor symptoms
- Fezolinetant (neurokinin 3 receptor antagonist) — newly licensed; highly effective; non-hormonal

## Male Hypogonadism

**Primary hypogonadism** (testicular failure): Low testosterone + elevated LH/FSH. Causes: Klinefelter syndrome (XXY — most common genetic cause), orchitis (mumps), torsion, chemotherapy, radiation.

**Secondary hypogonadism** (hypothalamic/pituitary failure): Low testosterone + low/normal LH/FSH. Causes: hypopituitarism, prolactinoma, haemochromatosis, obesity (elevated oestrogen from peripheral aromatisation), exogenous testosterone abuse, opioid use.

**Clinical**: Reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, fatigue, reduced muscle mass, gynaecomastia, osteoporosis, mood disturbance.

**Diagnosis**: Total testosterone (8–10am sample, fasting), LH, FSH, prolactin, SHBG, full blood count, DEXA scan for bone density.

**Management**: Testosterone replacement therapy (gel, patch, or IM injection) for symptomatic hypogonadism with confirmed low testosterone. Monitor Hb (polycythaemia), PSA (prostate), bone density. Clomifene or pulsatile GnRH to stimulate testicular function if fertility is desired.
## Complications

- **PCOS**: metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes (5-10x increased risk), cardiovascular disease, endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma (from chronic anovulation), infertility, psychological morbidity
- **Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS)**: complication of gonadotrophin therapy — third-space fluid shift, ascites, pleural effusion, AKI, VTE; severe OHSS requires hospitalisation
- **Premature ovarian insufficiency**: premature oestrogen deficiency causing osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease, and infertility; HRT is mandatory until natural menopause age
- **Untreated male hypogonadism**: osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome, sexual dysfunction, infertility, cardiovascular risk
- **HRT**: small increased risk of breast cancer with combined oestrogen-progestogen; VTE risk lower with transdermal route

## Clinical Insight

PCOS is a metabolic condition as much as a reproductive one. The ovarian and menstrual features are the presenting complaint — but the insulin resistance, cardiovascular risk, and type 2 diabetes risk are the long-term determinants of health outcomes. Every woman with PCOS should have an annual HbA1c, fasting lipids, and blood pressure check — regardless of her current reproductive goals.

HRT has been dramatically under-prescribed since the 2002 WHI trial, which was widely misinterpreted as showing HRT was unsafe for all women. The WHI studied an older, obese population using oral conjugated equine oestrogen + medroxyprogesterone acetate — a preparation no longer favoured. Modern HRT (transdermal oestradiol + micronised progesterone) has a considerably more favourable risk profile. The 2022 NICE guidelines explicitly state that for most women with menopausal symptoms, the benefits of HRT outweigh the risks. Many women are still being denied effective treatment based on outdated evidence.

Endometrial cancer risk in PCOS is real and preventable. Prolonged amenorrhoea (>3–4 months) leads to unopposed oestrogen exposure and endometrial proliferation. Inducing a withdrawal bleed with progestogen at least every 3 months — or prescribing the combined OCP — is both therapeutic and cancer-preventive. An irregular period in PCOS is not just an inconvenience; it is an endometrial cancer risk factor that requires active management.
