## Overview

Cirrhosis represents the end-stage of all chronic liver diseases — the final common pathway of sustained hepatic injury leading to diffuse fibrosis, architectural distortion, and regenerative nodule formation. Despite widely different aetiologies, cirrhosis produces a consistent clinical syndrome through two main pathophysiological consequences: **portal hypertension** (causing varices, ascites, and splenomegaly) and **hepatocellular dysfunction** (causing impaired synthetic function, encephalopathy, and jaundice). The complications of cirrhosis are themselves life-threatening and require systematic anticipation and management. Overall 5-year survival is ~50% — substantially reduced in decompensated disease.

---

## Pathophysiology

**Fibrosis and cirrhosis**: Repeated hepatic injury from any cause activates hepatic stellate cells (Ito cells), which normally store vitamin A in the space of Disse. With injury, they transdifferentiate into myofibroblasts, secreting collagen (predominantly types I and III) into the space of Disse. Diffuse perisinusoidal fibrosis obliterates normal hepatic architecture, compresses sinusoids, and creates regenerative nodules. These nodules lack normal portal-hepatic blood flow, resulting in both functional impairment and increased intrahepatic vascular resistance.

**Portal hypertension**: Normal portal venous pressure is 5–10 mmHg. In cirrhosis, fibrotic bands and regenerative nodules increase intrahepatic vascular resistance. Portal pressure >12 mmHg is the threshold for clinically significant portal hypertension — variceal formation, ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy risk escalate above this level. Splanchnic vasodilation (nitric oxide-mediated) increases portal blood flow, perpetuating and worsening portal hypertension. The RAAS and sympathetic nervous system are activated to compensate for reduced effective circulating volume — causing renal sodium and water retention and perpetuating ascites.

**Jaundice in cirrhosis**: Results from impaired hepatocyte conjugation and excretion of bilirubin, and from cholestasis with biliary obstruction. Bilirubin >2 mg/dL is visible clinically as jaundice.

**Common causes of cirrhosis:**

| Cause | Notes and Diagnostic Clues |
|-------|---------------------------|
| Alcohol-related liver disease | Most common in UK; GGT disproportionately elevated, MCV elevated, raised AST:ALT ratio >2:1; CAGE questionnaire |
| Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD/MAFLD) | Fastest growing cause worldwide; associated with metabolic syndrome (obesity, T2DM, dyslipidaemia, hypertension); now called MASLD |
| Chronic hepatitis C (HCV) | Blood-borne; ~25% develop cirrhosis in 20 years; curable with direct-acting antivirals |
| Chronic hepatitis B (HBV) | Blood-borne and sexual; treat with tenofovir or entecavir; HCC risk even without cirrhosis |
| Primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) | Middle-aged women (~90%); elevated ALP; antimitochondrial antibody (AMA) >95% sensitive and specific; ursodeoxycholic acid 13–15 mg/kg/day |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) | Strongly associated with UC; elevated ALP; beaded biliary tree on MRCP; cholangiocarcinoma risk; no proven medical therapy |
| Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) | Elevated IgG; ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibody (SMA), anti-LKM1; responds to prednisolone ± azathioprine |
| Haemochromatosis | Hereditary iron overload; HFE gene (C282Y homozygosity); ferritin >1000 µg/L, transferrin saturation >45%; treatment: phlebotomy |
| Wilson's disease | Copper accumulation; age <40; Kayser-Fleischer rings (copper deposited in Descemet's membrane); low serum caeruloplasmin; treat with penicillamine or trientine |
| Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency | PiZZ phenotype; accumulation of abnormal protein in hepatocytes + lung disease (emphysema); consider liver transplant in end-stage disease |

## Clinical Presentation

**Compensated cirrhosis**: May be entirely asymptomatic, discovered incidentally on imaging or abnormal liver function tests. Fatigue, anorexia, and right upper quadrant discomfort are common. Physical signs may be subtle or absent.

**Decompensated cirrhosis** — the development of any one of the following major complications: ascites, variceal haemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice, or spontaneous bacterial peritonitis. Decompensation marks a significant inflection point in prognosis.

**Signs of chronic liver disease** (consequences of impaired oestrogen metabolism, portal hypertension, and reduced synthetic function):

- **Hands/arms**: Leuconychia (white nails — hypoalbuminaemia), Terry's nails (proximal white, distal red — telangiectasias), clubbing, palmar erythema, Dupuytren's contracture (alcohol-related), asterixis/liver flap (encephalopathy)
- **Face/chest**: Parotid enlargement (alcohol), jaundice, spider naevi (>5 is significant — in the SVC drainage territory; central arteriole refills from centre outward, unlike telangiectasias), gynaecomastia (excess oestrogen), xanthelasma (PBC)
- **Abdomen**: Hepatomegaly or small liver (late disease), splenomegaly, ascites (shifting dullness, fluid thrill), caput medusae (dilated periumbilical veins from portosystemic shunting)
- **Other**: Testicular atrophy, loss of body hair, fetor hepaticus (sweet, musty "pear-drop" smell from dimethyl sulphide and ketones on the breath — severe hepatic failure)

**The major complications and their mechanisms:**

| Complication | Mechanism | Key Management |
|-------------|-----------|---------------|
| Variceal haemorrhage | Portal HTN >12 mmHg → portosystemic collaterals → varices → rupture | Band ligation; terlipressin IV; prophylaxis with propranolol |
| Ascites | Portal HTN + hypoalbuminaemia + RAAS activation → sodium/water retention | Sodium restriction; spironolactone; furosemide; LVP + albumin |
| Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) | Bacterial translocation → infection of ascitic fluid | IV ceftriaxone + IV albumin; norfloxacin prophylaxis |
| Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) | Portosystemic shunting → ammonia bypasses liver → astrocyte swelling | Lactulose; rifaximin; treat precipitant |
| Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS) | Splanchnic vasodilation → renal vasoconstriction → functional renal failure | Terlipressin + albumin; dialysis bridge; transplant |
| Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) | Chronic regeneration and DNA damage → oncogenesis | 6-monthly USS + AFP surveillance in all cirrhotic patients |
| Coagulopathy | Reduced hepatic synthesis of clotting factors (II, V, VII, IX, X, protein C/S) | Vitamin K; FFP/PCC for active bleeding |

## Diagnosis

**Liver synthetic function markers**: Albumin (reduced in chronic liver failure — best marker of chronic synthetic dysfunction), PT/INR (prolonged — most sensitive for acute deterioration), and bilirubin (elevated as disease progresses). Together, these three form the basis of prognostic scoring.

**Child-Pugh score** (quantifies severity of cirrhosis, guides transplant decisions):

| Variable | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Bilirubin | <2 mg/dL | 2–3 mg/dL | >3 mg/dL |
| Albumin | >3.5 g/dL | 2.8–3.5 g/dL | <2.8 g/dL |
| PT prolongation | <4 seconds | 4–6 seconds | >6 seconds |
| Ascites | None | Mild/controlled | Severe/refractory |
| Encephalopathy | None | Grade 1–2 | Grade 3–4 |

- **Child-Pugh A** (5–6 points): Compensated; 1-year survival >95%
- **Child-Pugh B** (7–9 points): Moderate; begin listing assessment for transplant
- **Child-Pugh C** (10–15 points): Decompensated; 1-year survival ~35%; high priority for transplant

**MELD score (Model for End-Stage Liver Disease)**: 10 × [0.957 × ln(creatinine) + 0.378 × ln(bilirubin) + 1.12 × ln(INR) + 0.643]. Scores 6–40; predicts 90-day mortality. Used for transplant prioritisation in the USA; **UKELD score** (incorporating serum sodium + creatinine + bilirubin + INR) used in the UK — UKELD ≥49 indicates >9% 1-year mortality and warrants transplant listing.

**Liver biopsy**: Gold standard for diagnosis and fibrosis staging, but non-invasive assessment (FibroScan — transient elastography, measuring liver stiffness in kPa; FIB-4 score — combines age, AST, ALT, and platelet count) is now preferred for initial staging to avoid biopsy risk.

**USS abdomen**: Assess liver texture (nodular surface, heterogeneous parenchyma), portal vein diameter (>13 mm suggests portal hypertension), spleen size (splenomegaly from portal hypertension), ascites. **6-monthly USS + AFP for HCC surveillance in all cirrhotic patients** — this is a fundamental standard of care.

**Ascitic fluid analysis** (diagnostic paracentesis in all new ascites or on decompensation):
- Neutrophil count ≥250 cells/mm³ = SBP — treat immediately without waiting for cultures
- SAAG (serum-ascites albumin gradient) ≥11 g/L = portal hypertension as cause (>97% sensitivity)
- Cytology — malignant cells suggest HCC or peritoneal carcinomatosis
- Culture (send 10 mL into blood culture bottles at the bedside) for identification and sensitivities

**Serology for underlying cause**: Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-HCV antibody, immunoglobulins (elevated IgG in AIH, IgM in PBC), autoantibodies (ANA, SMA, anti-LKM1 in AIH; AMA in PBC), ferritin + transferrin saturation (haemochromatosis), caeruloplasmin (Wilson's — low; unreliable in acute liver failure), alpha-1 antitrypsin level + phenotype, alpha-fetoprotein (HCC).

## Viral Hepatitis

**Hepatitis B (HBV)** — DNA virus; blood-borne, sexual, vertical transmission:

| Marker | Significance |
|--------|-------------|
| HBsAg | Surface antigen — present in active infection; persistent >6 months = chronic carrier |
| HBeAg | e antigen — marker of active viral replication and high infectivity |
| Anti-HBs | Immunity (from vaccination or recovery from infection) |
| Anti-HBc IgM | Acute hepatitis B |
| Anti-HBc IgG | Past infection |
| HBV DNA | Viral load — used to guide treatment |

Vaccination generates anti-HBs only (no anti-HBc). HBV carriers with ALT >30 IU/L, cirrhosis, or HBV DNA >2000 IU/mL require antiviral treatment: **tenofovir or entecavir** (oral nucleoside analogues — preferred over pegylated interferon for their superior efficacy and tolerability; required long-term as they suppress but rarely eradicate HBV). HBV increases risk of HCC even without cirrhosis — 6-monthly USS surveillance.

**Hepatitis C (HCV)** — RNA flavivirus; blood-borne (IVDU, blood products, sexual):
- ~85% develop chronic infection; ~25% develop cirrhosis over 20 years; 4% per year develop HCC once cirrhosis is established
- **Curable in >95%**: Direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) — NS5B/NS5A inhibitors (ledipasvir + sofosbuvir, glecaprevir + pibrentasvir, or sofosbuvir + velpatasvir for 8–12 weeks). Interferon-free regimens — excellent tolerance and near-universal cure across all genotypes including in established cirrhosis.

## Budd-Chiari Syndrome

Hepatic venous outflow obstruction — thrombosis of the hepatic veins or inferior vena cava at the hepatic vein level, causing hepatic venous congestion and sinusoidal hypertension.

**FA buzzword**: Young woman + hepatomegaly + ascites + right upper quadrant pain = Budd-Chiari until proven otherwise.

**Causes**: Hypercoagulable states in ~75%:
- **Most common**: Polycythaemia vera (JAK2 V617F mutation — screen all Budd-Chiari), antiphospholipid syndrome, paroxysmal nocturnal haemoglobinuria (PNH — FLAER test), factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation
- Oral contraceptive pill (particularly combined with thrombophilia)
- Pregnancy/puerperium
- Myeloproliferative neoplasms, Behçet's disease, antithrombin III deficiency

**Clinical presentation**: Acute or subacute hepatic failure, hepatomegaly (tender, congested liver), ascites (refractory and protein-rich), right upper quadrant pain, jaundice. Caudate lobe hypertrophy is a characteristic finding — the caudate lobe drains directly into the IVC and is spared from hepatic vein thrombosis, undergoing compensatory hypertrophy.

**Diagnosis**:
- **Doppler USS** (first-line) — absent or reversed hepatic vein flow; caudate lobe hypertrophy. Sensitivity limited by operator expertise and body habitus.
- **CT or MRI with venography** — confirms the diagnosis; identifies extent of thrombosis and caudate hypertrophy; the "comma-shaped" caudate lobe is characteristic on CT
- Liver biopsy: centrilobular congestion and necrosis (hepatic venules are the inflow, so centrilobular regions are most congested — the reverse of portal vein thrombosis)
- **Thrombophilia screen** (after anticoagulation is established): JAK2 mutation, antiphospholipid antibodies, factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutation, protein C/S, antithrombin III, PNH screen

**Management**:
- **Anticoagulation** (heparin then warfarin or DOAC) — all patients unless transplant is imminent
- Treat the underlying cause (e.g., hydroxyurea for PV; eculizumab for PNH; stop OCP)
- **TIPSS** — the primary intervention for acute Budd-Chiari; decompresses hepatic sinusoidal hypertension and restores hepatic blood flow; improves 5-year survival
- **Liver transplantation** — for acute liver failure or chronic disease not amenable to TIPSS. Lifelong anticoagulation required post-transplant (the underlying thrombophilia persists)

## Management

### Ascites

- Sodium restriction <88 mEq/day (<2 g/day of dietary sodium) — the mainstay; salt is the driver of fluid retention
- **Spironolactone** (aldosterone antagonist) — first-line; start 100 mg/day, titrate up to 400 mg/day. Counters the deranged RAAS. Watch for hyperkalaemia.
- **Furosemide** — add if spironolactone alone insufficient; maintain 100:40 ratio with spironolactone to maintain normonatraemia. Monitor U&E frequently.
- **Target weight loss**: 0.5 kg/day (with ascites only); 1 kg/day if peripheral oedema also present — faster loss risks precipitating HRS
- **Large-volume paracentesis (LVP)**: For tense or refractory ascites. Give **IV human albumin 8 g per litre of ascites drained** to prevent post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction (PPCD) — without albumin replacement, the already-vasodilated splanchnic circulation is further compromised, precipitating HRS. Failure to give albumin with LVP is a common and dangerous omission.
- **TIPSS** (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt): Metal stent placed between portal vein and hepatic vein, decompress portal pressure — for refractory ascites, reduces need for repeat paracentesis, but worsens hepatic encephalopathy (bypasses hepatic ammonia clearance)

### Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP)

Infection of ascitic fluid without any intra-abdominal source. Organisms: typically Gram-negative enteric bacteria (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella spp.*) or *Streptococcus* from bacterial translocation through the leaky gut wall. **Any cirrhotic patient with ascites who deteriorates suddenly — confusion, fever, abdominal pain — should be suspected and tapped immediately.**

- Diagnose: ascitic fluid neutrophil count ≥250 cells/mm³ — treat immediately without waiting for culture
- **IV ceftriaxone 2 g/day for 5 days** (first-line; piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g 8-hourly as an alternative)
- **IV albumin 1.5 g/kg on day 1 and 1 g/kg on day 3** — reduces HRS development and mortality in RCTs (Sort et al. 1999 — still the landmark trial; albumin prevents renal vasoconstriction triggered by the inflammatory response to infection)
- **Long-term secondary prophylaxis** after first SBP episode: oral norfloxacin 400 mg/day (reduces translocation of Gram-negative organisms); co-trimoxazole as an alternative. Primary prophylaxis with ciprofloxacin for high-risk patients (low ascitic protein <15 g/L + Child-Pugh C or renal impairment).

### Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)

Neuropsychiatric syndrome from accumulation of ammonia (and other nitrogenous waste) bypassing the liver through portosystemic shunts, causing astrocyte swelling and cerebral oedema. Grades:
- **Grade I**: Altered mood/behaviour, reversal of sleep pattern, impaired concentration
- **Grade II**: Increasing drowsiness, confusion, liver flap (asterixis)
- **Grade III**: Incoherent, restless, stupor, liver flap
- **Grade IV**: Coma

**Identify and treat the precipitant first** — this is the most important step:
- Infection (especially SBP, UTI, pneumonia) — most common
- GI bleeding (blood in gut is a nitrogenous load — 500 mL of blood in the GI tract is equivalent to a large protein meal)
- Constipation, dehydration
- Electrolyte disturbance (hypokalaemia stimulates renal ammoniagenesis)
- Sedating drugs (opioids, benzodiazepines)
- Renal failure

- **Lactulose** 30–50 mL 8-hourly — acidifies the colon (pH reduction traps NH₄⁺, reducing ammonia absorption) and stimulates catharsis. Target 2–4 soft stools per day. First-line for all grades.
- **Rifaximin** 550 mg 12-hourly — non-absorbable oral antibiotic that reduces gut ammonia-producing bacteria. Add to lactulose for recurrent or refractory HE (RFHE trial: 58% reduction in HE episodes, 50% reduction in hospitalisation). Minimal systemic effects; safe in renal impairment.

### Variceal Haemorrhage

See Upper GI Disease for acute management. **Primary prophylaxis** for medium/large varices: non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol 20–40 mg BD, titrated to resting heart rate 55–60 bpm; carvedilol 6.25 mg BD) or endoscopic band ligation (EBL) — reduces first bleed risk by ~50%. **Secondary prophylaxis**: propranolol + EBL (both combined are superior to either alone). TIPSS for patients who rebleed despite optimal medical and endoscopic therapy.

### Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)

Functional renal failure in advanced cirrhosis — renal vasoconstriction secondary to severe splanchnic vasodilation reducing effective renal perfusion. Kidneys are structurally normal — renal function recovers with liver transplantation.

- **HRS type 1 (HRS-AKI)**: Rapid creatinine doubling within 2 weeks. Median survival without treatment ~2 weeks. Triggered by SBP, LVP without albumin, nephrotoxins, hypovolaemia.
- **HRS type 2 (HRS-CKD)**: More gradual; typically in refractory ascites; survival ~3–6 months.

Diagnosis: creatinine rising in cirrhosis + ascites after exclusion of other causes (pre-renal, nephrotoxins, intrinsic renal disease). Urea is unreliable in liver failure (hepatic synthesis is impaired); use creatinine.

**Treatment**: **Terlipressin 2 mg IV every 4–6 hours** + **IV albumin 1 g/kg/day** — terlipressin is a splanchnic vasoconstrictor (V1 agonist) that increases systemic vascular resistance and improves renal perfusion. Reverses HRS-AKI in ~40–50% of patients. Renal replacement therapy as bridge. Liver transplantation is the only definitive treatment.

### Acute Liver Failure

Life-threatening liver injury in a previously healthy liver causing coagulopathy (INR >1.5) and encephalopathy within 26 weeks of onset. Hyperacute (<7 days) — most commonly paracetamol overdose in the UK; acute (7–21 days) — hepatitis A/B; subacute (4–26 weeks) — drug reactions, autoimmune hepatitis.

**King's College criteria** — predict poor outcome and need for transplant listing:

*Paracetamol-induced*:
- Arterial pH <7.3 after adequate resuscitation, >24 hours post-ingestion

*Or all three of*:
- PT >100 seconds (INR >6.5)
- Creatinine >3.4 mg/dL
- Grade III or IV encephalopathy

*Non-paracetamol*:
Three of five criteria:
1. Drug-induced cause
2. Age <10 or >40 years
3. Duration >7 days from jaundice to encephalopathy
4. PT >50 seconds
5. Bilirubin >17.5 mg/dL

### Liver Transplantation

The only definitive treatment for end-stage liver disease and acute liver failure meeting transplant criteria.

**Chronic indications**: Advanced cirrhosis of any cause (UKELD ≥49 for listing); hepatocellular cancer (Milan criteria: single lesion ≤5 cm or ≤3 lesions all ≤3 cm). Alcohol-related cirrhosis requires alcohol abstinence ≥6 months — demonstrated through clinical review, not just patient report.

**Contraindications**: Extrahepatic malignancy, severe cardiorespiratory disease, active sepsis, ongoing alcohol use (alcohol-related disease), expected medication non-compliance.

**Outcomes**: 1-year survival ~80%; 5-year survival 60–90% depending on indication. Immunosuppression (tacrolimus ± mycophenolate mofetil + prednisolone tapering) required lifelong.

## Complications

- Variceal haemorrhage — 30% 6-week mortality per episode even with treatment
- HRS — median survival 2 weeks untreated; reversal possible with terlipressin + albumin
- HCC — 2–3% per year incidence in cirrhotic patients; surveillance essential
- Malnutrition and sarcopenia — major independent determinant of post-transplant and overall outcomes; nutritional support and exercise prescription are underutilised interventions
- Coagulopathy — cirrhotic patients are paradoxically both pro-thrombotic (low protein C/S) and pro-haemorrhagic; standard INR overestimates bleeding risk because thrombin generation is rebalanced

## Clinical Insight

Albumin infusion after large-volume paracentesis is not optional. Draining 5 or more litres of ascites without albumin replacement causes post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction — the already-vasodilated splanchnic circulation receives a further reduction in effective volume, precipitating acute renal failure (HRS). The dose is **8 g of albumin per litre of ascites removed**, given as a concurrent infusion. This single step prevents a potentially fatal complication. The cost of the albumin is trivial compared with the cost of an HRS admission.

Rifaximin should be added to lactulose in any patient with a second episode of overt hepatic encephalopathy. It reduces the number of episodes by 58% and halves hospitalisations. Its non-absorbed profile means it can be used in patients with renal impairment and carries minimal *C. difficile* risk compared with systemically absorbed antibiotics.

A cirrhotic patient presenting with decompensation (new encephalopathy, worsening ascites, jaundice, or bleeding) requires a systematic search for a precipitant before attributing the deterioration to disease progression. The mnemonic for precipitants is: dehydration, constipation, covert alcohol relapse, infection (especially SBP — always tap the ascites), opiate use, and occult GI bleeding. Portal vein thrombosis is a less common but important precipitant. Missing a treatable precipitant in a patient who "appears to be progressing" is one of the most common avoidable errors in liver medicine.
