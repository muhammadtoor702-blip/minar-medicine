## Overview

Glaucoma is a progressive optic neuropathy characterised by characteristic patterns of optic nerve damage (loss of retinal ganglion cells and their axons at the optic nerve head) and corresponding visual field loss, with or without elevated intraocular pressure (IOP). It is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide, affecting approximately 60 million people. The fundamental problem is that glaucoma is asymptomatic until advanced — peripheral visual field is lost first, and by the time central vision is affected, up to 90% of retinal ganglion cells may have been destroyed. Earlier detection through screening and treatment to lower IOP remain the cornerstones of management. Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), the most common form, is a chronic, slowly progressive disease managed entirely differently from acute angle-closure glaucoma (AACG), which is an emergency.

## Pathophysiology

### Aqueous Humour Dynamics and IOP

**Normal aqueous circulation:**
Ciliary body (non-pigmented epithelium) → produces ~2–2.5 μL/min of aqueous → posterior chamber → through pupil → anterior chamber → **trabecular meshwork** (80% of drainage) → Schlemm's canal → aqueous collector channels → episcleral veins → systemic venous circulation. Minor: **uveoscleral pathway** (20%) — through ciliary muscle.

**IOP determinants:** IOP = (Rate of aqueous production) / (Facility of outflow)

Normal IOP: 10–21 mmHg (mean ~15 mmHg, SD 3 mmHg). IOP >21 mmHg = ocular hypertension (OHT) — a risk factor for glaucoma, not glaucoma itself.

### Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma — Mechanism

The angle (between iris and cornea) is open, but the trabecular meshwork itself becomes dysfunctional → **increased resistance to aqueous outflow** → elevated IOP → mechanical compression and ischaemic damage to axons at the optic nerve head (lamina cribrosa — the structural weak point where axons exit the eye).

**Retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death:**
- RGC axons compressed at lamina cribrosa → axoplasmic flow interrupted → loss of neurotrophic factor supply to RGC soma → apoptosis
- Loss follows anatomical patterns: inferior and superior poles of disc (corresponding to superior and inferior arcuate visual field defects)
- Optic disc changes: **cup enlargement** (cup:disc ratio >0.6 = suspicious; >0.8 = significant); **rim loss** (inferior > superior > nasal > temporal — ISNT rule for normal thickness order; violation indicates glaucoma); **notching** (focal rim loss); disc haemorrhage (Drance haemorrhage — specific predictor of progression)

**Risk factors for POAG:**
- IOP elevation (strongest modifiable factor) — but ~30–40% of POAG have IOP consistently <21 mmHg (normal tension glaucoma — NPT/NTG)
- Age (major risk factor; rare <40)
- Family history (first-degree relative: 4–9× increased risk)
- African or Caribbean ancestry (higher prevalence, earlier onset, more severe)
- Myopia (structurally susceptible disc/retina)
- Thin central corneal thickness (CCT) — thinner CCT = underestimates true IOP + independent structural risk factor
- Systemic hypotension (nocturnal dips reduce optic nerve perfusion pressure)

### Normal Tension Glaucoma (NTG)

Glaucomatous optic nerve damage and visual field loss with consistently normal IOP (<21 mmHg). Vascular mechanism (insufficient optic nerve perfusion) rather than mechanical IOP elevation proposed. Associated with: vasospasm (migraine, Raynaud's), systemic hypotension, sleep apnoea, nocturnal hypotension from antihypertensive overtreatment. Treated by lowering IOP even further below "normal" (by 30% from baseline).

### Pseudoexfoliation Glaucoma

Fibrillar proteinaceous material (pseudoexfoliative material, XFM) deposited on lens capsule, pupil margin, trabecular meshwork → clog drainage → IOP elevation. Associated with: cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer's disease, hearing loss. More severe disease than POAG; responds well to IOP-lowering but progresses more rapidly.

## Clinical Presentation

### Chronic (Open-Angle) Glaucoma — Asymptomatic Until Late

- No pain (contrast with acute angle-closure)
- No redness
- Visual acuity preserved until very late (central vision spared until advanced disease)
- Peripheral visual field loss — not noticed by patient until very advanced
- **Incidental discovery:** By optometrist during routine test (raised IOP, disc changes) — most glaucoma in UK is detected this way

### Visual Field Defects in Glaucoma

RGC axons follow arcuate paths from the periphery to the optic disc; corresponding visual field defects are arcuate:
- **Paracentral scotoma** (early — above or below fixation)
- **Arcuate scotoma (Bjerrum scotoma)** — extends from blind spot in an arc above/below fixation; characteristic of glaucoma
- **Nasal step** — defect that does not cross the horizontal meridian (nasal field of arcuate nerve fibres from opposite pole)
- **Altitudinal defect** (superior or inferior) — advanced disease
- **Tunnel vision** — end-stage (only central island remains)
- **Temporal island remains** — the very last bit of visual field to be lost (temporal fibres most resistant)

### Optic Disc Assessment

**Cup:disc ratio (CDR):** Ratio of vertical cup diameter to vertical disc diameter. Normal ~0.4–0.5; suspicious >0.6; in glaucoma, progressive enlargement. **Asymmetry >0.2 between eyes is suspicious.**

**Neuroretinal rim (NRR):** The tissue between cup edge and disc margin. Normal thickness: inferior > superior > nasal > temporal (**ISNT rule**). Violation of ISNT rule (e.g., inferior thinner than superior) = suspect glaucoma.

**Key disc changes in glaucoma:**
- NRR thinning or notching (inferior pole most susceptible)
- Disc haemorrhage (Drance haemorrhage at inferior or superior pole — indicates active progression)
- **Bayoneting** of vessels at the disc rim (vessels bend as they exit the enlarged cup)
- Peripapillary atrophy (atrophy of RPE/choroid around disc — "halo")
- Vertical elongation of cup

## Diagnosis

### Glaucoma Assessment

**IOP measurement (applanation tonometry):** Goldmann applanation tonometry is gold standard. **Central corneal thickness (CCT) correction:** Thick cornea (>580 μm) overestimates IOP; thin cornea (<510 μm) underestimates (and is independent risk factor). Most tonometers do not automatically correct for CCT.

**Gonioscopy:** Direct visualisation of the anterior chamber angle using a mirrored contact lens. **Essential to distinguish open-angle from narrow/closed angle** — determines glaucoma type and management. Open angle (grade 3–4 Shaffer) = POAG; closed angle = AACG or chronic angle-closure.

**Visual field testing (perimetry):**
- Humphrey visual field analyser (HVF): Standard; 24-2 program tests central 24°; provides mean deviation (MD), pattern standard deviation (PSD), and reliability indices
- Reliable field requires patient cooperation; assessed for fixation losses, false positives/negatives
- **Progression analysis:** Serial fields compared to identify worsening (MD worsening >–0.5 dB/year = fast progressor)

**Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of optic nerve and RNFL:**
- Retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL) thickness measured circumferentially around optic disc
- Thinning of RNFL precedes visual field loss by several years — earlier detection of progression
- Ganglion cell analysis (GCA) of macula: complementary measure
- OCT provides objective, reproducible measurement compared to subjective disc photography

**Corneal thickness measurement (pachymetry):** For IOP correction and risk stratification.

## Management

### Medical Management — IOP Lowering

The only proven treatment for glaucoma is **lowering IOP**. Every 1 mmHg reduction in IOP reduces glaucoma progression risk by approximately 10% (OHTS, CNTGS trials). Target IOP is individualised based on severity and rate of progression — typically aim for 20–30% reduction from baseline.

**Prostaglandin analogues (first-line):**
- **Latanoprost 0.005%** (or bimatoprost, travoprost) once nightly
- Mechanism: Increases uveoscleral outflow → lowers IOP by 25–35% (most effective single agent)
- Side effects: Iris pigmentation (heterochromia in unilateral use — permanent), periocular skin pigmentation, eyelash lengthening/darkening (hypertrichosis), conjunctival hyperaemia, uveitis exacerbation
- Contraindicated in inflammatory glaucoma; caution in uveitis

**Beta-blockers (second-line or adjunct):**
- **Timolol 0.5%** BD or once daily (once-daily gel formulation — Timoptol-LA)
- Mechanism: Reduces aqueous production → lowers IOP by 20–25%
- Systemic absorption: bronchospasm, bradycardia, depression, reduced exercise tolerance — contraindicated in asthma, COPD, heart block, cardiac failure
- Morning drops minimise nocturnal bradycardia

**Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (CAIs):**
- Topical: **Dorzolamide 2%** (Trusopt) BD-TID; brinzolamide
- Systemic: **Acetazolamide** (oral/IV) — used for acute IOP elevation; significant side effects (paraesthesia, renal stones, metabolic acidosis, blood dyscrasias)
- Mechanism: Inhibits ciliary body carbonic anhydrase → reduces aqueous production

**Alpha-2 agonists:**
- **Brimonidine 0.2%** BD: Reduces aqueous production + increases uveoscleral outflow; second-line adjunct; CNS depression in infants — never use in children <5 years; ocular allergy in ~10%

**Combination drops (fixed-dose):**
- Timolol + dorzolamide (Cosopt); timolol + latanoprost (Xalacom) — improve adherence; cost-effective

### Laser Treatment

**Selective laser trabeculoplasty (SLT):**
- Laser applied to trabecular meshwork → reduces resistance, improves outflow → 20–30% IOP reduction
- LiGHT trial (2019): SLT as first-line treatment for POAG equally effective as eye drops at 36 months, with significantly lower treatment burden and cost; increasingly used first-line, particularly for patients with adherence issues
- Can be repeated; works in ~85% initially; effect may wear off after 3–5 years

**Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI):** For narrow angles / angle-closure risk (see The Red Eye article).

### Surgical Management

**Trabeculectomy:** Creates a partial thickness sclerotomy with a conjunctival bleb (guarded filtration surgery) → allows aqueous to pass directly from anterior chamber under the conjunctiva → absorb → IOP dramatically reduced. Gold standard glaucoma surgery. Mitomycin C (anti-metabolite) applied to prevent bleb scarring → improves long-term IOP control.

**Indications:** Failed maximal medical therapy; rapid progression; patient preference (avoid multiple drops); low-resource settings.

**Complications:** Hypotony (too-low IOP → choroidal effusion, maculopathy), bleb infection (blebitis → endophthalmitis — lifelong risk with functioning bleb → immediate IV antibiotics), cataract formation.

**Minimally invasive glaucoma surgery (MIGS):** iStent, Hydrus microstent, XEN gel stent — safer profile than trabeculectomy but less IOP reduction; suitable for mild-moderate glaucoma especially at time of cataract surgery.

## Complications

- **Progressive visual field loss → blindness:** Irreversible; once RGCs are dead, they cannot regenerate; goal of treatment is to prevent further loss
- **Treatment side effects:** Prostaglandin-induced iris/skin pigmentation (cosmetic); beta-blocker systemic effects (bronchospasm, bradycardia, exacerbation of heart failure)
- **Trabeculectomy bleb infection:** Late-onset blebitis/endophthalmitis — cumulative lifetime risk ~2% (thin blebs, inferior blebs, contact lens wear increase risk); requires emergency IV antibiotics + intravitreal antibiotics; may lose eye
- **Normal tension glaucoma and nocturnal hypotension:** Antihypertensive drugs causing nocturnal BP dips → insufficient optic nerve perfusion pressure → progression despite low IOP; review antihypertensive regimen with GP/cardiologist; avoid excessive evening antihypertensive doses

## Clinical Insight

The fundamental paradox of glaucoma is that the more treatable it is (early), the less it can be felt. Chronic open-angle glaucoma causes no pain, no redness, no visual disturbance — until it has already destroyed most of the peripheral visual field. This is why the optometrist is the most important person in glaucoma care — not the ophthalmologist. Routine optometric tests (IOP measurement, fundoscopy, visual fields) detect early glaucoma in asymptomatic patients. In the UK, this has reduced the proportion of patients presenting with advanced glaucoma substantially — but awareness among the public remains low.

The cup:disc ratio is the most misunderstood measurement in ophthalmic examination. A CDR of 0.8 can be normal in a patient with a large disc (physiological cupping) and severely abnormal in a patient with a small disc. The key is not the absolute ratio but the pattern of rim tissue loss (ISNT violation, notching, disc haemorrhage) and its correlation with visual field. A patient with a CDR of 0.8 and no visual field defect may need close monitoring; a patient with CDR 0.7 + inferior notching + arcuate scotoma has glaucoma. This is why OCT RNFL provides a more objective and reproducible measure of structural loss than disc photography alone.

The LiGHT trial (Laser in Glaucoma and Ocular Hypertension Trial) changed the treatment paradigm for newly diagnosed POAG. By demonstrating that SLT as first-line treatment was equally effective as drops at 3 years, with higher quality of life (no daily drop instillation, no systemic side effects) and lower cost, it established SLT as a first-line option — particularly attractive for patients who struggle with drop compliance. The clinical teaching is that "drops are for life" is no longer the only option — and a single SLT treatment can provide 3–5 years of IOP control with similar efficacy.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 67F, asymptomatic, detected by optometrist. IOP 26/24 mmHg bilateral. Arcuate scotoma right, superior arcuate defect left. CDR 0.8 right (inferior thinning) + 0.7 left (inferior notching). Open angles on gonioscopy.

**Diagnosis: Primary Open-Angle Glaucoma (POAG), bilateral — moderate right, early-moderate left**

**Step 1 — Confirm diagnosis and baseline:**
- Repeat IOP × 2 separate occasions (done); pachymetry (CCT — for IOP correction)
- OCT RNFL both eyes (establish structural baseline, compare to normative database)
- Humphrey visual fields (24-2) both eyes — confirm and document baseline
- Gonioscopy confirmed open angles → POAG (not angle-closure)

**Step 2 — Treatment initiation:**
- Discuss with patient: IOP needs lowering to slow/halt progression; first-line options
- **Option A (NICE/RCOphth guideline):** Prostaglandin analogue — **latanoprost 0.005%** once nightly to both eyes
- **Option B (LiGHT trial informed):** SLT first-line — one procedure, avoids daily drops, equally effective; discuss patient preference
- Target IOP: 20–30% reduction from baseline (target ~16–18 mmHg for this patient)

**Step 3 — Monitoring:**
- Review at 6 weeks: IOP response, tolerance
- Visual fields 6-monthly initially; OCT RNFL every 6–12 months
- Adjust treatment if target IOP not achieved or progression on VF/OCT

**Step 4 — Second-line (if latanoprost insufficient):**
- Add timolol 0.5% or fixed combination (Xalacom = latanoprost + timolol) — check for asthma/COPD/cardiac contraindications
- Alternatively: add brimonidine or dorzolamide; SLT if not already performed

**Step 5 — Surgical consideration:**
- If maximum tolerated medical therapy + SLT fails to control IOP or rapid progression → **trabeculectomy + MMC**

**Exam trigger:** *Raised IOP + arcuate visual field defect + disc cupping/notching + open angles* → POAG; first-line prostaglandin analogue (latanoprost) or SLT. The distractor is confusing with AACG — POAG is painless, chronic, and bilateral, with open angles; AACG is acute, painful, unilateral with closed angle and corneal oedema.
