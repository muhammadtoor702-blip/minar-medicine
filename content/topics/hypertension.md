## Overview

Hypertension is the most important modifiable risk factor for premature cardiovascular death, accounting for roughly half of all vascular deaths globally — around 8 million per year. It causes myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, aortic dissection, chronic kidney disease, and retinopathy. The condition is almost universally asymptomatic until end-organ damage has already occurred, which is why screening matters far more than symptom-driven detection.

The vast majority — around 95% — is **primary (essential) hypertension**, with no single identifiable cause. Secondary hypertension accounts for the remaining 5%, but this proportion rises sharply in younger patients, those with resistant hypertension, abrupt-onset severe hypertension, or those with associated electrolyte abnormalities.

**Defining hypertension** is arbitrary — BP has a skewed continuous distribution in the population, and cardiovascular risk rises continuously with BP, so any threshold is a clinical convenience. Current practice treats if clinic BP ≥140/90 mmHg, confirmed by ambulatory monitoring, particularly when other cardiovascular risk factors are present.

## Pathophysiology

Blood pressure = cardiac output × total peripheral resistance. Primary hypertension arises from a polygenic interplay between:

- **Increased sympathetic tone** — raises both heart rate and peripheral vasoconstriction
- **RAAS overactivation** — angiotensin II causes vasoconstriction and stimulates aldosterone-mediated sodium and water retention, increasing circulating volume
- **Endothelial dysfunction** — impaired nitric oxide production reduces vasodilation capacity
- **Vascular remodelling** — structural narrowing of resistance arterioles as a consequence of sustained pressure, which then perpetuates hypertension independently of the initiating cause

The consequences of sustained hypertension are progressive. **Hyaline arteriolosclerosis** — thickening and hyalinisation of small vessel walls — develops in target organs including the kidneys, retina, and brain. In **malignant (accelerated) hypertension**, fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles produces rapidly progressive organ damage. Left ventricular hypertrophy, the heart's adaptive response to chronic pressure overload, initially compensates but eventually leads to diastolic dysfunction, impaired coronary reserve, and increased arrhythmia risk.

**Secondary causes** to identify actively:

| Cause | Prevalence | Clinical Clue |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| Renal parenchymal disease | Most common secondary cause | Elevated creatinine, proteinuria, haematuria |
| Renovascular disease | ~1% of all HTN | Young woman (fibromuscular dysplasia) or elderly smoker (atherosclerosis). Flash pulmonary oedema. Worsening renal function on ACEi. Renal bruit. |
| Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's) | 5–10% of all HTN | Spontaneous hypokalaemia without diuretics. Elevated aldosterone:renin ratio. |
| Phaeochromocytoma | Rare | Episodic "5 Hs": headache, hypertension, hyperhidrosis, hyperglycaemia, hypermetabolism. Elevated plasma metanephrines. |
| Cushing's syndrome | Rare | Central obesity, purple striae, proximal myopathy, moon face, buffalo hump |
| Coarctation of aorta | Rare; young patients | Radio-femoral delay, rib notching on CXR, BP differential between upper and lower limbs |
| Obstructive sleep apnoea | Increasingly recognised | Resistant HTN, snoring, obesity, daytime somnolence |
| Drugs | Common | OCP, NSAIDs, steroids, cocaine, amphetamines, liquorice, MAOIs |

## Clinical Presentation

Hypertension is **almost universally asymptomatic** until end-organ damage has occurred. Headache is no more common in hypertensives than in the general population. Epistaxis and flushing are frequently attributed to hypertension but are not reliable indicators.

When symptoms do occur, they signal complications or extreme elevation:

**Malignant (accelerated) hypertension** — a rapid rise in BP (typically systolic >200, diastolic >120 mmHg) with vascular damage. The pathological hallmark is fibrinoid necrosis. Features include: severe headache, visual disturbance, bilateral retinal haemorrhages and exudates, papilloedema. It can precipitate hypertensive encephalopathy, acute kidney injury, acute heart failure, or aortic dissection. Untreated, 90% die within 1 year. Treated, 70% survive 5 years.

**Hypertensive urgency** — severely elevated BP (≥180/120 mmHg) without evidence of acute end-organ damage. Reduce gradually over 24–48 hours with oral therapy — rapid reduction risks cerebral and coronary ischaemia.

**Hypertensive emergency** — severely elevated BP with acute end-organ damage: hypertensive encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage, acute LV failure, ACS, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury with haematuria, eclampsia. Requires IV treatment with a controlled reduction — no more than 25% fall in MAP in the first hour, then to 160/100 mmHg over 2–6 hours. Exception: aortic dissection requires urgent target SBP 100–120 mmHg.

## Diagnosis

**Never diagnose on a single reading.** White coat hypertension can elevate readings by 20 mmHg or more. Confirm with:
- **24-hour ambulatory BP monitoring (ABPM)** — preferred; day average ≥135/85 is diagnostic
- **Home BP monitoring (HBPM)** — at least 7 days, twice daily, discard day 1

**NICE Staging:**

| Stage | Clinic BP | ABPM/HBPM |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| Stage 1 | ≥140/90 | ≥135/85 |
| Stage 2 | ≥160/100 | ≥150/95 |
| Severe | ≥180/120 | — (treat on same day; assess for end-organ damage) |

**White coat hypertension** — elevated clinic BP but normal ABPM (day average <130/80). Still associated with increased CVD risk and all-cause mortality. Institute lifestyle changes and monitor annually for sustained hypertension.

**Masked hypertension** — normal clinic BP but elevated ABPM (≥135/85 daytime). Often missed. Suspect in patients with multiple risk factors whose clinic readings are consistently 130–139/85–89.

**Investigations — purpose-driven:**

| Purpose | Tests |
|---------|-------|
| Confirm diagnosis | ABPM or HBPM |
| Quantify overall risk | Fasting glucose, fasting cholesterol, eGFR |
| Identify end-organ damage | ECG (LVH — Sokolow-Lyon: S in V1 + R in V5/V6 ≥35mm); urine ACR (proteinuria); fundoscopy |
| Screen for secondary causes | U&E (K⁺ low → hyperaldosteronism?), creatinine, plasma metanephrines (phaeochromocytoma), renin:aldosterone ratio, 24-hour urinary free cortisol (Cushing's) |
| Special imaging | CT/MR angiography (renal artery stenosis), MR aorta (coarctation) |

**Grading hypertensive retinopathy (Keith-Wagener-Barker):**
- Grade 1: Silver or copper wiring (thickened arterial walls)
- Grade 2: Arteriovenous nipping (narrowing where arteries cross veins)
- Grade 3: Flame haemorrhages and cotton-wool spots → requires urgent treatment
- Grade 4: Papilloedema → hypertensive crisis; admit urgently

**Who to treat pharmacologically (NICE NG136):**
- Stage 1 (clinic ≥140/90, ABPM ≥135/85): drug treatment **only if** end-organ damage, established CVD, renal disease, diabetes, or 10-year QRISK3 ≥10%. Otherwise: lifestyle + annual review.
- Stage 2 (clinic ≥160/100, ABPM ≥150/95): drug treatment regardless of other risk factors.
- Severe (clinic ≥180/120): treat same day; exclude hypertensive emergency.

## Management

### Lifestyle Modification

Mandatory for all patients, regardless of whether medication is started:
- Salt restriction to <6g per day (reduces SBP by ~5 mmHg)
- DASH diet (high in fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy, low saturated fat)
- Weight loss — each 10 kg lost reduces BP by 5–10 mmHg
- Regular aerobic exercise ≥150 min/week (reduces SBP by ~4–5 mmHg)
- Alcohol reduction to <14 units/week
- Smoking cessation — essential for cardiovascular risk (does not directly lower BP)

### Pharmacotherapy — NICE Stepwise Approach

The ALLHAT trial confirmed that adequate BP reduction matters more than the specific drug used — though ethnicity modifies first-line choices.

| Step | First Choice | Rationale |
|------|-------------|----------|
| Step 1 | **ACEi or ARB** if age <55 and non-Black; **CCB** if age ≥55 or Black African/Caribbean | RAAS blockade more effective in younger patients; CCB/diuretics more effective in low-renin states |
| Step 2 | ACEi/ARB **+** CCB | Complementary mechanisms |
| Step 3 | ACEi/ARB **+** CCB **+** thiazide-like diuretic (indapamide or chlorthalidone preferred) | Triple blockade of RAAS, vasodilation, and volume |
| Step 4 (resistant) | K⁺ <4.5 mEq/L: add **spironolactone 25–50mg** (most effective 4th agent). K⁺ ≥4.5 mEq/L: add alpha-blocker (doxazosin) or beta-blocker. Seek specialist help. | Resistant HTN often driven by aldosterone excess even without Conn's syndrome |

**Beta-blockers** — not first-line for hypertension per NICE (less effective at preventing stroke than other agents), but appropriate if coexisting IHD, AF, or HFrEF.

**BP targets:**
- Most patients: <140/90 mmHg (clinic)
- Age <65, diabetes, CKD, established CVD: consider <130/80 mmHg
- Age ≥80: <150/90 mmHg — avoid over-treatment in frail elderly

### Drug Doses and Key Notes

**Thiazide-like diuretics:** Chlorthalidone 25–50mg/24h morning; indapamide 1.5mg/24h. Preferred over bendroflumethiazide (stronger evidence). Monitor U&E at 2 weeks and 3 months. SE: hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, hyperuricaemia (→ gout), impotence. CI: gout.

**CCBs:** Amlodipine 5–10mg OD. SE: ankle oedema (does not respond to diuretics — vasodilatory, not fluid overload), flushing, gingival hypertrophy. Do not use short-acting formulations. Diltiazem and verapamil slow AV conduction — never combine with beta-blockers (risk of complete heart block and LVF).

**ACE inhibitors:** Lisinopril 10–40mg/24h PO (max 40mg). Also first-choice in diabetes with microalbuminuria or proteinuria; CKD with proteinuria. SE: dry cough (10–15%, due to bradykinin accumulation) — switch to ARB. Angioedema (rare, but potentially fatal). Teratogenic — absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy. Monitor U&E 1–2 weeks after starting and after each dose increase. A creatinine rise of up to 30% is acceptable; >30% suggests renal artery stenosis.

**ARBs:** Candesartan 8–32mg/24h. Use when ACEi cough is intolerable. Do not combine ACEi + ARB — dual RAAS blockade doubles the risk of hyperkalaemia and AKI without additional antihypertensive benefit (ONTARGET trial).

**Spironolactone (Step 4):** Low-dose (25–50mg) is highly effective for resistant hypertension regardless of aldosterone status. Monitor U&E closely; avoid if eGFR <30 or K⁺ ≥5.0 mEq/L. SE: gynaecomastia (switch to eplerenone if troublesome).

**Special circumstances:**
- Pregnancy: labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa. ACEi and ARBs are absolutely contraindicated.
- CKD with proteinuria: ACEi or ARB regardless of ethnicity — independent renoprotective effect
- Phaeochromocytoma crisis: alpha-blocker (phenoxybenzamine or phentolamine) **first**, then add beta-blocker. Never give a beta-blocker alone — unopposed alpha stimulation causes paradoxical severe hypertension.

### Monitoring on Treatment

- Recheck BP at 1–2 months after starting or changing medication
- U&E and creatinine 1–2 weeks after starting ACEi/ARB/spironolactone, and after every dose increase
- K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L on ACEi/ARB + spironolactone: reduce or stop spironolactone immediately
- Once stable: annual review (6-monthly if high cardiovascular risk)
- Urinary drug screening if compliance suspected as reason for inadequate control

## Complications

- **Stroke** — ischaemic (small vessel lacunar) and haemorrhagic; leading complication of uncontrolled HTN
- **Myocardial infarction and coronary artery disease** — accelerated atherosclerosis
- **Heart failure** — hypertensive heart disease via LVH → diastolic dysfunction → systolic failure
- **Aortic dissection and aneurysm** — hypertension is present in >70% of dissections
- **CKD progressing to ESRD** — hypertensive nephrosclerosis; also accelerates diabetic nephropathy
- **Retinopathy and visual loss** — grade 3–4 retinopathy; branch or central retinal artery occlusion
- **Hypertensive encephalopathy** — from loss of cerebral autoregulation at extreme pressures

## Clinical Insight

The most common reason BP remains uncontrolled is under-treatment — failure to advance to step 2 or 3, or use of inferior agents (bendroflumethiazide instead of chlorthalidone/indapamide). When BP appears resistant, check adherence before adding a fourth agent — urinary drug screening is an underused and valuable tool.

Spontaneous hypokalaemia in a hypertensive patient not taking diuretics should always prompt measurement of the aldosterone:renin ratio to exclude primary hyperaldosteronism. This condition is far more prevalent than historically thought — affecting perhaps 5–10% of all hypertensives — and is both curable (unilateral adenoma → adrenalectomy) and important to identify.

Malignant hypertension requires urgent treatment but demands a controlled reduction. The temptation to bring BP down rapidly is dangerous — autoregulation of cerebral blood flow is set at a higher level in chronic hypertensives, so a rapid fall to what would be a "normal" BP can cause watershed ischaemia and stroke. Reduce MAP by no more than 25% in the first hour, then reassess.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 52-year-old man, asymptomatic. Clinic BP 168/102 on two readings 10 minutes apart. Smoker (15/day), family history of stroke, BMI 31. Normal urinalysis. Fundoscopy: silver wiring and arteriovenous nipping, no haemorrhages or papilloedema.

**Step 1 — Classify the hypertension stage:**
- BP 168/102 = **Stage 2 hypertension** (≥160/100 mmHg in UK NICE classification)
- Fundoscopic changes: silver wiring + AV nipping = **Grade 2 hypertensive retinopathy** — this constitutes end-organ damage

**Step 2 — Confirm before treating — but end-organ damage changes this:** NICE recommends ABPM confirmation before starting antihypertensives in most cases. However, **Grade 2 retinopathy = established end-organ damage** → treatment is warranted without waiting for ABPM. Arrange ABPM to establish baseline but start treatment now.

**Step 3 — Cardiovascular risk stratification:** Smoker + family history (stroke) + BMI 31 + Stage 2 HTN = **very high 10-year CV risk**. Use QRISK3 formally. This risk score also determines whether statin therapy is indicated (≥10% threshold = yes).

**Step 4 — Drug selection (NICE Step 1, age <55, non-black):**
- **ACE inhibitor** (ramipril 5–10 mg OD) or **ARB** as first choice
- Given Stage 2 BP with CV risk factors: consider starting Step 1 + Step 2 simultaneously — ACEi + **amlodipine** (CCB)
- Do not use bendroflumethiazide — inferior to chlorthalidone/indapamide for cardiovascular outcomes

**Step 5 — Other interventions:**
- **Statin** — atorvastatin 20 mg (high CV risk)
- **Smoking cessation** — most impactful single intervention for reducing cardiovascular events
- Weight loss, dietary sodium reduction, alcohol moderation, regular aerobic exercise
- BP target: **<130/80 mmHg** in high-risk individuals

**Step 6 — Monitor:** U&E + creatinine 2 weeks after starting ACEi. Recheck BP at 4–8 weeks; if not at target, advance to Step 2/3.

**Exam trigger:** *Hypertension + fundoscopic changes (AV nipping, silver wiring, haemorrhages)* → know the grading. Grade 1–2 = treated medically; Grade 3–4 (haemorrhages/papilloedema) = **hypertensive emergency** requiring hospital admission and IV antihypertensives.
