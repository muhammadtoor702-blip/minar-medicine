# Hypertension

## Clinical Scenario

A 52-year-old man attends for a routine health review with no complaints. His clinic BP is 168/102 mmHg on two readings taken 10 minutes apart. He smokes 15 cigarettes a day, has a first-degree family history of stroke, and has a BMI of 31. Urinalysis is normal. Fundoscopy shows mild arteriolar narrowing with no haemorrhages or exudates.

## Overview

Hypertension is a sustained elevation of blood pressure that represents one of the most important modifiable risk factors for myocardial infarction, stroke, heart failure, CKD, and peripheral arterial disease. The vast majority — around 90–95% — is primary (essential) hypertension, with no single identifiable cause. Secondary hypertension accounts for the remainder and should always be considered in younger patients, those with resistant hypertension, or atypical features.

## Pathophysiology

Blood pressure is the product of cardiac output and total peripheral resistance. Primary hypertension results from a complex, polygenic interplay between increased sympathetic tone, RAAS overactivation with sodium retention, endothelial dysfunction impairing nitric oxide-mediated vasodilation, and structural vascular remodelling.

The consequences of sustained elevation are progressive. Hyaline arteriolosclerosis — thickening and hyalinisation of small vessel walls — causes ischaemia in target organs. In malignant hypertension, fibrinoid necrosis of arterioles produces rapidly progressive damage to the kidneys, retina, and brain. Left ventricular hypertrophy, the heart's adaptation to chronic pressure overload, eventually leads to diastolic dysfunction and increased arrhythmia risk.

**Secondary causes** to consider actively:

| Cause | Clinical Clue |
|-------|--------------|
| Renal parenchymal disease | Most common secondary cause. Elevated creatinine, proteinuria. |
| Renovascular disease | Young woman (FMD) or elderly smoker (atherosclerosis). Flash pulmonary oedema. Worsening renal function on ACEi. |
| Primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's) | HTN + spontaneous hypokalaemia. Elevated aldosterone:renin ratio. |
| Phaeochromocytoma | Episodic HTN with headache, sweating, palpitations. Elevated urinary metanephrines. |
| Cushing's syndrome | Central obesity, striae, proximal myopathy, moon face. |
| Coarctation of aorta | Young patient, radio-femoral delay, rib notching on CXR, BP differential between arms. |
| Obstructive sleep apnoea | Resistant HTN, snoring, obesity, daytime somnolence. |

## Clinical Presentation

Hypertension is almost universally asymptomatic until end-organ damage occurs. When it does cause symptoms, it is usually through its complications: headache and visual disturbance in hypertensive emergency, chest pain from hypertensive heart disease or aortic dissection, or focal neurological deficits from stroke.

**Hypertensive urgency vs emergency:**

- **Urgency** — severely elevated BP (≥180/120 mmHg) without evidence of acute end-organ damage. Gradual oral treatment over 24–48 hours.
- **Emergency** — severely elevated BP with acute end-organ damage (hypertensive encephalopathy, ICH, acute LV failure, ACS, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury with haematuria, or grade 3–4 retinopathy). Requires IV treatment with a controlled, gradual reduction — typically no more than 25% in the first hour.

**Hypertensive emergency targets:**
- Aortic dissection — target HR <60 and systolic 100–120 mmHg. IV labetalol or esmolol first.
- All others — reduce MAP by no more than 25% in the first hour, then to 160/100 over the next 2–6 hours.

## Diagnosis

**Never diagnose hypertension on a single clinic reading.** White coat hypertension can elevate readings by 20 mmHg or more. Confirm with ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) or home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) in all patients.

**NICE Staging:**
- Stage 1: Clinic ≥140/90 confirmed by ABPM/HBPM ≥135/85
- Stage 2: Clinic ≥160/100 confirmed by ABPM/HBPM ≥150/95
- Severe: Clinic systolic ≥180 or diastolic ≥120

**Assessment of end-organ damage:**
- ECG — LVH, ischaemic changes
- U&E — renal function, electrolytes (hypokalaemia suggests Conn's)
- Urine ACR — proteinuria as marker of early nephropathy
- Fundoscopy — Keith-Wagener-Barker grade 3–4 indicates emergency

## Management

### Lifestyle Modification

Lifestyle changes are mandatory for all patients regardless of whether medication is started:
- Salt restriction to <6g per day
- DASH diet (high in fruits, vegetables, low-fat dairy)
- Weight loss — each 10kg lost reduces BP by 5–10 mmHg
- Regular aerobic exercise ≥150 min/week
- Alcohol reduction to <14 units/week
- Smoking cessation (essential for cardiovascular risk, though does not directly lower BP)

### Pharmacotherapy — NICE Stepwise Approach

| Step | Drug Choice |
|------|------------|
| Step 1 | ACEi (or ARB) if age <55 or diabetic. CCB if age ≥55 or Black African/Caribbean. |
| Step 2 | ACEi/ARB + CCB |
| Step 3 | ACEi/ARB + CCB + thiazide-like diuretic (indapamide or chlorthalidone preferred) |
| Step 4 (Resistant) | K+ <4.5: add spironolactone 25mg. K+ ≥4.5: add alpha-blocker (doxazosin) or beta-blocker. |

**BP targets:**
- Most patients: <140/90 (clinic), <135/85 (ABPM)
- Diabetes, CKD, established CVD: <130/80
- Age ≥80: <150/90 (avoid over-treatment in frail elderly)

**Special situations:**
- Pregnancy: labetalol, nifedipine, or methyldopa. ACEi and ARBs are absolutely contraindicated — they are teratogenic.
- CKD with proteinuria: ACEi or ARB first-line regardless of ethnicity
- Phaeochromocytoma crisis: give alpha-blocker (phentolamine) first. Never give a beta-blocker alone — unopposed alpha-stimulation causes paradoxical hypertension.

## Complications

- Stroke — ischaemic and haemorrhagic
- Myocardial infarction and heart failure
- CKD progressing to ESRD
- Aortic dissection and aneurysm
- Retinopathy and visual loss
- Hypertensive encephalopathy

## Clinical Insight

The most common reason BP remains uncontrolled is under-treatment — not starting step 2 or 3 therapy, or using bendroflumethiazide instead of the preferred thiazide-like diuretics (indapamide, chlorthalidone). When BP is resistant, check adherence before adding another agent.

ACEi and ARB should never be combined. The dual blockade of RAAS doubles the risk of hyperkalaemia and AKI without any additional antihypertensive benefit (ONTARGET trial).

Spontaneous hypokalaemia in a hypertensive patient — particularly one who is not on diuretics — should always prompt measurement of the aldosterone:renin ratio to exclude primary hyperaldosteronism, which is far more common than historically appreciated (affecting perhaps 5–10% of all hypertensives).
