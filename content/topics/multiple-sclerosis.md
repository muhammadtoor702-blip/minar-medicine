## Overview

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated, demyelinating disease of the central nervous system, characterised by inflammatory plaques of demyelination and axonal loss that are disseminated in space (different CNS regions) and time (episodes separated by at least 30 days). It is the most common cause of neurological disability in young adults in temperate climates, with a 2:1 female predominance and peak onset in the third to fourth decades. The clinical course is highly variable.

## Pathophysiology

MS involves a complex interplay of genetic susceptibility (HLA-DRB1*15:01 is the strongest genetic risk factor) and environmental triggers. Autoreactive T lymphocytes (predominantly CD4+ Th1 and Th17 cells) breach the blood-brain barrier and attack myelin — the lipid sheath insulating axons — and oligodendrocytes. CD8+ T cells and B cells also contribute to the inflammatory cascade.

**Demyelination** slows and blocks axonal conduction, producing the symptoms of an acute relapse. Initial remyelination can occur, explaining the recovery seen after relapses in early disease. Over time, however, remyelination becomes incomplete, and **axonal transection and neuronal loss** — the substrate of progressive disability — accumulate. Progressive disability is largely driven by this neurodegeneration rather than ongoing inflammation.

**Uhthoff's phenomenon**: Temporary worsening of MS symptoms with body temperature elevation (fever, hot bath, exercise). Myelin-depleted axons conduct more poorly at higher temperatures. Symptoms resolve when temperature normalises — critical distinguishing feature from a true relapse.

**Lhermitte's sign**: An electric shock or tingling sensation radiating down the spine and into the limbs on neck flexion, caused by demyelination in the dorsal columns of the cervical cord. Not specific to MS (also occurs in subacute combined degeneration of the cord, cervical myelopathy) but highly suggestive in the appropriate context.

## Clinical Presentation

MS produces a remarkably varied clinical picture depending on which CNS regions are affected. Common presentations:

**Optic neuritis**: Subacute, typically unilateral visual loss over days, with pain on eye movement (from traction on the inflamed optic nerve sheath). Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD/Marcus Gunn pupil) — the swinging light test reveals sluggish response in the affected eye. Colour desaturation (especially red). Full recovery in ~85% within 3 months, though subtle deficits may persist. In 50% of patients, optic neuritis is the first presentation of MS.

**Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO)**: Lesion in the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF) in the brainstem. Impaired adduction of the ipsilateral eye during horizontal gaze, with nystagmus in the contralateral abducting eye. Bilateral INO in a young adult is virtually pathognomonic of MS.

**Spinal cord syndromes**: Paraparesis, sensory level, bladder dysfunction (urgency, retention, incontinence), sexual dysfunction, Lhermitte's sign. Transverse myelitis (complete or partial spinal cord inflammation).

**Cerebellar/brainstem**: Ataxia, intention tremor, dysarthria (scanning speech), diplopia, vertigo, facial numbness.

**Cognitive symptoms**: Slowed processing speed, attention and memory difficulties — often underestimated and underreported.

**Fatigue**: The most common symptom overall. Often described as overwhelming, disproportionate to exertion. Profoundly impacts quality of life.

**MS disease courses:**

| Type | Description | Frequency |
|------|------------|-----------|
| Relapsing-remitting (RRMS) | Distinct relapses with full or partial recovery; no progression between relapses | ~85% at onset |
| Secondary progressive (SPMS) | Begins as RRMS; gradual progression begins, with or without relapses | ~50% of RRMS after 10–15 years |
| Primary progressive (PPMS) | Gradual progressive disability from onset; no relapses | ~15% at onset; older, male:female ~1:1 |

## Diagnosis

The **McDonald Criteria 2017** require demonstration of **dissemination in space (DIS)** and **dissemination in time (DIT)** — either clinically or on MRI — for a diagnosis of MS.

**DIS on MRI**: Lesions in ≥2 of 4 characteristic locations:
- Periventricular (immediately adjacent to lateral ventricles — "Dawson's fingers")
- Juxtacortical/cortical
- Infratentorial (brainstem, cerebellum)
- Spinal cord

**DIT on MRI**: Simultaneous presence of gadolinium-enhancing (active) and non-enhancing (old) lesions at any time, or new T2 lesion on follow-up scan.

A clinically isolated syndrome (CIS) — a single clinical episode suggestive of MS — can be diagnosed as MS at first presentation if DIS and DIT are met on MRI.

**MRI brain and spine with gadolinium**: The cornerstone investigation. T2/FLAIR hyperintense white matter lesions; gadolinium-enhancing lesions indicate active inflammation (BBB disruption).

**CSF analysis**: Not required for diagnosis if MRI criteria are fulfilled, but supportive:
- **Oligoclonal bands** (OCBs) — IgG bands present in CSF but not serum; found in >95% of MS patients; indicate intrathecal immunoglobulin synthesis. Not specific to MS (also in encephalitis, neurosarcoidosis).
- Mild lymphocytosis (<50 cells)
- Normal glucose

**Visual evoked potentials (VEPs)**: Delayed P100 latency indicates optic nerve demyelination, even when clinically silent — demonstrates dissemination in space.

**Differential diagnosis of CNS white matter lesions:** NMOSD (neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder — anti-AQP4 or anti-MOG antibodies, longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions), ADEM (acute disseminated encephalomyelitis — monophasic, post-infectious, children), CNS vasculitis, Susac syndrome, migraine-related white matter changes, CADASIL, B12 deficiency, HIV encephalopathy.
## Management

### Acute Relapse

**IV methylprednisolone 1 g/day for 3–5 days** — accelerates recovery from relapses but does not alter the final outcome or long-term prognosis. Indicated for disabling relapses. Outpatient administration is feasible.

### Disease-Modifying Therapies (DMTs)

DMTs reduce relapse rate and slow MRI lesion accumulation. They do not reverse established disability. The principle is early, effective treatment — waiting until disability accumulates represents a lost therapeutic window.

**Efficacy-stratified approach (NICE NG220):**

| Tier | Drugs | Reduction in ARR | Indication |
|------|-------|-----------------|-----------|
| Moderate efficacy | Beta-interferons (IFN-β-1a, IFN-β-1b), glatiramer acetate, dimethyl fumarate, teriflunomide | 30–50% | Mild-moderate active RRMS |
| High efficacy | Natalizumab (anti-VLA-4), ocrelizumab (anti-CD20), alemtuzumab, cladribine | 60–70% | Active RRMS or rapid escalation |
| Progressive MS | Ocrelizumab (primary progressive MS — ORATORIO trial), siponimod (secondary progressive with relapses) | Variable | PPMS; active SPMS |

**Natalizumab** carries risk of PML (progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy) from JC virus reactivation — risk stratification with JC antibody titres and MRI surveillance is mandatory.

**Alemtuzumab** — highly effective but carries serious risks: secondary autoimmunity (thyroid disease, ITP, anti-GBM disease) in ~30% requiring 4-year monitoring after the last infusion.

**Ocrelizumab** — anti-CD20, depletes B cells. Only DMT proven to slow progression in PPMS.

### Symptomatic Management

MS symptoms require active, targeted management:

| Symptom | Management |
|---------|-----------|
| Fatigue | Amantadine, modafinil, energy conservation strategies, graded exercise |
| Spasticity | Physiotherapy, baclofen, tizanidine, cannabis-based Sativex |
| Bladder dysfunction | Anticholinergics (overactive bladder), ISC (retention), botulinum toxin |
| Neuropathic pain | Gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline |
| Depression | SSRIs, psychotherapy |
| Cognitive | Cognitive rehabilitation, fatigue management |
| Tremor | Beta-blockers, clonazepam, DBS in severe cases |

## Complications

- Progressive disability — secondary progressive phase is the major long-term concern
- Relapse-induced disability that does not fully recover (incomplete remission)
- Bladder complications — urinary tract infections, renal damage from chronic retention
- Aspiration pneumonia in advanced disease
- Depression — affects ~50%; suicide risk 7× higher than general population
- Osteoporosis — from corticosteroid use and immobility

## Clinical Insight

The diagnosis of MS requires dissemination in space and time. A single clinical episode, however typical, does not meet criteria for MS. When a patient presents with their first episode (CIS), the MRI may already demonstrate DIS and DIT — diagnosing MS at first presentation. This distinction matters because it determines when to initiate disease-modifying therapy.

Uhthoff's phenomenon is not a relapse. When a patient reports temporary worsening of symptoms with a hot shower, exercise, or fever, they do not need IV steroids — they need cooling strategies and reassurance. Treating pseudo-relapses with steroids exposes patients unnecessarily to side effects. The key question is whether symptoms are genuinely new or represent worsening of pre-existing deficits.

Bladder symptoms in MS are both common and clinically important — yet often go unreported by patients out of embarrassment. Untreated detrusor overactivity leads to infections; untreated urinary retention leads to renal damage. Ask about bladder function at every review. Urodynamic assessment guides management. This is not a minor quality-of-life issue — it is a major determinant of independence and renal health.
