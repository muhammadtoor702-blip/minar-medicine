## Overview

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections in clinical practice, accounting for approximately 15% of all community-prescribed antibiotics. They range from uncomplicated lower UTI (cystitis) — a self-limiting, low-risk condition in healthy women — to complicated upper UTI (pyelonephritis) and urosepsis, which carry significant morbidity and mortality. This patient has recurrent uncomplicated cystitis; her three episodes in one year qualify as recurrent UTI and warrant a structured prevention strategy — not just another course of antibiotics. Repeated empirical trimethoprim courses without culture guidance risk selecting resistant organisms.

---

## Pathophysiology

Most UTIs result from faecal flora ascending from the perineum through the urethra into the bladder — the short female urethra (4 cm vs 20 cm in males) explains the 30:1 female predominance. Haematogenous seeding is rare and occurs primarily with *S. aureus* bacteraemia and *Candida* fungaemia.

**Causative organisms:**

| Organism | Frequency | Key Features |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| *Escherichia coli* | ~80% of community UTIs | Type 1 and P fimbriae for uroepithelial adhesion; most common cause of all clinical UTI syndromes |
| *Klebsiella pneumoniae* | ~5–10% | Common in diabetics and hospital-acquired; ESBL strains increasingly prevalent |
| *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* | ~5–10% | **Second most common in young sexually active women**; coagulase-negative; produces urease |
| *Proteus mirabilis* | ~5% | Urease-producer → ammonia → alkaline urine → struvite stone formation; characteristic fishy smell |
| *Enterococcus faecalis* | ~5% | Hospital-acquired, catheter-associated; intrinsically resistant to many antibiotics |
| *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* | <5% | Hospital-acquired, structural abnormality, immunocompromised; multidrug resistance |

**Virulence factors of uropathogenic *E. coli*:** P fimbriae bind to P-blood group antigens on urothelium (explains susceptibility clustering in certain women); type 1 fimbriae bind to mannose residues (basis of D-mannose prophylaxis); alpha-haemolysin damages urothelial cells; aerobactin scavenges iron in urine.

**Risk factors for UTI:**
- Female anatomy (short urethra, proximity to anus)
- Sexual activity ("honeymoon cystitis" — E. coli inoculation at intercourse)
- Urinary stasis — structural abnormality, obstruction, vesicoureteric reflux, neurogenic bladder, post-void residual
- Urinary catheter — **most common cause of hospital-acquired UTI** (3–7% risk per catheter-day; biofilm formation prevents antibiotic penetration)
- Diabetes mellitus — glucosuria provides bacterial growth medium; impaired neutrophil function
- Pregnancy — progesterone relaxes ureteral smooth muscle → stasis; physiological hydroureter
- Immunosuppression, CKD
- Post-menopausal status — oestrogen deficiency reduces Lactobacillus colonisation, raising vaginal pH and uropathogen colonisation

**Complicated vs uncomplicated UTI:**

| Uncomplicated | Complicated |
|--------------|------------|
| Healthy non-pregnant woman | Pregnancy |
| No structural/functional urological abnormality | Structural abnormality (obstruction, calculi, stent, ileal conduit) |
| Normal renal function | Neurogenic bladder, urinary retention |
| Community-acquired | Male sex (anatomically unlikely UTI — always investigate) |
| — | Diabetes, immunosuppression |
| — | Hospital-acquired or catheter-associated |
| — | AKI or CKD (eGFR <30) |

## Clinical Presentation

**Lower UTI (cystitis):** Dysuria, urinary frequency, urgency, suprapubic discomfort, haematuria (macro or micro), cloudy or malodorous urine. **Absent fever and absent flank pain** — their presence indicates upper tract involvement.

**Upper UTI (pyelonephritis):** All of the above plus **fever (>38°C), rigors, loin/flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, nausea, and vomiting**. Systemic features indicate renal parenchymal infection. Up to 30% of women with apparent cystitis symptoms have subclinical pyelonephritis.

**Urosepsis:** Pyelonephritis progressing to systemic infection meeting sepsis criteria — tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, confusion. Gram-negative bacteraemia with endotoxin release. Can progress to septic shock and multi-organ failure.

**Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB):** Significant bacteriuria (≥10⁵ CFU/mL) without symptoms. **Do not treat except in pregnancy** (ascending pyelonephritis risk, preterm labour) **and before urological procedures** (prevents post-procedure bacteraemia). Treating ASB in elderly patients, diabetics, or catheterised patients does not reduce symptomatic UTI rates but does increase antibiotic resistance and *Clostridioides difficile* infection. Bacteriuria in the absence of symptoms is colonisation, not infection.

**UTI in elderly patients:** Presents atypically — confusion, falls, reduced mobility, functional decline — without classic urinary symptoms. However, bacteriuria is extremely common in elderly patients without infection (~25–50% of women in care homes have bacteriuria). Always confirm **new or worsened urinary symptoms** are present before attributing confusion to UTI and treating — this prevents inappropriate antibiotic prescribing, the dominant driver of C. difficile and resistance in elderly care.

**Recurrent UTI:** ≥2 episodes in 6 months or ≥3 in 12 months. Requires microbiological culture, investigation for precipitating factors, and a structured prevention plan.

**Sterile pyuria** — leucocytes in urine with no growth on standard culture. Do not dismiss; do not treat empirically. Key causes:

| Cause | Investigation |
|-------|--------------|
| **Urinary TB** (most important) | Early morning urine × 3 for AFB and TB culture |
| Partially treated UTI | History of recent antibiotics |
| Chlamydial urethritis | NAAT swab/urine for *C. trachomatis* |
| Renal calculus | CT KUB |
| Interstitial nephritis | Drug history; renal biopsy if suspected |
| Bladder cancer | Urine cytology; cystoscopy |

## Diagnosis

**Urine dipstick:** Leucocyte esterase (WBCs) + nitrites together have ~80% positive predictive value for UTI in symptomatic patients. Negative dipstick in a low-probability patient largely excludes UTI. Nitrites are produced by gram-negative organisms (Enterobacteriaceae) that reduce dietary nitrates — *Enterococcus* and *Staphylococcus saprophyticus* are nitrite-negative despite causing UTI.

**Urine MC&S (mid-stream clean-catch urine):** Required before antibiotics in: pyelonephritis, complicated UTI, pregnant women, men (always), recurrent UTI, and treatment failure. Significant bacteriuria = ≥10⁵ CFU/mL of a single organism. Lower thresholds apply in symptomatic patients (≥10² CFU/mL in catheter samples).

**Bloods (pyelonephritis/urosepsis):** FBC, U&E, CRP, blood cultures (before antibiotics), lactate. Raised inflammatory markers support pyelonephritis; renal function may be impaired.

**Imaging:**
- **Renal ultrasound**: if pyelonephritis not responding at 48–72 hours (exclude abscess or obstruction), all men with a first UTI, and all children after a febrile UTI (assess for VUR/renal scarring)
- **CT urography**: recurrent UTI in men, suspected structural abnormality, stones, persistent haematuria
- **DMSA scan**: gold standard for renal scarring in children after UTI

## Management

### Uncomplicated Lower UTI (Women)

Choose based on local antibiogram — trimethoprim resistance exceeds 20% in many areas:
- **Nitrofurantoin MR 100 mg twice daily × 3 days** — preferred first-line (NICE NG109); lower resistance rates than trimethoprim; concentrates in urine and bladder but NOT in tissue (do not use for pyelonephritis). Avoid if eGFR <45 mL/min — reduced efficacy and risk of pulmonary toxicity with long-term use.
- **Trimethoprim 200 mg twice daily × 7 days** — only if local resistance rates <20% and no history of prior resistance; avoid in first trimester of pregnancy (folate antagonism)
- **Pivmecillinam 400 mg three times daily × 3 days** — good evidence, minimal resistance, well tolerated; increasingly used as first-line in some centres

Urine culture is not required for uncomplicated first-presentation lower UTI in women — treat empirically. Obtain culture if symptoms persist at 48 hours or recur within 2 weeks.

### Pyelonephritis

Send urine and blood cultures before antibiotics. Admit if unable to tolerate oral medication, haemodynamically unstable, severe comorbidity, or failure on oral therapy at 24 hours.

**Outpatient (mild-moderate, tolerating orally):**
- Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily × 7 days — first-line for good tissue penetration; avoid if fluoroquinolone-resistant E. coli is prevalent locally
- Co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times daily × 14 days — culture-guided

**Inpatient (severe, unable to tolerate oral):**
- IV co-amoxiclav or IV cephalosporin (cefuroxime, ceftriaxone)
- Gentamicin IV for Gram-negative cover in urosepsis — single daily dosing; monitor levels
- Step down to targeted oral therapy when CRP falling, patient afebrile and tolerating oral fluids
- Total duration: 10–14 days

**Imaging:** Ultrasound at 48–72 hours if not improving — exclude obstruction or abscess. CT abdomen-pelvis with contrast if abscess suspected (gas-forming organisms in diabetes = emphysematous pyelonephritis — surgical emergency).

### Catheter-Associated UTI

Only treat if the patient has **symptoms** — fever, new flank pain, suprapubic pain, rigors. Pyuria alone in a catheterised patient is not an indication for antibiotics. **Remove or change the catheter** — this alone often resolves bacteriuria. Culture, then treat guided by sensitivity for 5–7 days.

### Recurrent UTI in Women

**Non-antibiotic prevention (first-line):**
- High fluid intake >1.5–2 L/day
- Post-coital voiding
- **Topical vaginal oestrogen** — most effective prevention in post-menopausal women; restores Lactobacillus colonisation, lowers vaginal pH, reduces uropathogen colonisation; preferred over systemic HRT for urogenital symptoms
- Cranberry products (reduces E. coli fimbriae adhesion to urothelium — modest but consistent evidence)
- D-mannose (competitive inhibitor of type 1 fimbriae binding — emerging evidence)

**Antibiotic prevention:**
- **Post-coital prophylaxis** (if UTIs consistently follow intercourse): single dose nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg or trimethoprim 100 mg
- **Continuous low-dose prophylaxis**: nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg at night or trimethoprim 100 mg at night — reduces recurrence rate by ~80%
- **Self-start (patient-initiated) therapy**: for motivated, well-informed patients who reliably recognise their symptoms — start antibiotics themselves when symptoms develop, avoiding delay

## Complications

- **Pyelonephritis → renal abscess** (fever not responding at 72 hours; air in the kidney on CT = emphysematous pyelonephritis in diabetics — surgical emergency)
- **Urosepsis → septic shock and multi-organ failure** — most common lethal outcome of UTI
- **Chronic pyelonephritis → reflux nephropathy → CKD** — particularly with childhood UTI and vesicoureteric reflux
- **Perinephric abscess** — extension of renal abscess through the renal capsule; may track through the retroperitoneum; often requires percutaneous drainage
- **Iatrogenic C. difficile infection** — from broad-spectrum antibiotics used for resistant or complicated UTI

## Clinical Insight

Nitrofurantoin concentrates in the urine to achieve bactericidal levels, but barely penetrates renal parenchyma. Using nitrofurantoin for pyelonephritis is a prescribing error that results in inadequate tissue drug levels and clinical treatment failure despite "appropriate" antibiotic coverage. The rule is simple: nitrofurantoin treats bladder infections; ciprofloxacin or co-amoxiclav treats kidney infections.

Sterile pyuria — white cells in the urine with a negative standard bacterial culture — must never be dismissed as "a negative result." The most important diagnosis to exclude is **urinary tuberculosis**: Mycobacterium tuberculosis does not grow on standard urine culture media and causes a characteristic sterile pyuria. Early morning urine samples for AFB smear and TB culture (three samples on consecutive days) are the investigation. A missed renal TB diagnosis leads to progressive renal destruction and potential spread.

Recurrent UTIs in a young woman on repeated trimethoprim courses without culture guidance is a pattern that reliably selects for resistant E. coli. By the time the patient presents requesting another prescription, resistance rates in her personal flora may be 50% or higher. Send a culture before each treatment course, perform a vaginal examination (if indicated), and invest time in behavioural and preventive strategies — post-coital voiding, adequate hydration, D-mannose, and if needed, low-dose prophylaxis. Another empirical trimethoprim course is not a management plan; it is a temporary fix that worsens the medium-term problem.
