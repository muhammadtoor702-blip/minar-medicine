## Overview

The conditions in this chapter — endometriosis, polycystic ovary syndrome, uterine fibroids, ovarian cysts, and pelvic inflammatory disease — collectively account for a substantial proportion of gynaecology outpatient consultations. They share a tendency to be underdiagnosed (endometriosis averages 7–10 years from symptom onset to diagnosis) and an outsized impact on fertility, quality of life, and long-term health.

Understanding the pathophysiology of each condition explains the logic of every treatment option — why a GnRH analogue works for fibroids, why metformin helps PCOS, and why drainage alone is inadequate for endometriomas.

## Pathophysiology

**Endometriosis**: The most widely accepted theory is **retrograde menstruation** — shed endometrial cells travel retrogradely through the fallopian tubes and implant on peritoneal surfaces, ovaries, and the rectovaginal septum. Why only some women develop endometriosis is likely due to an impaired immune clearance mechanism — natural killer cells and macrophages normally clear refluxed endometrial debris; in women with endometriosis, this clearance fails. Ectopic endometrial tissue responds to the same hormonal cycles as the uterine endometrium, bleeding with each menstrual cycle, triggering a local inflammatory response → fibrosis → adhesions. The alternative **coelomic metaplasia** theory proposes differentiation of peritoneal mesothelial cells into endometrium-like tissue — this explains rare cases in men on oestrogen therapy.

**PCOS**: The central abnormality is **insulin resistance** (affecting ~70% of women with PCOS). Hyperinsulinaemia acts synergistically with LH to stimulate theca cell androgen production. Simultaneously, elevated insulin suppresses SHBG production in the liver, increasing free androgen bioavailability. Elevated androgens disrupt follicle development → small follicles arrested at 2–9 mm (the "necklace" sign on USS) → anovulation. The hypothalamic-pituitary axis contributes: increased LH pulse frequency → higher LH:FSH ratio (classically >2.5:1, though not required for diagnosis).

**Fibroids**: Monoclonal smooth muscle tumours dependent on oestrogen and progesterone for growth. Each fibroid arises from a single transformed myocyte. They shrink reliably after the menopause — a predictable, reassuring natural history for women approaching that transition.

**PID**: Ascending infection. Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae ascend from the cervix via the endometrium to the fallopian tubes, causing acute salpingitis. Anaerobes and vaginal flora frequently co-infect, particularly in IUD-related PID. The inflammatory cascade causes fibrosis and scarring of the fallopian tubes → blocked tubes → infertility and ectopic risk.

## Clinical Presentation

**Endometriosis — the Three Ds:**
- **Dysmenorrhoea** — progressive (worsens over years), starts before menstrual flow, not relieved by standard analgesia
- **Dyspareunia** — deep, positional (posterior fornix tenderness on examination suggests rectovaginal disease)
- **Dyschezia** — pain on defaecation, especially cyclically, suggests posterior compartment / rectovaginal septum or rectal involvement

Other features: chronic pelvic pain, cyclical symptoms (rectal bleeding, haematuria, shoulder pain — rare but indicate diaphragmatic or urological disease), and infertility (distorted pelvic anatomy + inflammatory effects on oocyte quality and implantation).

Examination: tender nodularity in the posterior fornix or uterosacral ligaments, fixed retroverted uterus, adnexal masses (endometriomas), reduced uterine mobility.

**PCOS — Rotterdam criteria (diagnosis requires 2 of 3)**:
1. Oligo/anovulation (irregular or absent periods, cycles >35 days)
2. Clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism — Ferriman-Gallwey score, acne, androgenic alopecia; or elevated total/free testosterone or DHEAS)
3. Polycystic ovarian morphology on USS (≥20 follicles 2–9 mm per ovary in ISUOG 2023 criteria, or ovarian volume >10 mL in either ovary)

Metabolic features: central obesity, acanthosis nigricans (insulin resistance marker — velvety hyperpigmentation at axillae and neck), impaired glucose tolerance.

**Fibroids — symptoms by location:**

| Type | Location | Typical Symptoms |
|------|----------|-----------------|
| Submucosal | Inside uterine cavity | Heavy menstrual bleeding, intermenstrual bleeding, infertility |
| Intramural | Within myometrium | Bulk symptoms, dysmenorrhoea, HMB |
| Subserosal | Outer uterine surface | Pressure symptoms (urinary frequency, constipation, abdominal distension) |
| Pedunculated | On a stalk (can be subserosal or submucosal) | Torsion of pedicle → acute pain |

**Red degeneration** (occurs during pregnancy): Fibroid outgrows its blood supply → central infarction → acute severe pain + uterine tenderness + fever. Managed conservatively (analgesia + hydration).

**PID**: Bilateral lower abdominal pain (acutely unwell), purulent vaginal discharge, fever. **Cervical motion tenderness (CMT)** — "chandelier sign" — moving the cervix causes severe pain as the inflamed adnexa are disturbed. Adnexal tenderness, tenderness in both iliac fossae.

**Ovarian cysts** — presentation depends on complication:
- Uncomplicated: often asymptomatic; dull pelvic ache; incidentally found on USS
- **Torsion**: Sudden, severe, unilateral, colicky pain with nausea and vomiting — the ovary twists on its ligament, compromising blood supply
- **Rupture/haemorrhage**: Acute lower abdominal pain ± haemoperitoneum; can mimic ectopic pregnancy
- Symptoms from size: pressure on bladder (urinary frequency), rectum (constipation), abdominal distension

## Diagnosis

**Endometriosis**: **Diagnostic laparoscopy is the gold standard** — direct visualisation and histological confirmation of endometrial glands and stroma outside the uterus. Typical laparoscopic appearances: powder-burn (black/blue) lesions, haemosiderin deposits, white scarred peritoneum, "chocolate" cysts of the ovaries. USS can detect endometriomas but cannot visualise peritoneal deposits.

**ASRM staging** (I–IV): Based on location, depth, extent of adhesions, and number of lesions. Critically, staging **correlates poorly with symptom severity** — Stage I disease can cause debilitating pain; Stage IV disease can be relatively asymptomatic.

PCOS investigations:
- USS pelvis: ovarian morphology
- Serum testosterone, SHBG, DHEAS, LH, FSH (LH:FSH ratio — elevated but not diagnostic)
- Fasting glucose + insulin (assess insulin resistance), fasting lipid profile
- Exclude differentials: thyroid function, prolactin, 21-hydroxylase deficiency (17-OHP), Cushing's (dexamethasone suppression test if clinically suspected)

Fibroids: USS pelvis (confirms diagnosis, maps location and size); MRI for complex cases or pre-operatively. Saline-infusion sonohysterography (SIS) or hysteroscopy to assess submucosal involvement.

**PID**: Clinical diagnosis — no single test is definitive. Endocervical swabs for Chlamydia (NAAT) and gonorrhoea (culture). Raised CRP and WBC support the diagnosis. USS if tubo-ovarian abscess suspected. IUCD removal if in situ (improves response to treatment).

**Ovarian cysts**: TVS (first-line), RMI score (CA-125 × USS score × menopausal status — score ≥200 requires urgent gynaecology oncology referral). CT/MRI for complex or malignant-looking masses.

## Management

**Endometriosis**:

*Medical*: The goal is suppression of the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis → reduced oestrogen → reduced endometrial stimulation. Options:
- **Combined OCP** or **progestogens** (norethisterone, medroxyprogesterone) — suppress ovulation, reduce endometrial proliferation; **not curative** but effective for symptom control
- **GnRH analogues** (e.g., leuprorelin, goserelin): Continuous (non-pulsatile) GnRH stimulation → pituitary downregulation → "medical oophorectomy" → profound oestrogen suppression → dramatic symptom relief and endometrioma shrinkage. Use short-term only (6 months) due to bone density loss; use **add-back HRT** (low-dose oestrogen + progestogen) to mitigate this without losing efficacy
- **Levonorgestrel IUS**: Effective for pain and HMB; suitable for long-term use

*Surgical*:
- **Peritoneal disease**: Excision is superior to ablation (laser/diathermy) — lower recurrence rates, better pain outcomes
- **Endometriomas**: **Excise the cyst wall** (cystectomy), not drain-and-ablate. The recurrence rate after drainage alone is very high (~50%) because residual endometriotic wall tissue continues to respond to cycles. However, ovarian cystectomy risks reducing ovarian reserve — relevant if fertility is a priority.
- **Deep infiltrating endometriosis**: Specialist centre surgery; may involve bowel resection, ureteric dissection

*Fertility*: Surgical treatment of endometriomas improves spontaneous conception rates. For persistent infertility: IUI or IVF. IVF success rates in endometriosis are slightly lower than in unexplained infertility — counsel accordingly.

**PCOS**:

| Goal | Intervention |
|------|-------------|
| Regulate cycles / protect endometrium | Combined OCP OR cyclic progestogen every 3 months |
| Ovulation induction (fertility) | **Letrozole** (aromatase inhibitor, first-line) OR **clomifene citrate** (anti-oestrogen, binds hypothalamic ER → increased GnRH/FSH) |
| Insulin resistance / metabolic | **Metformin** — improves insulin sensitivity, suppresses ovarian androgen, restores ovulation in ~40% |
| Hirsutism/acne | OCP ± anti-androgen (spironolactone, cyproterone acetate) |
| Weight | Lifestyle and weight loss — **5–10% weight loss restores ovulation in many women**; most effective intervention overall |

Long-term surveillance: annual BP, fasting glucose/HbA1c, fasting lipids. Ensure **endometrial protection** — women with anovulation need induced withdrawal bleeds (OCP or progestogen) at least every 3 months to prevent endometrial hyperplasia from continuous unopposed oestrogen.

**Fibroids**:
- **Watchful waiting**: Reasonable for asymptomatic or peri-menopausal women (will regress after menopause)
- **Hormonal**: OCP, norethisterone, levonorgestrel IUS — reduce bleeding; do not shrink fibroids
- **GnRH analogues**: Shrink fibroids by ~35–65% (oestrogen withdrawal); used **short-term pre-operatively** to reduce fibroid volume and correct anaemia; not for long-term use (bone density loss)
- **Ulipristal acetate** (selective progesterone receptor modulator): Withdrawn in UK/Europe 2020 due to hepatotoxicity concerns
- **Uterine artery embolisation (UAE)**: Fluoroscopy-guided occlusion of uterine arteries → fibroid infarction → volume reduction. Excellent for symptom relief. **Not recommended** if future pregnancy desired (increased miscarriage and obstetric complication risk)
- **Myomectomy**: Surgical removal of fibroids preserving uterus — for women desiring future fertility; higher recurrence of fibroids vs. hysterectomy
- **Hysterectomy**: Definitive cure — appropriate when fertility no longer desired and symptoms refractory to other treatments

**PID**:

Outpatient regimen (mild–moderate disease):
- **Ceftriaxone 1 g IM stat** (covers gonorrhoea)
- **Doxycycline 100 mg BD × 14 days** (covers Chlamydia, atypicals)
- **Metronidazole 400 mg BD × 14 days** (covers anaerobes)

Inpatient IV regimen (severe disease, tubo-ovarian abscess, unable to tolerate oral, uncertain diagnosis):
- IV cefoxitin + IV doxycycline → transition to oral once improving

**Partner notification and treatment** is mandatory — contact tracing to prevent reinfection.

Remove IUCD if present (re-insert 6 weeks after treatment if still desired).

**Ovarian cysts — management by risk stratification**:
- **Premenopausal, simple, thin-walled, <5 cm**: Reassure, repeat USS at 6–12 weeks (most functional cysts resolve)
- **Simple, 5–7 cm**: Annual USS surveillance
- **>7 cm, solid components, complex morphology, or post-menopausal**: Surgical evaluation; calculate RMI
- **Torsion**: Surgical emergency — **laparoscopic de-torsion** (preserves ovary if viable) or oophorectomy if infarcted; do not delay
- **Dermoid cyst (mature cystic teratoma)**: Surgical removal (risk of torsion, rupture causing chemical peritonitis)

## Complications

**Endometriosis**: Infertility (up to 50% of infertile women have endometriosis), deep infiltrating disease affecting rectum/ureter/bladder, psychological impact of chronic pain, malignant transformation in endometriomas (rare — endometrioid and clear cell ovarian cancer have an endometriosis association).

**PCOS**: T2DM (7× increased risk), metabolic syndrome, dyslipidaemia, NAFLD, cardiovascular disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, endometrial cancer (from chronic anovulation + unopposed oestrogen), anxiety and depression (significantly increased prevalence), pregnancy complications (gestational diabetes, pre-eclampsia, preterm birth).

**Fibroids**: Iron deficiency anaemia (from HMB), urinary retention (large anterior fibroids), hydronephrosis (compressing ureter), infertility, recurrence after myomectomy, obstetric complications (malpresentation, placenta praevia, PPH, red degeneration).

**PID**: **Fitz-Hugh–Curtis syndrome** — perihepatic adhesions causing right upper quadrant pain, pleuritic in character; adhesions described as "violin strings" between the liver capsule and anterior abdominal wall on laparoscopy; occurs in ~25% of PID cases. **Tubo-ovarian abscess** (TOA) — drainage may be required. Long-term: chronic pelvic pain, ectopic pregnancy, infertility (one episode PID → 10–15% infertility; three episodes → ~50%).

## Clinical Insight

The woman in this scenario has **moderate-to-severe endometriosis** by clinical and sonographic criteria: 8 years of progressive symptoms (the Three Ds), bilateral ovarian endometriomas (4 cm right, 3 cm left), and infertility after 18 months of unprotected intercourse.

The endometriomas are the key finding that changes management. Medical therapy alone (OCP, progestogens) will suppress symptoms while on treatment but will not resolve the endometriomas, and endometriomas themselves impair ovarian function and fertilisation. The evidence supports **surgical excision (cystectomy)** of the endometriomas to improve both pain and spontaneous conception rates — though with the important caveat that cystectomy reduces ovarian reserve (anti-Müllerian hormone falls after surgery).

Immediate management considerations:
1. Refer to specialist endometriosis centre (bilateral endometriomas = advanced disease)
2. Check AMH before surgery (baseline ovarian reserve)
3. Laparoscopic cystectomy — excise wall, not drain
4. If no spontaneous conception within 6 months post-surgery: IVF
5. Post-surgery suppression (LNG-IUS or progestogen) to reduce recurrence if fertility is achieved

Do not give GnRH analogues to shrink the cysts before surgery if fertility is the priority — the delay costs time, and these women often have reduced ovarian reserve already.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**Working through the case**: A 27-year-old woman with 8 years of progressive dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia, and dyschezia — the Three Ds of endometriosis. Now presenting with infertility at 18 months. Bilateral endometriomas on USS.

This is **moderate-to-severe endometriosis with bilateral endometriomas** causing infertility. The USS confirms the diagnosis without requiring laparoscopy at this stage (endometriomas are pathognomonic for endometriosis).

Investigations:
- Check AMH and antral follicle count (AFC) — assess ovarian reserve before surgery
- Partner semen analysis (assess all causes of infertility simultaneously)
- USS to confirm findings
- Consider MRI pelvis if deep infiltrating disease suspected (rectovaginal nodularity, ureteric involvement)

Management plan:
- Laparoscopic bilateral endometrioma cystectomy (excise wall — not drain and ablate)
- Peritoneal disease excision at the same laparoscopy
- Post-operative monitoring: repeat AMH 3 months post-surgery
- Trial of natural conception for 6 months; if unsuccessful, refer for IVF
- Discuss long-term suppression after fertility completed (LNG-IUS is the most evidence-based long-term option)

**Exam trigger:** The classic presentation is a woman with years of progressive cyclical pelvic pain, the Three Ds (dysmenorrhoea, dyspareunia, dyschezia), infertility, and endometriomas on USS — the answer is endometriosis requiring laparoscopic cystectomy. The most common distractor is recommending drainage and ablation of the endometrioma rather than excision; drainage alone has a ~50% recurrence rate because the residual endometriotic cyst wall remains, and ablation does not adequately treat the underlying disease — cystectomy is the evidence-based approach.
