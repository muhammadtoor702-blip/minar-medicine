## Overview

Acid-base homeostasis maintains arterial pH between 7.35 and 7.45. Deviations beyond this range impair enzyme function, alter drug ionisation, and disrupt cardiovascular physiology. Four primary disorders exist — metabolic acidosis, metabolic alkalosis, respiratory acidosis, and respiratory alkalosis — and they may coexist as mixed disorders. A systematic stepwise approach to ABG interpretation is essential: it is a logical deductive process, not a pattern-recognition exercise. This patient has a high anion gap metabolic acidosis from DKA with appropriate respiratory compensation (Kussmaul breathing); the deep sighing respirations represent the respiratory system attempting to blow off CO₂ to compensate.

---

## Pathophysiology

**Henderson-Hasselbalch equation:** pH = pKa + log([HCO₃−] / (0.03 × PaCO₂))

The pH depends on the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO₂. The respiratory system controls PaCO₂ within minutes; the kidneys control HCO₃− over hours to days. A primary disorder in one triggers compensatory changes in the other — always in the same direction (both try to restore the ratio toward 7.4), never overshooting normal pH.

**The kidney's role in acid-base:** The proximal tubule reabsorbs ~85% of filtered HCO₃−. The distal nephron excretes H+ as NH₄+ (the dominant mechanism — generates new HCO₃−) and titratable acid (H₂PO₄−). Renal compensation is powerful but slow: maximal HCO₃− regeneration takes 3–5 days in chronic respiratory acidosis.

## The Systematic ABG Approach

A reliable six-step method that applies to any ABG:

**Step 1 — pH:** Acidaemia (<7.35), alkalaemia (>7.45), or normal (7.35–7.45)?
*Note: "normal" pH does not exclude a mixed disorder — compensation or two opposing disorders can normalise pH.*

**Step 2 — Primary disorder:** Which parameter explains the pH abnormality?
- pH low: PaCO₂ high → respiratory acidosis; OR HCO₃− low → metabolic acidosis
- pH high: PaCO₂ low → respiratory alkalosis; OR HCO₃− high → metabolic alkalosis

**Step 3 — Compensation:** Is the response appropriate? Compensation returns pH toward 7.4 but never fully corrects it. If compensation is greater or less than predicted, a second primary disorder is present.

| Primary Disorder | Expected Compensation | Formula |
|----------------|----------------------|---------|
| Metabolic acidosis | ↓ PaCO₂ (hyperventilation) | **Winter's formula**: PaCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃ + 8 (±2) |
| Metabolic alkalosis | ↑ PaCO₂ (hypoventilation) | PaCO₂ = 0.7 × HCO₃ + 21 (±2) |
| Respiratory acidosis (acute) | ↑ HCO₃ 1 mEq/L per 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂ | |
| Respiratory acidosis (chronic) | ↑ HCO₃ 3.5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg ↑ PaCO₂ | |
| Respiratory alkalosis (acute) | ↓ HCO₃ 2 mEq/L per 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂ | |
| Respiratory alkalosis (chronic) | ↓ HCO₃ 5 mEq/L per 10 mmHg ↓ PaCO₂ | |

**Step 4 — Anion gap** (if metabolic acidosis):
AG = Na+ − (Cl− + HCO₃−). Normal = 8–12 mEq/L.
*Correct for albumin: for every 1 g/dL albumin falls below 4 g/dL, add 2.5 mEq/L to the measured AG — hypoalbuminaemia masks a raised AG.*

**Step 5 — Delta-delta ratio** (if raised AG metabolic acidosis):
(Measured AG − 12) ÷ (24 − measured HCO₃−)
- Ratio <0.4: pure normal AG metabolic acidosis
- Ratio 0.4–1: mixed high AG + normal AG metabolic acidosis
- Ratio 1–2: pure high AG metabolic acidosis
- Ratio >2: mixed high AG metabolic acidosis + metabolic alkalosis (the HCO₃− is inappropriately high for the degree of AG elevation — look for vomiting or diuretic use)

**Step 6 — Clinical context:** Integrate with history, examination, and investigations.

**Applying the steps to this patient:**
Step 1: pH 7.18 → acidaemia. Step 2: HCO₃ 8 mEq/L → metabolic acidosis. Step 3: Winter's predicted PaCO₂ = (1.5 × 8) + 8 = 20 mmHg — measured PaCO₂ 21 mmHg is appropriate compensation (no additional respiratory disorder). Step 4: AG = 140 − (102 + 8) = 30 mEq/L → high AG. Cause = DKA.

## Metabolic Acidosis

### High Anion Gap Metabolic Acidosis

A raised AG means unmeasured anions are accumulating in the plasma, consuming bicarbonate. Mnemonic — **MUDPILES:**

| Letter | Cause | Key Feature |
|--------|-------|-------------|
| M | Methanol | Elevated osmolar gap; visual disturbance |
| U | Uraemia | Creatinine elevated; organic acid accumulation |
| D | DKA / starvation / alcoholic ketoacidosis | Ketonaemia; insulin deficiency |
| P | Propylene glycol; paracetamol (late) | ICU sedation; paracetamol overdose with ALF |
| I | Isoniazid; iron | INH inhibits pyridoxal phosphate → lactic acidosis |
| L | Lactic acidosis | Most common in hospital; low or normal AG if measured as L-lactate only |
| E | Ethylene glycol | Osmolar gap; calcium oxalate crystals in urine; AKI |
| S | Salicylates | Mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis |

**Lactic acidosis** is the most common cause of high AG metabolic acidosis in hospitalised patients. Lactate >18 mg/dL (>2 mmol/L) is significant; >45 mg/dL (>5 mmol/L) is severe.
- **Type A** (hypoperfusion — inadequate tissue oxygen delivery): septic shock, cardiogenic shock, haemorrhagic shock, mesenteric ischaemia, carbon monoxide poisoning
- **Type B** (mitochondrial dysfunction without global hypoperfusion): metformin toxicity in AKI (most important drug cause), liver failure (reduced lactate clearance), mitochondrial disease, HIV medications (NRTIs), malignancy (Warburg effect)

**DKA — mechanism and management:**

Absolute insulin deficiency (or severe relative deficiency) → uninhibited lipolysis → free fatty acid delivery to liver → hepatic ketogenesis → acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate accumulate → high AG metabolic acidosis. Simultaneously: glucagon excess drives glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis → hyperglycaemia. Osmotic diuresis → severe volume depletion and electrolyte losses.

**DKA management (ADA protocol):**

1. **IV fluid resuscitation**: 0.9% NaCl 1 L/hour for first 2 hours, then 0.45% NaCl or 0.9% NaCl depending on serum sodium and corrected sodium. Switch to 5% dextrose + 0.45% NaCl when glucose <250 mg/dL.

2. **Insulin infusion**: Fixed-rate IV insulin 0.1 units/kg/hour. Do NOT start insulin until K+ >3.5 mEq/L — insulin drives K+ into cells and will cause fatal hypokalaemia if K+ is already low. If K+ <3.5 mEq/L, replace potassium first.

3. **Potassium replacement**: All DKA patients are total body potassium depleted (acidosis drives K+ out of cells, and osmotic diuresis causes urinary K+ loss). Serum K+ appears falsely normal or elevated at presentation. As insulin is given and acidosis corrects, K+ re-enters cells precipitously — monitor K+ every 1–2 hours.

4. **Resolution criteria**: pH >7.3, HCO₃ >15 mEq/L, anion gap <12 mEq/L, and blood ketones <0.3 mmol/L (or urinary ketones negative on diluted sample). Do NOT use glucose normalisation as the resolution criterion — hyperglycaemia clears faster than ketoacidosis.

5. **Transition to subcutaneous insulin**: Overlap IV and SC insulin by 30–60 minutes to prevent rebound ketosis.

**Cerebral oedema** is the most feared complication of DKA treatment — particularly in children and young adults. Occurs with overly aggressive fluid resuscitation and rapid osmolality correction. Suspect if there is headache, deteriorating consciousness, or Cushing's response during treatment. Treat with IV mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg and restrict free water.

### Normal Anion Gap (Hyperchloraemic) Metabolic Acidosis

HCO₃− loss is replaced by chloride — the anion gap remains normal. The urine anion gap (UAG = urine Na + K − Cl) distinguishes renal from GI causes:
- **Negative UAG** (urine Cl > urine Na + K): NH₄+ excretion intact → GI cause (diarrhoea, ileostomy)
- **Positive UAG** (urine Cl < urine Na + K): NH₄+ excretion impaired → renal cause (RTA, CKD)

| Cause | Mechanism | Distinguishing Feature |
|-------|-----------|----------------------|
| Diarrhoea | HCO₃− loss in stool | Negative UAG; hypokalaemia |
| Ileostomy / fistula | Alkaline intestinal fluid lost | Negative UAG |
| Excessive 0.9% NaCl | Supraphysiological Cl− load | Post-resuscitation; dilutional |
| **Type 1 RTA** (distal) | Distal tubule cannot secrete H+ | Urine pH >5.5 despite acidaemia; hypokalaemia; nephrocalcinosis. Causes: Sjögren's, SLE, amphotericin |
| **Type 2 RTA** (proximal) | Cannot reabsorb HCO₃− | Fanconi syndrome (glucosuria, aminoaciduria, phosphaturia). Causes: myeloma, Wilson's, ifosfamide |
| **Type 4 RTA** (hyperkalaemic) | Aldosterone deficiency/resistance → impaired NH₄+ | **Hyperkalaemia** + acidosis; most common RTA. Causes: diabetic nephropathy (hyporeninaemic hypoaldosteronism), ACEi/ARB, NSAIDs |
| Early CKD | Reduced NH₄+ excretion | eGFR typically 20–40 |

## Metabolic Alkalosis

Metabolic alkalosis results from excess base gain or acid loss. The kidney has enormous capacity to excrete bicarbonate and would normally correct alkalosis rapidly — metabolic alkalosis is sustained only when the kidney is prevented from excreting HCO₃−, usually by volume depletion (ECFV contraction) or hypokalaemia.

**Saline-responsive (urine Cl− <20 mEq/L) vs saline-resistant (urine Cl− >20 mEq/L):**

| Category | Urine Cl− | Cause | Treatment |
|---------|-----------|-------|-----------|
| Saline-responsive | <20 mEq/L | Vomiting, nasogastric suction, post-diuretic, post-hypercapnia | IV 0.9% NaCl + KCl replacement |
| Saline-resistant | >20 mEq/L | Conn's syndrome, Cushing's, Bartter/Gitelman syndrome | Treat underlying cause |

**Vomiting and paradoxical aciduria:** HCl loss in vomit raises bicarbonate. Volume depletion and hypokalaemia from prolonged vomiting drive the kidney to reabsorb Na+ at the expense of H+ secretion in the distal tubule — producing acid urine despite systemic alkalosis. The treatment is volume replacement (saline) — without this, the kidney will continue to excrete acid and perpetuate the alkalosis regardless of HCO₃− levels. Giving bicarbonate in this setting makes things worse.

## Respiratory Acidosis

Alveolar hypoventilation → CO₂ retention → PaCO₂ rises → pH falls.

**Causes by mechanism:**
- **Central respiratory drive suppression**: opiates, benzodiazepines, alcohol, brainstem injury, obesity hypoventilation syndrome
- **Neuromuscular failure**: Guillain-Barré, myasthenia gravis, MND, high cervical cord injury, critical illness polyneuropathy
- **Chest wall restriction**: severe kyphoscoliosis, obesity (BMI >50), thoracoplasty
- **Airway disease**: severe COPD or asthma exacerbation, upper airway obstruction

**Acute vs chronic hypercapnia:** In chronic hypercapnia (COPD, OHS), renal compensation raises HCO₃− over days to weeks — pH is near-normal despite elevated PaCO₂. An acute rise on this chronic baseline produces a sharp fall in pH because the buffering capacity is already used up. The ABG shows high PaCO₂, high HCO₃− (compensation), but low-normal or low pH — the "acute-on-chronic" pattern.

**Management:** Treat the underlying cause. NIV (BiPAP) for COPD with acute hypercapnic respiratory failure — reduces need for intubation. **Target SpO₂ 88–92% in known COPD** — supplemental oxygen that abolishes hypoxic drive causes respiratory depression and worsens CO₂ retention.

## Respiratory Alkalosis

Alveolar hyperventilation → CO₂ blown off → PaCO₂ falls → pH rises.

**Causes:**
- **Hypoxia-driven** (reflex hyperventilation): pneumonia, PE, pulmonary oedema, altitude
- **Direct central stimulation**: anxiety/panic (most common), salicylate toxicity (early phase — stimulates respiratory centre directly), pregnancy (progesterone stimulates breathing), liver failure (hepatic encephalopathy), fever, pain
- **Iatrogenic**: over-ventilation on mechanical ventilator

**Features of acute alkalosis:** Perioral tingling, carpopedal spasm (Trousseau's sign), tetany — from reduced ionised calcium. Alkalosis increases calcium binding to albumin, lowering the free ionised fraction. Symptomatic alkalosis may require IV calcium gluconate.

**Salicylate toxicity:** Classically a **mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis**. Early stage: salicylates directly stimulate the respiratory centre → respiratory alkalosis. Later: uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation → lactic acid + ketone accumulation → high AG metabolic acidosis. The diagnostic clue is the mixed picture — either disorder alone would suggest different diagnoses.

## Mixed Disorders

| Combination | Clinical Example | Diagnostic Clue |
|------------|----------------|-----------------|
| High AG metabolic acidosis + respiratory alkalosis | Salicylate toxicity, sepsis, liver failure | PaCO₂ lower than Winter's prediction |
| High AG metabolic acidosis + metabolic alkalosis | DKA with vomiting; renal failure + vomiting | Delta-delta ratio >2 |
| High AG + normal AG metabolic acidosis | DKA + saline resuscitation; lactic acidosis + diarrhoea | Delta-delta ratio <1 |
| Respiratory acidosis + metabolic alkalosis | COPD + diuretics; COPD + vomiting | HCO₃ higher than expected for chronic CO₂ retention |
| Respiratory acidosis + metabolic acidosis | Cardiac arrest; severe COPD + sepsis | pH very low; both PaCO₂ up and HCO₃ down |

## Complications

- **Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1)**: myocardial depression, vasodilation, impaired consciousness, arrhythmias
- **DKA**: cerebral oedema (particularly children and young adults with rapid correction), fatal hypokalaemia during insulin infusion, aspiration
- **Severe metabolic alkalosis**: tetany, seizures, arrhythmias from associated hypokalaemia and reduced ionised calcium
- **Respiratory alkalosis (acute)**: cerebral vasoconstriction → dizziness, syncope, paraesthesiae
- **Lactic acidosis**: multi-organ failure; Type A mortality >50%
- **Iatrogenic bicarbonate therapy**: paradoxical CSF acidosis (CO₂ crosses blood-brain barrier faster than HCO₃−), alkalosis-related hypokalaemia, worsening intracellular acidosis

## Clinical Insight

Winter's formula is the single most important compensation calculation. In any metabolic acidosis, calculate the expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃) + 8 ± 2. If the measured PaCO₂ is higher than predicted — there is a concurrent respiratory acidosis (respiratory muscles are failing to compensate; consider opiates, neuromuscular disease, fatigue). If the measured PaCO₂ is lower than predicted — there is a concurrent respiratory alkalosis (a separate stimulus to hyperventilate beyond the metabolic compensation, such as sepsis, salicylates, or hepatic failure). Most mixed disorders are revealed by this single calculation.

In DKA, the potassium appears normal or elevated at presentation because acidosis drives K+ out of cells. This is deceptive — total body K+ is profoundly depleted from osmotic diuresis. As insulin is given and pH corrects, K+ re-enters cells and serum K+ can fall catastrophically. Never start insulin with K+ <3.5 mEq/L. Check K+ every 1–2 hours and replace aggressively throughout the infusion.

Salicylate toxicity must be considered in any mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis. The pattern is almost diagnostic when found together. Early aspirin overdose looks like a pure respiratory alkalosis (salicylates stimulate the respiratory centre); as the overdose progresses, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation generates organic acids and the picture shifts toward mixed. Always measure a salicylate level in any unexplained ABG showing this combination, particularly in a patient found collapsed at home.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 19-year-old woman with T1DM, missed insulin for 2 days. Confused, vomiting, RR 28 (deep sighing respirations). ABG: pH 7.18, PaO₂ 99 mmHg, PaCO₂ 21 mmHg, HCO₃ 8 mEq/L, BE −18. Glucose 504 mg/dL. Strong ketones in blood and urine.

**Step 1 — Read the ABG systematically:**
- pH 7.18 = **acidosis**
- HCO₃ 8 mEq/L = low = **metabolic (primary)**
- PaCO₂ 21 mmHg = low = respiratory compensation (Kussmaul breathing)
- Diagnosis so far: **primary metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation**

**Step 2 — Check compensation with Winter's formula:**
Expected PaCO₂ = (1.5 × HCO₃) + 8 ± 2 = (1.5 × 8) + 8 ± 2 = **20 ± 2 mmHg**
Actual PaCO₂ = 21 mmHg → **within the expected range** → **appropriate compensation** → no concurrent respiratory disorder. This is a **pure high anion gap metabolic acidosis**.

**Step 3 — Confirm DKA:** Glucose 504 mg/dL + ketonemia + metabolic acidosis = **DKA**. Classic presentation in T1DM + missed insulin (most common precipitant is infection; this case is missed medication).

**Step 4 — Check potassium BEFORE starting insulin (the most important step):**
- Acidosis drives K⁺ out of cells → serum K⁺ may appear normal or elevated at presentation
- Total body K⁺ is profoundly depleted from osmotic diuresis
- Insulin rapidly drives K⁺ back into cells → serum K⁺ can fall precipitously
- **Never start insulin if K⁺ <3.5 mEq/L** → replace potassium first
- Check K⁺ every 1–2 hours throughout treatment

**Step 5 — DKA management:**
- **IV 0.9% saline** resuscitation (1 L over 1 hour, then titrate to response)
- **Fixed-rate insulin infusion 0.1 units/kg/hour** — once K⁺ ≥3.5 mEq/L
- Add KCl to IV fluids from the outset (unless K⁺ ≥5.5 mEq/L)
- Switch to 10% glucose when blood glucose <200 mg/dL (to allow continued insulin without hypoglycaemia)
- Resolution criteria: pH >7.3 + HCO₃ >15 mEq/L + blood ketones <0.6 mmol/L
- Do not stop insulin infusion until eating and drinking; switch to SC insulin at the next meal

**Exam trigger:** *DKA + normal serum K⁺ on admission* → do not be reassured; total body K⁺ is depleted. Start K⁺ replacement immediately. Starting insulin without correcting K⁺ can cause fatal cardiac arrhythmia from hypokalaemia.
