## Overview

Anaphylaxis is a severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. It is characterised by rapid onset, involvement of multiple organ systems, and — without prompt treatment — the potential for death from airway obstruction or cardiovascular collapse within minutes. Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis; do not wait for investigations or test results before treating. The single most important intervention is intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine), and the single most dangerous error is delaying it or administering it by the wrong route. Every doctor who encounters sick patients must be able to recognise anaphylaxis and administer adrenaline without hesitation.

---

## Pathophysiology

### IgE-Mediated (Classic Anaphylaxis)

Prior sensitisation to an allergen produces allergen-specific IgE antibodies, which bind to high-affinity receptors (FcεRI) on mast cells and basophils. On re-exposure, cross-linking of IgE molecules by the allergen triggers rapid, massive **mast cell degranulation**, releasing:

- **Histamine** (preformed): binds H1 receptors → vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, bronchoconstriction, urticaria, angioedema
- **Tryptase** (preformed): a serine protease — its elevation in serum confirms mast cell activation
- **Prostaglandins and leukotrienes** (newly synthesised): potent bronchoconstrictors; LTC4/D4/E4 (slow-reacting substances of anaphylaxis) prolong bronchoconstriction
- **Platelet-activating factor (PAF)**: vasodilation, increased permeability

The combined effect: systemic vasodilation + massive capillary leak → distributive shock; airway oedema + bronchospasm → asphyxia.

### Non-IgE Mechanisms

- **Complement activation** (radiocontrast media, dextran): direct complement activation generates anaphylatoxins (C3a, C5a) → mast cell activation independent of IgE
- **Direct mast cell activation** (NSAIDs, opioids): no prior sensitisation required; may occur on first exposure; explains why aspirin/NSAIDs can cause severe reactions even without prior anaphylaxis history
- **Bradykinin-mediated angioedema** (ACE inhibitors): bradykinin degradation is blocked → accumulation → angioedema of the face and larynx; does NOT involve mast cells; NOT treated with adrenaline (no histamine component); treated by stopping the ACEi; icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist) for severe cases

### Why Adrenaline Works — and Why Nothing Else Does

Adrenaline (epinephrine) acts on:
- **Alpha-1 receptors**: vasoconstriction → reverses distributive shock, reduces mucosal oedema (including laryngeal angioedema)
- **Beta-1 receptors**: positive chronotropy and inotropy → improves cardiac output
- **Beta-2 receptors**: bronchodilation → reverses bronchoconstriction

Antihistamines (chlorphenamine) and corticosteroids (hydrocortisone) act too slowly to reverse the acute physiological crisis — they have no role in preventing or reversing cardiovascular collapse or laryngeal oedema in the acute phase. They are adjunctive only.

## Clinical Presentation

Anaphylaxis presents with rapid onset (seconds to minutes after exposure, occasionally up to 1–2 hours with food allergens) involving multiple organ systems:

**Skin/mucosal (80–90% of cases — most visible feature):**
- Urticaria (hives) — raised, itchy wheals that move and coalesce
- Flushing, erythema, pruritus
- Angioedema — deeper tissue swelling; periorbital, lip, tongue, laryngeal oedema

**Upper airway (severe — may be absent in early stages):**
- Stridor — from laryngeal/subglottic oedema; indicates upper airway compromise
- Hoarse voice — oedema of vocal cords
- Muffled voice, difficulty swallowing (drooling) — indicates severe tongue/laryngeal oedema
- **A rapidly progressing angioedema of the tongue or floor of mouth is a surgical airway emergency**

**Lower airway:**
- Bronchospasm → wheeze, prolonged expiration, reduced air entry
- Tachypnoea, SpO₂ falling

**Cardiovascular:**
- Hypotension + tachycardia = distributive shock from vasodilation and capillary leak
- May progress to cardiovascular collapse and cardiac arrest (predominantly pulseless electrical activity from profound vasodilation)
- In patients taking beta-blockers: bradycardia may occur instead of tachycardia (beta receptors blocked, alpha-mediated vasodilation unopposed)

**Other:** Anxiety, sense of doom, confusion (reduced cerebral perfusion), gastrointestinal (nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea).

**Important:** Anaphylaxis can occur WITHOUT skin features in 10–20% of cases — a patient who develops cardiovascular collapse or severe bronchospasm shortly after a drug or food exposure has anaphylaxis until proven otherwise, regardless of whether urticaria is present.

## Diagnosis

**Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis — do not delay treatment.**

Diagnostic criteria (likelihood that it is anaphylaxis):
1. Sudden onset of severe illness involving skin/mucosa **AND** airway and/or cardiovascular compromise — likely anaphylaxis
2. Sudden severe airway, breathing, or circulation problem after exposure to a known or probable allergen — likely anaphylaxis even without skin features

**Serum tryptase:** Mast cell tryptase peaks 15–60 minutes after onset of anaphylaxis, remains elevated for 3–6 hours. Sample timing: at presentation, at 1–2 hours post-onset, and baseline (>24 hours after event at follow-up). A raised peak tryptase confirms mast cell activation but a normal tryptase does NOT exclude anaphylaxis (tryptase may be normal in food-triggered anaphylaxis and in biphasic reactions). Used primarily for confirmation after the event, not for acute diagnosis.

**Do not check tryptase before treating.**

## Management

### Immediate — ABCDE

**Position:** Lay the patient flat with legs elevated (improves venous return) unless: airway compromise/breathing difficulty → semi-recumbent or sitting. **Critically: do NOT stand the patient up** — this causes pooling of blood in dilated peripheral vessels ("empty heart" syndrome), which can precipitate cardiac arrest in anaphylactic shock. Multiple deaths have been reported from patients being stood up to walk to resuscitation bays.

**Call for help:** Activate the cardiac arrest team if haemodynamic collapse is imminent.

### Adrenaline — The Only Life-Saving Drug

**Adrenaline 1:1000 (0.1%) — 0.5 mg (0.5 mL) IM into the outer lateral thigh (vastus lateralis)**

This is the correct dose, route, concentration, and site. Every element matters:

- **1:1000** = 1 mg/mL concentration for IM use (NOT 1:10,000 which is for IV use in cardiac arrest)
- **IM** (not SC, not IV): IM achieves faster and higher peak plasma levels than SC; IV adrenaline outside cardiac arrest is dangerous — risks hypertensive crises and ventricular fibrillation even at low doses
- **Lateral thigh** (not deltoid): the vastus lateralis has a rich blood supply and subcutaneous fat is thinner here → more reliable absorption; deltoid has more variable fat depth
- **Repeat after 5 minutes** if no improvement — there is no maximum number of doses; keep giving it

In **cardiac arrest from anaphylaxis**: standard ALS algorithm with adrenaline 1 mg (1:10,000) IV every 3–5 minutes.

**Special case — patients on beta-blockers:** Beta-blockers blunt the beta-2 bronchodilator response and block positive chronotropy. In refractory anaphylaxis in a beta-blocked patient: **glucagon 1–2 mg IV** — bypasses beta receptors to activate adenylyl cyclase via glucagon receptors, increasing heart rate and contractility independently.

### Airway

- High-flow O₂ via non-rebreathe mask (15 L/min)
- If stridor or progressive angioedema: prepare for intubation NOW, before the airway is lost
- **Early intubation** before airway swelling renders laryngoscopy impossible — a mildly swollen larynx that can be intubated easily will become completely obstructed if adrenaline is given and fails
- Have a **surgical airway** (cricothyrotomy) kit immediately available — angioedema is the most common cause of failed intubation in anaphylaxis
- Video laryngoscopy preferred for anticipated difficult airway

### Circulation

- IV access (large-bore) + rapid infusion of crystalloid 500–1000 mL 0.9% NaCl initially (may need several litres — capillary leak from anaphylaxis can be massive)
- If refractory hypotension despite adrenaline and fluids: noradrenaline infusion
- Continue monitoring HR, BP, SpO₂

### Adjuncts (secondary — do not delay adrenaline for these)

- **Chlorphenamine 10 mg IV/IM** (H1 antihistamine): reduces urticaria and pruritus; no role in preventing or reversing cardiovascular collapse
- **Hydrocortisone 200 mg IV**: may prevent or attenuate prolonged reactions; onset of action 4–6 hours — does not help in the acute phase
- **Nebulised salbutamol** (2.5–5 mg): for persistent bronchospasm after adrenaline

### Biphasic Reaction

Recurrence of anaphylaxis 1–72 hours after initial recovery **without further allergen exposure**, occurring in 5–20% of patients. Mechanism unclear (biphasic release of mediators?). Risk factors: prolonged time to first adrenaline, more severe initial reaction, required two or more adrenaline doses.

**Observation period:** Minimum **6 hours** after the last dose of adrenaline for mild-moderate anaphylaxis. **12–24 hours** for severe cases (required multiple adrenaline doses, severe hypotension, significant bronchospasm, suspected food allergen — food-triggered reactions tend to have longer and more variable biphasic periods).

## Complications

- **Death**: From asphyxia (laryngeal oedema — if adrenaline is delayed) or cardiovascular collapse
- **Hypoxic brain injury**: From prolonged hypotension or airway obstruction
- **Post-resuscitation myocardial injury**: Demand ischaemia from the profound haemodynamic stress
- **Biphasic anaphylaxis**: Up to 20% risk; can be more severe than the initial episode
- **Psychosocial**: PTSD, anxiety, lifestyle restriction (allergen avoidance), fear of subsequent reactions
- **Failed airway**: Laryngeal angioedema is among the most feared causes of cannot-intubate/cannot-oxygenate

## Clinical Insight

The most common cause of death from anaphylaxis is delayed administration of adrenaline. Not the wrong drug, not the wrong diagnosis — delayed treatment. Studies consistently show that adrenaline is underused, given too late, or given by the wrong route (IV instead of IM). Every healthcare provider should be able to draw up and administer IM adrenaline within 60 seconds of recognising anaphylaxis. This is a technical skill, not a clinical decision — if anaphylaxis is possible, give adrenaline. The consequences of giving unnecessary adrenaline to a fit young person with mild urticaria are trivial (transient tachycardia, anxiety). The consequences of withholding adrenaline from a patient with laryngeal oedema are irreversible.

ACE inhibitor angioedema is not anaphylaxis — and treating it as anaphylaxis with adrenaline does not work. ACEi angioedema is bradykinin-mediated (not histamine-mediated), it does not respond to adrenaline, antihistamines, or steroids, and the definitive treatment is stopping the ACEi. The clinical distinction: ACEi angioedema is typically isolated to the face, lips, tongue, and airway — no urticaria, no hypotension, no bronchospasm. It can develop months to years after starting the medication. The treatment is icatibant (bradykinin B2 receptor antagonist) or C1 esterase inhibitor concentrate for severe cases with airway compromise.

Latex anaphylaxis is a critical safety consideration in the operating theatre. Healthcare workers and patients with multiple prior surgeries (particularly spina bifida patients, who have the highest prevalence of latex allergy) are at high risk. Any patient with known latex allergy must have latex-free management throughout their hospital stay — this must be flagged before anaesthesia.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 23-year-old, 15 minutes after first amoxicillin dose. Stridor, urticaria, throat tightening. SpO₂ 91%, BP 72/40, HR 134, confused.

**Step 1 — Diagnose and act simultaneously:** This is anaphylaxis. All four criteria are met: (1) recent allergen exposure (amoxicillin), (2) skin features (urticaria), (3) upper airway compromise (stridor), (4) cardiovascular compromise (BP 72/40). No investigation needed. No further thought needed. Administer adrenaline.

**Step 2 — Adrenaline 0.5 mg IM lateral thigh (1:1000):**
- Draw up 0.5 mL of 1:1000 adrenaline in a 1 mL syringe
- Inject IM into the outer lateral thigh (through clothing is acceptable in emergency)
- Reassess in 5 minutes — if no improvement, give a second dose 0.5 mg IM

**Step 3 — Manage the airway:**
- SpO₂ 91% + stridor = upper airway compromise from laryngeal angioedema
- Call anaesthetics NOW — do not wait for adrenaline to work before calling for airway help
- High-flow O₂ 15 L/min NRB mask
- Prepare video laryngoscope + surgical airway kit
- If stridor worsens despite adrenaline → intubate early (while you still can)

**Step 4 — IV fluids for shock:**
- Two large-bore IV cannulae
- 0.9% NaCl 500 mL bolus rapidly; repeat as needed
- BP 72/40 = profound distributive shock; may need 2–3 L of crystalloid
- If BP does not respond to adrenaline + fluids → noradrenaline infusion

**Step 5 — Observation and discharge planning:**
- Minimum 6h observation from last adrenaline dose (biphasic reaction risk)
- Chlorphenamine 10 mg IM (antihistamine for urticaria) + hydrocortisone 200 mg IV (attenuates prolonged reaction)
- Serum tryptase at 1h and 4h (confirm mast cell activation)
- On discharge: prescribe 2× adrenaline auto-injectors (EpiPen 0.3 mg), written anaphylaxis action plan, allergen avoidance advice (all penicillins and cephalosporins), MedicAlert bracelet, urgent allergy referral

**Exam trigger:** *Drug-triggered urticaria + stridor + BP 72/40 + SpO₂ 91%* → anaphylaxis. The first and only life-saving intervention is IM adrenaline 0.5 mg 1:1000 into the lateral thigh. The distractor is giving chlorphenamine or hydrocortisone first — these are adjuncts with no role in reversing cardiovascular collapse or laryngeal oedema acutely. A second common error is giving IV adrenaline in a non-arrest patient — this risks arrhythmia and hypertensive crisis.
