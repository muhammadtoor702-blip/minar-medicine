## Overview

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and remains one of the most significant infectious diseases globally — the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent (ahead of HIV). The UK sees approximately 5,000 cases annually, with the highest rates in urban areas and in people born in high-burden countries (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe). The clinical challenge lies in early diagnosis, correct management, and preventing transmission and drug resistance.

## Pathophysiology

*M. tuberculosis* is an aerobic, slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus transmitted by inhalation of respiratory droplets from a person with active pulmonary TB. A single cough can release thousands of bacilli. Small particle droplet nuclei remain suspended in air for hours.

**Primary infection:** Inhaled bacilli reach the alveoli and are engulfed by alveolar macrophages. The bacilli survive within macrophages, evading destruction by inhibiting phagosome-lysosome fusion. A granulomatous response forms — the **Ghon focus** (subpleural consolidation, usually mid or lower zone) together with ipsilateral hilar lymphadenopathy forming the **primary (Ghon) complex**. In 90–95% of immunocompetent individuals, cell-mediated immunity contains the infection — the bacilli persist in a dormant state (**latent TB infection, LTBI**) but do not cause active disease.

**Post-primary (reactivation) TB:** Reactivation of dormant bacilli — usually in the **upper lobes** (high oxygen tension favours growth) — occurs in immunosuppression (HIV, diabetes, corticosteroids, anti-TNF agents, CKD), malnutrition, or advancing age. This is the typical presentation of adult pulmonary TB.

**Miliary TB** — haematogenous dissemination producing uniform 1–2mm nodules throughout both lung fields and potentially seeding all organs. Seen in primary progressive TB (children, immunocompromised) or in reactivation with overwhelming immunosuppression.

## Clinical Presentation

**Pulmonary TB:** The classic presentation is a subacute illness of weeks to months with:
- **Productive cough** — initially dry, later mucopurulent; haemoptysis may occur from cavitation or Rasmussen aneurysm rupture
- **Constitutional symptoms** — fever (classically low-grade and intermittent), drenching night sweats, weight loss, anorexia, fatigue
- CXR: upper lobe consolidation, cavitation, hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, fibrosis

**Extrapulmonary TB:** Any organ can be affected — TB lymphadenitis (most common extrapulmonary site in the UK: firm, matted, non-tender cervical lymphadenopathy; may form "collar stud" abscess), pleural TB (unilateral exudative effusion with lymphocyte predominance), TB meningitis (subacute meningitis with cranial nerve palsies and CSF showing high protein, low glucose, lymphocytosis), spinal TB (Pott's disease — vertebral collapse with gibbus deformity, potentially cord compression), renal and genitourinary TB (sterile pyuria — white cells in urine with no growth on standard culture), pericardial TB.

**TB in HIV:** CD4 <200: atypical presentations — lower lobe involvement, no cavitation, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, disseminated/miliary disease. A high index of suspicion is required.

## Diagnosis

**Sputum smear microscopy (ZN stain):** Detects acid-fast bacilli — rapid (hours), cheap, but low sensitivity (~50–70% in smear-positive pulmonary TB) and cannot distinguish species. At least three sputum samples (including an early morning specimen) should be sent.

**Mycobacterial culture:** Gold standard for diagnosis — high sensitivity, identifies species and antibiotic sensitivities. Slow (results in 2–8 weeks for MGIT liquid culture).

**Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT — e.g., GeneXpert MTB/RIF):** Rapid molecular detection of *M. tuberculosis* and rifampicin resistance within 2 hours. WHO endorses as the primary test for pulmonary TB. Increasingly standard in the UK.

**Tuberculin skin test (Mantoux) and IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay — e.g., QuantiFERON-TB Gold):** For diagnosing **latent TB infection (LTBI)**, not active disease. IGRA is preferred in the UK — more specific than Mantoux (unaffected by BCG vaccination). A positive IGRA means the person has been infected with *M. tuberculosis* at some point; it cannot distinguish latent from active disease.

**CXR and CT thorax:** Upper lobe consolidation with cavitation is the classic active pulmonary TB pattern. Hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, miliary nodules. Old TB: upper lobe fibrosis, calcified Ghon focus and Ranke complex, volume loss.

**Bronchoscopy with BAL:** If sputum cannot be obtained or smears are negative but suspicion remains high.

**Tissue biopsy:** For extrapulmonary sites — lymph node, pleural, bone biopsy. Histology shows **caseating granulomas** with central caseous necrosis, surrounded by epithelioid macrophages, Langhans giant cells, and lymphocytes.

## Management

### Active Pulmonary TB (Standard Regimen)

Treatment uses multiple drugs to prevent resistance emergence. The standard **RIPE** regimen:

| Phase | Duration | Drugs |
|-------|---------|-------|
| Intensive phase | 2 months | Rifampicin + Isoniazid + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol |
| Continuation phase | 4 months | Rifampicin + Isoniazid |
| Total duration | 6 months | — |

**CNS TB (meningitis, cerebral tuberculoma):** 12 months total; add dexamethasone (reduces mortality and neurological sequelae).

**Spinal TB:** 6–9 months; surgical decompression if cord compression.

**Directly observed therapy (DOT)** — considered for patients at risk of non-adherence or with drug resistance.

**Drug side effects to know:**

| Drug | Key Side Effects |
|------|----------------|
| Rifampicin | Orange discolouration of body fluids; powerful CYP450 inducer (reduces efficacy of OCP, warfarin, many others); hepatotoxicity |
| Isoniazid | Peripheral neuropathy (give pyridoxine/B6 prophylaxis); hepatotoxicity; lupus-like syndrome |
| Pyrazinamide | Hepatotoxicity; hyperuricaemia and gout; arthralgia |
| Ethambutol | Optic neuritis (check visual acuity and colour vision before starting; monitor monthly) |

### Latent TB Infection (LTBI)

Treat to prevent future reactivation, particularly in:
- HIV-positive individuals
- Those starting anti-TNF therapy, corticosteroids, or other immunosuppressants
- Recent contacts of active cases
- Recent arrivals from high-burden countries

Regimens: isoniazid for 6 months (standard), or rifampicin + isoniazid for 3 months (3HP), or rifampicin alone for 3–4 months.

### Infection Control and Contact Tracing

- Isolate suspected or confirmed smear-positive patients in negative-pressure rooms until deemed non-infectious (typically 2 weeks of effective treatment)
- Notify all cases to the local health protection team — TB is a notifiable disease
- Contact tracing: test all household and close contacts for LTBI and active disease

### Drug-Resistant TB

- **MDR-TB:** Resistant to at least rifampicin and isoniazid. Requires ≥18–24 months of second-line drugs (fluoroquinolones, linezolid, bedaquiline). Specialist management.
- **XDR-TB:** Additionally resistant to fluoroquinolones and ≥1 second-line injectable. Very limited treatment options.

## Complications

- Haemoptysis — from cavitation or Rasmussen aneurysm; can be massive and life-threatening
- Spontaneous pneumothorax — from subpleural cavitation
- Respiratory failure — extensive or miliary disease
- TB meningitis — high mortality and morbidity even with treatment
- Immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome (IRIS) — in HIV-positive patients starting ART, deterioration as immune function recovers and mounts a response to mycobacterial antigens

## Clinical Insight

Ethambutol causes dose-dependent optic neuritis — a reversible cause of visual loss that becomes irreversible if not detected early. Baseline visual acuity and colour vision testing before starting ethambutol, and monthly review thereafter, is not optional. Patients must be counselled to report any visual change immediately.

Rifampicin is one of the most potent inducers of hepatic CYP450 enzymes. It dramatically reduces plasma levels of many co-administered drugs — the combined oral contraceptive pill is rendered ineffective, warfarin requires dose increases, and antiretroviral drugs are significantly affected. Reconcile all concurrent medications before starting TB treatment.

A pleural effusion in a young person from a TB-endemic country, with lymphocyte predominance and negative standard culture, should be investigated for TB pleural disease. Pleural fluid ADA (adenosine deaminase) >40 U/L is highly suggestive. Standard sputum culture and Mantoux/IGRA should be performed, and pleural biopsy sent for histology and mycobacterial culture.
