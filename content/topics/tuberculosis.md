## Overview

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and remains one of the most significant infectious diseases globally — the leading cause of death from a single infectious agent worldwide. The UK sees approximately 5,000 cases annually, with the highest rates in urban areas and in people born in high-burden countries (South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Eastern Europe). The clinical challenge lies in early recognition, microbiological confirmation, appropriate treatment, and preventing transmission and drug resistance.

---

## Pathophysiology

*M. tuberculosis* is an aerobic, slow-growing, acid-fast bacillus transmitted by inhalation of respiratory droplet nuclei from a person with active pulmonary TB. Small particle aerosols (1–5 µm) remain suspended in air for hours — a single cough can release thousands of bacilli.

**Primary infection:** Inhaled bacilli reach the alveoli and are engulfed by alveolar macrophages. The bacilli survive within macrophages, evading destruction by **inhibiting phagosome-lysosome fusion** — the organism actively prevents the vesicle containing it from fusing with lysosomes. A granulomatous response forms — the **Ghon focus** (subpleural consolidation, usually mid or lower zone) together with ipsilateral hilar lymphadenopathy forms the **primary (Ghon) complex**. In 90–95% of immunocompetent individuals, cell-mediated immunity (CD4+ T-cells, TNF-α, IFN-γ) contains the infection — bacilli persist in a dormant state within granulomas (**latent TB infection, LTBI**) but do not cause active disease.

**Post-primary (reactivation) TB:** Reactivation of dormant bacilli — preferentially in the **upper lobes** (high alveolar PO₂ favours mycobacterial growth) — occurs when immunity wanes: HIV (most important — risk increases 20-fold per year with declining CD4), diabetes mellitus, corticosteroids, **anti-TNF therapy** (TNF-α is critical for granuloma formation and integrity; anti-TNF drugs dissolve existing granulomas, liberating dormant bacilli), other immunosuppressants, malnutrition, advancing age, silicosis, and CKD.

**Why anti-TNF carries specific TB risk:** Granulomas depend on TNF-α for their formation and maintenance. Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept) disrupt this, allowing reactivation of latent infection. **Screen with IGRA before starting any anti-TNF agent** — treat LTBI before commencing anti-TNF.

**Miliary TB** — haematogenous dissemination producing uniform 1–2mm nodules throughout both lung fields ("millet seed" pattern on CXR) and potentially seeding all organs. Seen in primary progressive TB (children, immunocompromised) or in reactivation with severe immunosuppression. Carries high mortality.

## Clinical Presentation

**Pulmonary TB:** The classic presentation is a subacute illness of weeks to months:
- **Productive cough** — initially dry, later mucopurulent; haemoptysis from cavitation or **Rasmussen aneurysm** rupture (pseudoaneurysm from erosion of a pulmonary artery adjacent to a cavitating lesion — can cause massive, life-threatening haemoptysis)
- **Constitutional symptoms** — fever (classically low-grade, intermittent, with drenching night sweats), weight loss, anorexia, fatigue, malaise
- CXR: upper lobe consolidation with cavitation, hilar lymphadenopathy, pleural effusion, fibrosis, volume loss

**Extrapulmonary TB (approximately 40% of UK cases):**

| Site | Presentation |
|------|-------------|
| **TB lymphadenitis** | Most common extrapulmonary site in UK. Firm, matted, non-tender cervical lymphadenopathy. May form a "collar stud" abscess (tracking through deep cervical fascia to the skin surface) |
| **Pleural TB** | Unilateral exudative pleural effusion; lymphocyte predominance; ADA >40 U/L; standard sputum cultures frequently negative |
| **TB meningitis** | Subacute meningitis over days-weeks; cranial nerve palsies (III, IV, VI, VII); high protein, low glucose, lymphocytosis in CSF; high mortality even with treatment |
| **Spinal TB (Pott's disease)** | Vertebral collapse (anterior wedging → gibbus deformity); paraspinal abscess; may cause spinal cord compression and paraplegia; MRI is investigation of choice |
| **Renal/genitourinary TB** | **Sterile pyuria** — white cells in urine with no bacterial growth on standard aerobic culture (mycobacteria do not grow on standard media); haematuria; frequency; dysuria; ureteric strictures |
| **Pericardial TB** | Constrictive pericarditis; exudative effusion; can cause cardiac tamponade |

**TB in HIV:** As CD4 count falls, TB becomes more atypical:
- CD4 200–350/µL: classic upper lobe disease, cavitation possible
- CD4 <200/µL: lower lobe involvement, no cavitation, mediastinal lymphadenopathy, miliary disease
- CD4 <50/µL: disseminated disease, extrapulmonary dominant, negative smears in 50%

## Diagnosis

**Sputum smear microscopy (Ziehl-Neelsen stain):** Detects acid-fast bacilli — rapid (hours), cheap, but only ~50–70% sensitivity in smear-positive pulmonary TB. Cannot distinguish *M. tuberculosis* from non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM). At least three sputum samples (including at least one early-morning specimen — highest bacillary load) should be sent.

**Mycobacterial culture:** Gold standard — highest sensitivity, identifies species, provides antibiotic susceptibility. Slow: 2–8 weeks for MGIT liquid culture. Essential for confirming species and directing treatment in drug resistance.

**Nucleic acid amplification testing (NAAT — GeneXpert MTB/RIF, Xpert Ultra):** Rapid molecular detection of *M. tuberculosis* and rifampicin resistance within 2 hours. WHO recommends as the primary test for pulmonary TB. Increasingly standard in UK laboratories. Sensitivity superior to smear microscopy, particularly in HIV-positive patients. A negative GeneXpert does not exclude TB — culture must still be sent.

**IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay — QuantiFERON-TB Gold, T-SPOT.TB):** For diagnosing **latent TB infection (LTBI)**, not active disease. Measures IFN-γ release by sensitised T-cells in response to *M. tuberculosis*-specific antigens (ESAT-6, CFP-10). Preferred over Mantoux in the UK — **not affected by BCG vaccination** (BCG uses different antigens). A positive IGRA means past exposure to *M. tuberculosis*; it cannot distinguish latent from active disease — clinical assessment and CXR are essential.

**Tuberculin skin test (Mantoux test):** Intradermal purified protein derivative (PPD); induration at 48–72 hours. Positive (≥10mm in immunocompetent, ≥5mm in HIV): indicates sensitisation. Limitations: false-positive with BCG; false-negative in severe immunosuppression, miliary TB, sarcoidosis, malnutrition.

**CXR and CT thorax:** Upper lobe consolidation with cavitation is the classic pattern of active pulmonary TB. Old TB: upper lobe fibrosis, calcified Ghon focus, calcified hilar nodes, bronchiectasis. Miliary TB: bilateral 1–2mm nodules throughout. CT better characterises cavitation, miliary pattern, and hilar/mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

**Bronchoscopy with BAL:** If sputum cannot be obtained (unable to expectorate) or smears are negative but suspicion remains high; also used for bronchial sampling in patients with mediastinal lymphadenopathy.

**Tissue biopsy for extrapulmonary sites:** Histology shows **caseating granulomas** — central caseous necrosis (cheese-like acellular material) surrounded by epithelioid macrophages and Langhans giant cells (multinucleate, horseshoe-shaped nuclear arrangement). **Non-caseating granulomas** suggest sarcoidosis. Send tissue for both histology AND mycobacterial culture.

## Management

### Active Pulmonary TB (Standard Regimen)

Multiple drugs are used concurrently to prevent resistance emergence — each drug alone would select resistant mutants within the large bacterial load present in a cavitating lesion.

**RIPE regimen:**

| Phase | Duration | Drugs |
|-------|---------|-------|
| Intensive phase | 2 months | **R**ifampicin + **I**soniazid + **P**yrazinamide + **E**tambutol |
| Continuation phase | 4 months | Rifampicin + Isoniazid |
| **Total** | **6 months** | — |

**Extended durations:** CNS TB (meningitis, cerebral tuberculoma) — **12 months** total; add dexamethasone 0.3–0.4mg/kg/day for 4 weeks then taper (reduces mortality and neurological sequelae). Spinal TB — **6–9 months**; surgical decompression if cord compression.

**Treatment monitoring:**
- Baseline: FBC, LFTs (rifampicin, isoniazid, and pyrazinamide all potentially hepatotoxic), renal function, visual acuity and colour vision (ethambutol)
- Repeat LFTs at 2 weeks and if any symptoms of hepatitis (nausea, vomiting, jaundice, RUQ pain)
- Stop all drugs if transaminases rise to >5× ULN or any symptoms of hepatitis; reintroduce sequentially
- Monthly visual acuity and colour vision testing while on ethambutol

**Drug side effects:**

| Drug | Key Side Effects | Management |
|------|----------------|-----------|
| **Rifampicin** | Orange discolouration of body fluids (sweat, tears, urine — warn patients); **potent CYP450 inducer** (reduces efficacy of OCP, warfarin, antiretrovirals, phenytoin, corticosteroids, many others); hepatotoxicity | Counsel on drug interactions; women on OCP need additional contraception |
| **Isoniazid** | **Peripheral neuropathy** (pyridoxine/B6 deficiency — give 10–25mg/day prophylactically); hepatotoxicity; lupus-like syndrome | Pyridoxine supplementation mandatory; check LFTs |
| **Pyrazinamide** | Hepatotoxicity (most hepatotoxic of the four); hyperuricaemia and gout; arthralgia | Check LFTs; avoid in severe gout |
| **Ethambutol** | **Optic neuritis** (dose-dependent; check visual acuity and colour vision monthly — irreversible if detected late); not renally cleared — dose-reduce in CKD | Baseline and monthly visual assessment; counsel patient to report any visual change immediately |

### Latent TB Infection (LTBI)

Treat to prevent future reactivation:

| Regimen | Duration | Notes |
|---------|---------|-------|
| Isoniazid + pyridoxine | 6 months | Standard; well-tolerated |
| Rifampicin + isoniazid | 3 months (3HR) | Shorter; equivalent efficacy; preferred for adherence |
| Rifampicin alone | 3–4 months | If isoniazid resistance suspected |

**Screen and treat LTBI before:**
- Starting anti-TNF therapy or other biologics (IGRA before every course)
- Starting immunosuppressants (transplant, high-dose steroids, chemotherapy)
- Recent contacts of smear-positive cases
- People with HIV
- Migrants from high-burden countries (as part of new entrant screening)

### HIV-TB Co-infection — Timing of ART

Starting ART too early risks **IRIS** (see below); too late leaves the patient immunosuppressed:
- CD4 <50/µL: start ART **2 weeks** after starting TB treatment
- CD4 50–500/µL: start ART **8 weeks** after starting TB treatment
- CD4 >500/µL: consider deferring ART until TB treatment completed

**Avoid rifampicin with most protease inhibitors** — CYP3A4 induction dramatically reduces PI levels. Use **rifabutin** (weaker CYP inducer) instead of rifampicin in HIV-positive patients on PIs. **Dolutegravir** (an integrase inhibitor) is less affected — now the preferred NNRTI backbone.

### Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS)

A paradoxical worsening of TB disease after starting ART, as the recovering immune system mounts an exaggerated inflammatory response to mycobacterial antigens. Occurs in ~8–43% of HIV-TB patients starting ART. Features: fever, lymphadenopathy, worsening pulmonary infiltrates, new lesions — **despite microbiological response** (cultures turning negative). Treatment: continue both TB and ART; NSAIDs for mild cases; corticosteroids for severe IRIS.

### Infection Control and Notification

- Isolate suspected or confirmed smear-positive patients in **negative-pressure rooms** until deemed non-infectious (typically after ≥2 weeks of effective treatment AND clinical improvement)
- Notify all cases to the **local health protection team** — TB is a notifiable disease in the UK
- Contact tracing: test all household and close contacts for LTBI and active disease
- **Directly observed therapy (DOT)** — for patients at risk of non-adherence, MDR-TB, or with prior treatment failure

### Drug-Resistant TB

**MDR-TB:** Resistant to at least rifampicin AND isoniazid. Requires 18–24 months of second-line therapy. Modern **BPaL regimen** (bedaquiline + pretomanid + linezolid) has produced remarkable results in XDR-TB (ZeNix/TB-PRACTECAL trials — >90% success in 6 months). Specialist management at regional MDR-TB centre.

**XDR-TB:** Additionally resistant to fluoroquinolones and ≥1 second-line injectable. Very limited treatment options; requires specialist tertiary management.

## Complications

- **Haemoptysis** — from cavitation or Rasmussen aneurysm rupture; can be life-threatening; interventional radiology (bronchial artery embolisation) may be required for massive haemoptysis
- **Spontaneous pneumothorax** — from subpleural cavitation eroding to the pleural surface
- **Respiratory failure** — extensive or miliary disease; rare with prompt treatment
- **TB meningitis** — high mortality (20–50%) and morbidity even with treatment; neurological sequelae in survivors
- **IRIS** — paradoxical worsening after ART initiation in HIV-positive patients
- **Constrictive pericarditis** — late complication of pericardial TB; pericardiectomy may be required

## Clinical Insight

Ethambutol causes dose-dependent optic neuritis — a reversible cause of visual loss that becomes irreversible if not detected early. Baseline visual acuity and colour vision testing before starting, and monthly review thereafter, is not optional. Patients must be explicitly counselled to report any visual change immediately and to return urgently if it develops. Failing to do this baseline assessment is a clinical and medicolegal error.

Rifampicin is one of the most potent inducers of hepatic CYP450 enzymes. It dramatically reduces plasma levels of many co-administered drugs — the combined oral contraceptive pill is rendered ineffective (women must use additional contraception), warfarin requires dose increases, and antiretroviral drugs are significantly affected. Reconcile all concurrent medications before starting TB treatment.

Sterile pyuria is pathognomonic of renal TB until proven otherwise. White cells in urine with no bacterial growth on standard aerobic culture — the mycobacteria will not grow on the media used for standard urine cultures. If standard urine cultures are negative but pyuria persists, send early-morning urine (three consecutive specimens) for ZN stain and mycobacterial culture, and perform renal USS/CT to look for upper urinary tract disease.
