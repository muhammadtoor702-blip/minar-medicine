# Pituitary Disorders and Calcium Metabolism

## Clinical Scenario

A 42-year-old man presents with a 3-year history of progressive headaches and recent bitemporal visual field loss. He reports erectile dysfunction and reduced libido. His shoe size has increased by two sizes over five years, his ring no longer fits, and his wife has noticed his face has changed. His hands are large with spade-like fingers. Fasting glucose is 9.2 mmol/L. IGF-1 is markedly elevated. MRI pituitary shows a 2.4 cm macroadenoma with suprasellar extension and optic chiasm compression.

## Overview

The pituitary gland — the "master gland" — regulates the thyroid, adrenal cortex, gonads, growth, and water balance through trophic hormones. Pituitary pathology produces disease through two mechanisms: **hormone excess** (from functioning adenomas) and **hormone deficiency** (from mass effect, surgery, or radiation destroying normal tissue). Disorders of calcium metabolism, while primarily parathyroid and renal in origin, are included here given their clinical overlap with endocrine presentations and shared management principles.

## Acromegaly

### Pathophysiology

Acromegaly results from chronic excess of **growth hormone (GH)**, almost always from a GH-secreting pituitary adenoma (~98%). GH excess → stimulates the liver to produce **IGF-1** (insulin-like growth factor 1) → promotes tissue growth. IGF-1 is the principal mediator of GH's anabolic effects and is used both as a diagnostic marker and for treatment monitoring.

When GH excess occurs before epiphyseal fusion (rare): **gigantism** (linear height increase). After fusion (typical adult acromegaly): somatic enlargement without increased height.

GH opposes insulin action → insulin resistance → **diabetes mellitus** develops in ~25–30% of acromegaly patients. GH also causes sodium and water retention → hypertension (~40%).

### Clinical Presentation

The disease onset is insidious — mean delay from symptom onset to diagnosis is **8–10 years**. Old photographs are the most useful diagnostic tool.

**Somatic changes:**
- Enlargement of hands (spade-like), feet (increased shoe size), jaw (prognathism), nose, lips, tongue (macroglossia)
- Coarsening of facial features
- Widening of interdental spacing
- Frontal bossing
- Increased skin thickness, oiliness, hyperhidrosis

**Mass effects from pituitary macroadenoma:**
- **Headache** (dural stretch)
- **Bitemporal hemianopia** (optic chiasm compression) — classically begins with superior temporal quadrants
- **Hypopituitarism** (compression of adjacent gonadotroph, thyrotroph, corticotroph cells) → hypogonadism (most common), hypothyroidism, secondary adrenal insufficiency

**Systemic complications:**
- Diabetes mellitus (~25–30%)
- Hypertension (~40%)
- **Cardiomyopathy** — the leading cause of death in untreated acromegaly
- Obstructive sleep apnoea (~70%)
- Arthropathy and carpal tunnel syndrome
- Colorectal polyps and cancer (2–3× increased risk — colonoscopy at diagnosis)
- Vertebral fractures (paradoxically, despite high GH — IGF-1-mediated periosteal expansion paradoxically impairs cortical bone quality)

### Diagnosis

**IGF-1** (elevated, age- and sex-adjusted): Best screening test. Remains elevated throughout the day (unlike GH which is pulsatile). A normal IGF-1 effectively excludes active acromegaly.

**Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with GH**: 75 g glucose → measure GH at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120 minutes. In normal individuals, GH suppresses to <1 µg/L. In acromegaly, **GH fails to suppress** (paradoxical rise in ~25%). Gold standard for biochemical diagnosis.

**MRI pituitary**: Identifies the adenoma and its relationship to the optic chiasm, cavernous sinuses, and surrounding structures.

**Formal visual field testing** (Humphrey perimetry) if suprasellar extension is present.

### Management

**Transsphenoidal pituitary surgery** (first-line): Remission rates ~80–85% for microadenoma, ~50% for macroadenoma. GH falls within hours of successful surgery.

**Medical therapy** (if surgery fails, incomplete resection, or pre-operative debulking):
- **Somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs)**: Octreotide LAR, lanreotide — first-line medical therapy. Suppress GH/IGF-1, may shrink tumour. Effective in ~60–70%. Side effects: gallstones, GI symptoms.
- **Pegvisomant**: GH receptor antagonist — blocks GH action at target tissues. Normalises IGF-1 in >95%. Does not reduce tumour size. Used when SRLs fail.
- **Cabergoline**: Dopamine agonist — modest efficacy in mixed GH/prolactin tumours.

**Radiotherapy**: For persistent disease after surgery and medical therapy failure. Slow effect (years).

## Prolactinoma

The most common pituitary adenoma. Excess prolactin inhibits GnRH pulsatility → secondary hypogonadism.

**Clinical:**
- **Women**: Galactorrhoea (milk production outside of pregnancy), amenorrhoea, infertility, osteoporosis (from oestrogen deficiency)
- **Men**: Erectile dysfunction, infertility, reduced libido; galactorrhoea (less common); often diagnosed late due to less obvious symptoms — typically presents as macroadenoma with mass effects
- Both: Headache, visual field defect (macroadenoma)

**Causes of hyperprolactinaemia (not just prolactinoma):**
- Pregnancy and lactation (physiological)
- Drugs — dopamine antagonists (metoclopramide, domperidone, haloperidol, risperidone, chlorpromazine) → most common cause of non-physiological hyperprolactinaemia
- Hypothyroidism (TRH stimulates prolactin)
- Stalk effect (any large pituitary or parasellar mass compresses the portal vessels carrying dopamine to the pituitary, releasing dopamine inhibition of prolactin) — prolactin <5000 mU/L suggests stalk effect rather than true prolactinoma
- Renal failure, liver disease, chest wall trauma

**Diagnosis**: Prolactin level. Beware the "hook effect" — very large prolactinomas may give a falsely normal prolactin due to antibody saturation at high concentrations; dilute the sample if a large tumour is present.

**Management**: **Dopamine agonists (cabergoline first-line, bromocriptine alternative)** — reduce prolactin to normal and shrink the tumour in >90% of patients. Surgery reserved for intolerance/resistance to dopamine agonists or acute visual deterioration. Cabergoline has cardiac valve risks at high doses (used in Parkinson's) — at endocrine doses, the risk is low but echocardiography is recommended.

## Hypopituitarism

Deficiency of one or more pituitary hormones. Causes: pituitary adenoma (or its treatment), craniopharyngioma, head trauma, pituitary apoplexy (infarction or haemorrhage into a pituitary adenoma — acute emergency: severe headache, visual loss, hypopituitarism), post-partum pituitary infarction (Sheehan's syndrome), radiation, sarcoidosis, hypophysitis.

**Hormonal deficiencies (order of loss — GH fails first)**: GH → FSH/LH → TSH → ACTH → ADH (diabetes insipidus, if posterior pituitary involved).

**Assessment**: Full pituitary hormone profile (IGF-1, LH/FSH + testosterone/oestradiol, TSH + fT4, short synacthen test or insulin tolerance test for ACTH/GH, prolactin, early morning cortisol, sodium/osmolality for ADH). MRI pituitary.

**Replacement**: Cortisol (hydrocortisone — always prioritise before thyroid replacement), levothyroxine, sex steroids, GH (in adults with documented deficiency and quality of life impairment). DDAVP for diabetes insipidus.

## Calcium Metabolism Disorders

### Primary Hyperparathyroidism (PHPT)

**Most common cause of hypercalcaemia in the outpatient setting.** Caused by: solitary parathyroid adenoma (~85%), multigland hyperplasia (~15%), parathyroid carcinoma (<1%). MEN1 and MEN2A increase risk.

**Pathophysiology**: Excess PTH → increased bone resorption (osteoclast activation) → hypercalcaemia + elevated ALP (bone turnover). PTH also promotes phosphate excretion (hypophosphataemia) and 1-alpha-hydroxylation of vitamin D (→ elevated 1,25-OH vitamin D → increased intestinal calcium absorption).

**Clinical (the "bones, stones, groans, moans" mnemonic):**
- **Bones**: Osteitis fibrosa cystica (severe — rare in modern diagnosed disease); subperiosteal bone resorption (radial aspect of middle phalanges on X-ray); pathological fractures
- **Stones**: Nephrolithiasis (calcium oxalate/phosphate) and nephrocalcinosis — risk in all PHPT
- **Groans**: Abdominal symptoms — nausea, vomiting, constipation, anorexia, peptic ulcers (PTH stimulates gastrin)
- **Moans**: Neuropsychiatric — depression, anxiety, cognitive impairment, fatigue, muscle weakness

**Often asymptomatic** (discovered incidentally on routine biochemistry).

**Biochemistry**: Elevated calcium + **elevated or inappropriately normal PTH** (this combination is diagnostic). Normal or low phosphate. Elevated 24-hour urine calcium. Elevated ALP in significant bone disease.

**Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (FHH)**: Rare mimic of PHPT. Inactivating mutations in the calcium-sensing receptor → hypercalcaemia with low urinary calcium. **Calcium:creatinine clearance ratio (CCCR) <0.01 suggests FHH** — important to exclude before parathyroid surgery. No treatment required.

**Management**: Parathyroidectomy — indicated for: symptomatic PHPT, serum calcium >0.25 mmol/L above upper limit, age <50, eGFR <60, nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis, T-score <–2.5 on DXA. Localise with **sestamibi scan** (nuclear medicine — identifies adenoma) and USS neck.

Medical management (for those not undergoing surgery): Cinacalcet (calcimimetic — activates CaSR → reduces PTH); annual calcium monitoring.

### Hypercalcaemia — Differential Diagnosis

| Cause | PTH | Key Clues |
|-------|-----|----------|
| Primary hyperparathyroidism | ↑ or normal | Outpatient; asymptomatic; chronic |
| Malignancy | ↓ | PTHrP elevated (humoral); bone mets; weight loss; acute |
| Sarcoidosis | ↓ | Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy; ACE elevated; 1,25-OH vitamin D high |
| Vitamin D toxicity | ↓ | Supplements; 25-OH vitamin D very high |
| Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia | ↑ or normal | Low urine calcium; CCCR <0.01; family history |
| Thiazide diuretics | ↓ | Drug history |
| Milk-alkali syndrome | ↓ | Excess calcium carbonate antacids |

**Hypercalcaemia of malignancy** (most common cause of hypercalcaemia in hospitalised patients):
- **Humoral hypercalcaemia** — PTHrP (PTH-related peptide) secreted by squamous cell carcinomas (lung, head/neck), renal cell carcinoma, breast → activates PTH receptor
- **Osteolytic metastases** — breast cancer, multiple myeloma
- **Lymphoma** — 1,25-OH vitamin D production

**Acute hypercalcaemia management**: IV saline 0.9% (rehydration and calciuresis — first-line). IV bisphosphonates (zoledronate or pamidronate — for malignancy-related hypercalcaemia). Denosumab for bisphosphonate-refractory. Calcitonin (rapid effect, short-lived). Prednisolone (for sarcoidosis and lymphoma — reduces 1-alpha-hydroxylation).

### Hypoparathyroidism

Most commonly iatrogenic — **post-thyroidectomy** or post-parathyroidectomy. Autoimmune hypoparathyroidism and DiGeorge syndrome (22q11 deletion) are other causes.

**Clinical**: Neuromuscular irritability from hypocalcaemia — Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation for 3 minutes above systolic), Chvostek's sign (facial twitch on tapping the facial nerve). Perioral paraesthesia, muscle cramps, tetany, seizures, prolonged QT interval.

**Biochemistry**: Low calcium, elevated phosphate, low/undetectable PTH.

**Management**: **Calcium supplements + active vitamin D (alfacalcidol or calcitriol)** — oral, lifelong. **Recombinant PTH (teriparatide or palopegteriparatide)** — licensed for chronic hypoparathyroidism when standard therapy is insufficient.

## Clinical Insight

Acromegaly kills through cardiovascular disease — the cardiomyopathy and associated hypertension and diabetes. Achieving biochemical remission (normalised IGF-1, suppressed GH on OGTT) is associated with return of cardiovascular risk to near-normal. The delay in diagnosis — averaging 8–10 years — represents years of unrecognised cardiovascular and metabolic damage. Old photographs are the most sensitive and underused diagnostic tool.

Hypercalcaemia in an outpatient with an elevated or inappropriately normal PTH = primary hyperparathyroidism until proven otherwise. Hypercalcaemia in a hospitalised or acutely unwell patient with a suppressed PTH = malignancy until proven otherwise. This simple dichotomy narrows the differential immediately and directs investigation efficiently.

Secondary adrenal insufficiency from exogenous corticosteroid use is the most common cause of adrenal insufficiency seen in clinical practice — far more common than Addison's disease. Any patient who has taken >7.5 mg prednisolone daily for more than 3 weeks has significant HPA axis suppression and cannot mount a stress response. They need steroid cover for surgery, illness, or any physiological stress — and must never have steroids abruptly withdrawn.
