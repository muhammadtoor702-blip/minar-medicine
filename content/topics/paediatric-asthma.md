## Overview

Asthma is the most common chronic disease of childhood, affecting approximately 1 in 11 children in the UK and 1 in 12 in the United States. It is characterised by reversible airflow obstruction, bronchial hyperresponsiveness, and airway inflammation. The vast majority of childhood asthma is well-controlled with inhaled therapy; the clinical challenge lies in recognising the deteriorating child, delivering appropriate acute treatment without delay, and building a long-term management plan that prevents future attacks. Acute severe asthma in a child is life-threatening — a silent chest (no wheeze audible due to severely reduced airflow) is a pre-terminal sign, not a sign of improvement, and near-fatal asthma attacks can be fatal in minutes.

## Pathophysiology

### Airway Inflammation and Remodelling

Childhood asthma is predominantly driven by type 2 (Th2) airway inflammation triggered by aeroallergen sensitisation (house dust mite, grass pollen, cat/dog dander, Alternaria moulds). The process:

1. **Sensitisation phase:** Dendritic cells present allergen peptides to naïve T cells → Th2 differentiation → IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 production → IgE class switching in B cells → allergen-specific IgE production → mast cell priming
2. **Early phase response (minutes):** Re-exposure → cross-linking of IgE on mast cells → degranulation → histamine, leukotrienes (LTC4, LTD4), prostaglandins → bronchoconstriction, mucus secretion, oedema
3. **Late phase response (4–8 hours):** Eosinophil, T-cell, and basophil recruitment → amplified inflammation → persistent bronchoconstriction + mucus plugging
4. **Airway remodelling (long-term, uncontrolled asthma):** Subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy, goblet cell metaplasia → fixed airflow obstruction, reduced lung function

### Acute Attack Physiology

During an acute attack: bronchoconstriction + mucosal oedema + mucus plugging → increased airway resistance → **air trapping** (incomplete expiration before next breath → progressive hyperinflation). Consequences:
- Increased work of breathing (accessory muscle use, recession)
- Flattened diaphragm (inefficient inspiratory mechanics)
- Ventilation-perfusion mismatch → hypoxaemia (low SpO₂)
- Respiratory alkalosis initially (hyperventilation), then **respiratory acidosis** as the child tires (a rising PaCO₂ is an ominous sign of fatigue — the asthmatic child normally has a low PaCO₂; normal or high PaCO₂ = impending respiratory failure)

**The silent chest paradox:** When airways are so severely obstructed that airflow is minimal, the wheeze disappears — there is insufficient airflow to generate the sound. A child who was wheezing loudly and is now "quieter" may be dramatically worse, not better.

## Clinical Presentation

### Assessment of Severity (BTS/SIGN 2019 — modified for age <5 and ≥5)

**Children ≥5 years:**

| Feature | Moderate | Severe | Life-Threatening |
|---------|---------|--------|-----------------|
| SpO₂ | ≥92% | <92% | <92% |
| PEFR | 50–75% best/predicted | 33–50% | <33% |
| Speech | Sentences | Words/phrases | Cannot speak |
| RR | Mild increase | Marked increase | >30/min |
| HR | <120 | 120–140 | >140 |
| Recession | None/mild | Moderate | Severe/silent chest |
| Mental status | Normal | Agitated | Confused/drowsy |

**Life-threatening features (any one = life-threatening):**
- SpO₂ <92% despite supplemental O₂
- Silent chest
- PEFR <33% predicted
- Cyanosis
- Exhaustion, poor respiratory effort
- Altered consciousness
- Hypotension

**Children <5 years:** PEFR measurement is not possible. Rely on:
- SpO₂, work of breathing (recession, nasal flaring), feeding ability, mental state
- Wheeze + recession + SpO₂ <92% = severe

### Symptoms and Triggers

**Typical symptoms:** Episodic wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness, cough (particularly nocturnal and early morning — circadian variation in airway tone). Symptoms triggered by or worsening with: viral infections (most common trigger in children), exercise, cold air, allergen exposure, strong emotions, smoke/pollution.

**Differential diagnosis in children:**
- **Viral-induced wheeze (preschool wheeze):** In children <5, wheeze during viral infections without interval symptoms; many do not have "true" asthma; responds to salbutamol; often remits by age 6. Do not diagnose "asthma" in children under 5 without clear evidence of reversible airflow obstruction.
- **Foreign body aspiration:** Sudden onset choking + unilateral fixed wheeze + chest X-ray showing unilateral air trapping
- **Tracheomalacia/laryngomalacia:** Structural airway abnormality; stridor more prominent than wheeze
- **Cystic fibrosis:** Recurrent respiratory infections + wheeze + failure to thrive; sweat chloride >60 mEq/L

## Diagnosis

**Diagnosis in children ≥5:** Combination of characteristic history (episodic wheeze, nocturnal cough, triggers) + objective evidence of variable airflow obstruction:
- **Spirometry:** FEV₁/FVC ratio <70% + significant bronchodilator reversibility (≥12% and ≥200 mL increase in FEV₁ after salbutamol)
- **Peak flow variability:** >20% diurnal variation over 2 weeks (morning/evening diary)
- **Bronchial challenge test** (methacholine or exercise): hyper-responsiveness

**Children <5:** No objective test reliably diagnoses asthma; diagnosis is clinical + therapeutic trial of inhaled salbutamol.

**Acute attack investigations:**
- SpO₂ (continuous monitoring)
- Blood gas (VBG or ABG): if severe/life-threatening — PaCO₂ >45 mmHg = hypoventilation = near-fatal
- CXR: only if first presentation or suspected complication (pneumothorax, pneumonia — infections trigger attacks)
- FBC, CRP: if infective trigger suspected

## Management

### Acute Attack — Stepwise Approach

**Step 1 — Immediate (all severe attacks):**
- Sit upright (reduces work of breathing)
- **Supplemental O₂:** High-flow via non-rebreathe mask; target SpO₂ ≥94%
- **Salbutamol (beta-2 agonist):** The cornerstone of acute treatment
  - **Spacer and pMDI (preferred for mild-moderate):** 10 puffs of 100 mcg via spacer, one puff at a time, 30 seconds between puffs = equivalent to nebulised salbutamol in mild-moderate attacks (NAEPP/BTS)
  - **Nebulised salbutamol (severe/life-threatening):** 2.5 mg (<5 years) or 5 mg (≥5 years) in 2.5–3 mL 0.9% NaCl; driven by oxygen (not air — the patient is hypoxic); repeat every 20–30 minutes in continuous fashion for severe attacks

**Step 2 — Add ipratropium bromide (moderate-severe):**
- **Nebulised ipratropium 0.25 mg (<12 years) or 0.5 mg (≥12 years)** combined with salbutamol in the nebuliser — anticholinergic bronchodilator; additive effect to salbutamol in acute attacks; give every 20 minutes × 3 doses (first hour), then 4–6 hourly

**Step 3 — Corticosteroids:**
- **Oral prednisolone:** 1–2 mg/kg/day (maximum 40 mg) for 3–5 days — reduces airway inflammation, prevents late-phase response, reduces hospitalisation and relapse. Oral is as effective as IV in moderate attacks. Start early — onset of action 4–6 hours.
- **IV hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg (max 100 mg) 6-hourly:** If unable to swallow, persistent vomiting, or life-threatening attack

**Step 4 — Severe/life-threatening — additional treatments:**

**Magnesium sulphate IV (for severe attack not responding to salbutamol/ipratropium/steroids):**
- **IV MgSO₄ 40–50 mg/kg (max 2 g) over 20 minutes** — bronchodilator via calcium antagonism in smooth muscle; MAGNETIC trial confirmed benefit in children aged 2–16 with acute severe asthma (SpO₂ <92%); reduces hospitalisation by 30% compared to placebo
- Nebulised isotonic MgSO₄ (338 mg/4 mL) as adjunct — less evidence but some centres use routinely

**IV salbutamol (for near-fatal attacks unresponsive to nebulised therapy):**
- **IV salbutamol 5 mcg/kg over 5 minutes** loading, then infusion 1–5 mcg/kg/min — bypasses airway obstruction; requires cardiac monitoring (arrhythmia, hypokalaemia)
- Potassium supplementation required — high-dose salbutamol causes hypokalaemia (drives K⁺ into cells via beta-2 → Na-K-ATPase)

**Aminophylline (PICU only):**
- IV aminophylline: loading dose 5 mg/kg over 20 minutes (if not on theophylline), then infusion 1 mg/kg/h
- Monitor levels: target serum theophylline 10–20 mg/L; narrow therapeutic index (nausea, arrhythmia, seizures at toxic levels)
- Used as last resort in ICU for severe intractable asthma

**PICU/intubation:**
- Intubation in asthma is a last resort — positive pressure ventilation in a severely obstructed airway can cause dynamic hyperinflation, tension pneumothorax, and cardiovascular collapse
- If intubation required: ketamine (bronchodilator) preferred induction agent; use long expiratory time (I:E ratio 1:3 to 1:4), low respiratory rate; tolerate mild hypercapnia (permissive hypercapnia strategy)

### Chronic Management — BTS/SIGN Stepwise Approach

**SABA reliever (all steps):** Salbutamol via spacer/pMDI PRN — used as first-line reliever at all steps. Increasing use = poor control → step up.

| Step | Treatment |
|------|-----------|
| 1 — Mild intermittent | SABA PRN only |
| 2 — Mild persistent | Low-dose ICS (beclomethasone 100–200 mcg/day; fluticasone 50–100 mcg/day) |
| 3 — Moderate persistent (≥5 yrs) | Low-dose ICS + LABA (salmeterol or formoterol — **LABA never without ICS in children**) OR medium-dose ICS |
| 4 — Severe (≥5 yrs) | Medium-dose ICS + LABA; add LTRA (montelukast) |
| 5 — Uncontrolled on step 4 | Refer to specialist; consider oral prednisolone, anti-IgE (omalizumab), biologics (dupilumab, mepolizumab) |

**Inhaler technique and adherence:** The most common cause of inadequately controlled asthma. In children under 5: spacer with mask required. In older children: spacer + pMDI (preferred over dry powder inhaler until age 8–10). Check technique at every review — poor inhaler technique accounts for a large proportion of "treatment failures."

**Written Asthma Action Plan (WAAP):** Every asthmatic child should have a personalised written plan specifying: green zone (well-controlled), amber zone (early deterioration — increase SABA, start prednisolone), red zone (emergency — call 999). Plans reduce emergency attendances and hospitalisation by >50%.

## Complications

- **Near-fatal/fatal asthma:** Most preventable — risk factors: previous near-fatal attack (ventilation), psychosocial factors, brittle asthma, NSAID/aspirin sensitivity, poor adherence, overuse of SABA with no ICS
- **Status asthmaticus:** Acute severe asthma not responding to standard treatment
- **Pneumothorax/pneumomediastinum:** Air leaks from overdistended alveoli; rarely require intervention unless tension
- **Hypokalaemia:** From high-dose nebulised/IV salbutamol and systemic steroids — monitor potassium in intensive treatment
- **Growth effects of ICS:** Low-dose ICS causes no significant growth suppression (Childhood Asthma Management Program); medium-high doses cause 1–2 cm reduction in adult height — use lowest effective dose
- **Atopy triad:** Asthma + eczema + allergic rhinitis — the atopic march; rhinitis worsens asthma control; treat rhinitis (intranasal steroid) to improve asthma

## Clinical Insight

A normal or rising PaCO₂ in an asthmatic child is the most dangerous finding in paediatric emergency medicine. Children with asthma maintain hyperventilation (low PaCO₂) throughout an attack as compensation — when they start to tire, CO₂ rises. The initial rise from, say, PaCO₂ 28 mmHg to 38 mmHg (which appears "normal") represents significant respiratory decompensation in the context of an acute attack. Any PaCO₂ ≥40 mmHg in a child with acute severe asthma indicates impending respiratory failure and demands PICU involvement.

Magnesium sulphate IV is under-used in acute severe paediatric asthma. The evidence is clear (MAGNETIC trial, multiple Cochrane reviews), the treatment is simple and safe, and the indication is well-defined: SpO₂ <92% after 40 minutes of optimal beta-2 agonist therapy. Yet many A&E departments either don't use it or use it too late. It should be the third drug in every severe attack, not an afterthought.

The LABA-without-ICS prohibition in children is absolute and evidence-based. In the SMART trial (in adults), salmeterol monotherapy was associated with increased asthma mortality. LABAs have no anti-inflammatory effect; they mask worsening inflammation while underlying disease progresses. A child prescribed a LABA inhaler should always be on an ICS simultaneously — any child with a LABA prescription without concurrent ICS should be reviewed urgently.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 9-year-old, known asthmatic, 3h of wheeze, 10 puffs salbutamol no relief. Too breathless to speak in sentences, sitting forward. RR 36, HR 128, SpO₂ 90%, PEFR 32% predicted. Severe-to-life-threatening attack.

**Step 1 — Classify: life-threatening asthma:**
- PEFR 32% (<33%) = life-threatening criterion
- SpO₂ 90% (<92%) = life-threatening criterion
- Cannot speak in sentences = severe (approaching life-threatening)
- Immediate action required

**Step 2 — Oxygen + nebulised salbutamol + ipratropium:**
- High-flow O₂ via non-rebreathe mask (15 L/min), target SpO₂ ≥94%
- **Salbutamol 5 mg nebulised** (driven by O₂) + **ipratropium 0.25 mg** in same nebuliser — first dose
- Repeat every 20 minutes for first hour (3 back-to-back nebulisers)

**Step 3 — Oral/IV corticosteroids:**
- **Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg)** — if can swallow and not vomiting
- If vomiting or GCS concerns: **IV hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg** (max 100 mg)

**Step 4 — IV MgSO₄ (after 20 minutes of bronchodilator therapy with incomplete response):**
- SpO₂ remains <92%: **IV MgSO₄ 40 mg/kg (max 2 g) in 100 mL 0.9% NaCl over 20 minutes**
- Monitor BP during infusion

**Step 5 — Bloods and monitoring:**
- VBG: check PaCO₂ — if ≥40 mmHg = PICU now
- Serum potassium (high-dose salbutamol → hypokalaemia — replace if K⁺ <3.0 mEq/L)
- Continuous SpO₂, HR, RR monitoring; reassess after every nebuliser

**Step 6 — Escalation plan:**
- No improvement after 3 salbutamol/ipratropium nebulisers + MgSO₄ → contact PICU
- IV salbutamol infusion
- Consider aminophylline if PICU
- Intubation as absolute last resort

**Discharge planning (when stable):**
- PEFR ≥75% predicted + SpO₂ ≥94% + able to use inhaler + tolerating oral intake
- Written Asthma Action Plan + spacer device + prednisolone to complete course
- Step up regular ICS or review current therapy
- Follow-up with paediatric respiratory team within 48h

**Exam trigger:** *Child + known asthma + PEFR <33% + SpO₂ <92%* → life-threatening asthma. Salbutamol + ipratropium nebulisers + corticosteroids + IV MgSO₄. The distractor is continuing nebulised salbutamol alone without escalating to IV MgSO₄ or calling PICU when the patient fails to respond.
