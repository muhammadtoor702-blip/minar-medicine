## Overview

Sepsis is defined as **life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection** (Sepsis-3 definition, 2016). It is not simply infection with a systemic response — it represents the transition from a controlled, beneficial immune response to a destructive, dysregulated one causing organ dysfunction. Septic shock is sepsis with circulatory failure — a vasopressor is required to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg despite adequate fluid resuscitation, and lactate remains >18 mg/dL (>2 mmol/L). Septic shock carries a >40% in-hospital mortality.

---

## Pathophysiology

Infection → release of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) → activation of pattern recognition receptors (Toll-like receptors on innate immune cells) → massive cytokine storm (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) → systemic inflammatory cascade.

This cascade produces the haemodynamic hallmarks of septic shock:
- **Systemic vasodilation** (nitric oxide-mediated) → distributive shock → hypotension despite initially high cardiac output
- **Increased capillary permeability** → intravascular volume leaks into the extravascular space → effective hypovolaemia
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction** → impaired cellular oxygen utilisation → lactate accumulation even when oxygen delivery is adequate ("cytopathic hypoxia")
- **Microvascular thrombosis and endothelial injury** → organ ischaemia → multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)

In severe sepsis, **disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC)** may develop — widespread microvascular thrombosis consumes clotting factors and platelets, producing a paradoxical bleeding tendency. DIC in sepsis: prolonged PT/APTT, low fibrinogen, elevated D-dimer, thrombocytopenia. Treat the underlying sepsis; FFP and cryoprecipitate for active bleeding.

**Types of shock — the essential differential:**

| Type | Mechanism | CO | SVR | Peripheries | Example |
|------|-----------|----|----|------------|---------|
| **Distributive** | Vasodilation | ↑ (early) → ↓ (late) | ↓↓ | Warm, vasodilated | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic |
| **Hypovolaemic** | Volume loss | ↓ | ↑ | Cool, clammy | Haemorrhage, burns, vomiting/diarrhoea |
| **Cardiogenic** | Pump failure | ↓↓ | ↑↑ | Cool, clammy; elevated JVP; pulmonary oedema | MI, acute severe HF, arrhythmia, myocarditis |
| **Obstructive** | Mechanical obstruction to flow | ↓ | ↑ | Depends on cause | Massive PE, cardiac tamponade, tension PTX |

Distinguishing shock type matters critically — treatments differ and some interventions are directly harmful in the wrong type (aggressive IV fluids in cardiogenic shock worsens pulmonary oedema; fluid in obstructive shock is not the primary problem).

## Clinical Presentation

**qSOFA score** (quick bedside screen — ≥2 criteria suggests high risk of poor outcome, should prompt full SOFA assessment and sepsis workup):
- Altered mental status (any new confusion)
- RR ≥22/min
- Systolic BP ≤100 mmHg

**SOFA score elements — organ dysfunction in sepsis:**
- **Brain:** GCS reduction (confusion, obtundation)
- **Lung:** PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio <400 mmHg; need for respiratory support
- **Kidney:** Creatinine rising; oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/hr for >2 hours)
- **Liver:** Bilirubin >1.2 mg/dL
- **Coagulation:** Platelets <150 × 10⁹/L
- **Cardiovascular:** MAP <65 mmHg; vasopressor requirement

**Common sources of sepsis — always seek the source:**

| Source | Clinical Clues | Initial Focus Investigations |
|--------|---------------|---------------------------|
| **Pulmonary** (most common) | Cough, consolidation on CXR, focal signs | CXR, sputum culture, urinary Legionella/pneumococcal antigen |
| **Urinary** | Dysuria, loin pain, urinalysis abnormal | Urine MC&S, renal USS if obstruction suspected |
| **Abdominal/biliary** | Peritonism, guarding, RUQ pain, jaundice | CT abdomen, LFTs, lipase, surgical review |
| **Skin/soft tissue** | Cellulitis, wound infection, crepitus | Lactate (necrosis marker), urgent surgical assessment |
| **CNS** | Meningism, photophobia, purpuric rash | CT head, LP (if safe), meningococcal PCR |
| **Intravascular** | Indwelling catheter/line, IV drug use | Remove line; blood cultures from line and peripheral |
| **Endocarditis** | New murmur, embolic phenomena | Echocardiography, prolonged blood cultures |

**Necrotising fasciitis** — surgical emergency. Severe pain **out of proportion** to skin appearance, crepitus on palpation, bullae, rapidly spreading erythema, systemic toxicity. The hallmark is that the skin often looks deceptively benign while deep tissue is already necrotic. Do not wait for imaging if clinically suspected. Emergency surgical debridement within hours is the only lifesaving intervention.

## Diagnosis

**Investigations — simultaneous with resuscitation (not sequential):**
- **Blood cultures × 2 sets** from different venepuncture sites, before antibiotics — mandatory
- FBC (neutrophilia or neutropenia in severe sepsis), U&E (renal impairment), LFTs (bilirubin, transaminases), coagulation (DIC screen — PT, APTT, fibrinogen, D-dimer)
- **Lactate** — initial and serial measurements every 2 hours until <18 mg/dL (<2 mmol/L); a marker of anaerobic metabolism and tissue hypoperfusion
- ABG — metabolic acidosis (elevated anion gap from lactic acidosis), hypoxaemia, ventilatory status
- Blood glucose, serum calcium, CRP, procalcitonin
- Urinalysis and urine MC&S, CXR, ECG

**Lactate interpretation:**

| Lactate | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| <18 mg/dL (<2 mmol/L) | Normal |
| 18–36 mg/dL (2–4 mmol/L) | Elevated — begin resuscitation, investigate source |
| >36 mg/dL (>4 mmol/L) | Septic shock — independent mortality predictor; requires aggressive intervention |

Target **≥10% lactate clearance per 2 hours** as a resuscitation endpoint. Failure to clear lactate predicts poor outcome and should prompt reassessment of adequacy of source control and resuscitation.

## Management

### The Hour-1 Bundle (Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021)

Complete all five elements within **1 hour** of recognition:

1. **Measure lactate** — repeat if initial >18 mg/dL
2. **Blood cultures × 2** — before antibiotics; do not delay antibiotics >45 minutes for cultures
3. **Broad-spectrum antibiotics** — within 1 hour. Every hour of delay increases mortality by approximately 7%
4. **IV crystalloid 30 mL/kg** — if hypotensive (MAP <65 mmHg) or lactate ≥36 mg/dL. Reassess after each bolus with passive leg raise or clinical assessment
5. **Vasopressors** — if MAP <65 mmHg persists after initial fluid resuscitation

### Antibiotic Selection

Cover the most likely source with broad-spectrum agents, then **de-escalate** as soon as cultures and sensitivities are available — antimicrobial stewardship reduces resistance and adverse effects:

| Source | Empirical Regimen |
|--------|------------------|
| Community-acquired, unknown source | Piperacillin-tazobactam (Tazocin) IV ± gentamicin |
| Pneumonia (severe CAP) | Co-amoxiclav IV + clarithromycin IV; add levofloxacin if Legionella suspected |
| Urosepsis | Co-amoxiclav IV; meropenem if ESBL-producing organism risk |
| Abdominal sepsis | Piperacillin-tazobactam IV or meropenem IV |
| Neutropenic sepsis | Piperacillin-tazobactam IV + gentamicin; add antifungal (caspofungin) if >5 days of fever |
| Suspected meningococcal/bacterial meningitis | Ceftriaxone 2g IV **immediately**; add dexamethasone 0.15mg/kg QDS for 4 days |

**Source control** is as important as antibiotics — drain infected fluid collections (abscess, empyema, biliary obstruction), debride necrotic tissue (necrotising fasciitis), remove infected foreign bodies (lines, prostheses) as rapidly as possible.

### Vasopressors

Indicated when MAP <65 mmHg persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation:

- **Noradrenaline (norepinephrine)** — first-line vasopressor. Alpha-1 agonist → systemic vasoconstriction → raises SVR and MAP. Target MAP ≥65 mmHg. SOAP-II trial confirmed noradrenaline is superior to dopamine — dopamine caused significantly more tachyarrhythmias and was associated with higher mortality in cardiogenic shock subgroup. **Dopamine has no role as a first-line vasopressor in septic shock.**
- **Vasopressin** (0.03–0.04 units/min) — add as a second agent if high-dose noradrenaline required (>0.25–0.5 mcg/kg/min); reduces noradrenaline requirements and may have organ-protective effects
- **Dobutamine** — add to noradrenaline if myocardial dysfunction (low CO despite adequate filling pressures); not a vasopressor — pure inotrope; lowers SVR

### Corticosteroids

IV **hydrocortisone 200mg/day** (as 50mg 6-hourly or continuous infusion) for patients in **refractory septic shock** — defined as requiring escalating vasopressor doses despite adequate fluid resuscitation. ADRENAL and APROCCHSS trials: steroids reduce vasopressor duration and time to shock reversal, but a mortality benefit is seen primarily in the most severe patients. Fludrocortisone (50 mcg OD PO) may be added (APROCCHSS trial protocol).

### Fluid Resuscitation

- Initial resuscitation: 30 mL/kg IV crystalloid (balanced crystalloid — Hartmann's/PlasmaLyte preferred over 0.9% NaCl which causes hyperchloraemic acidosis in large volumes)
- Reassess after each bolus — passive leg raise test (raise legs 45° for 1 minute; if stroke volume increases ≥10%, patient is fluid-responsive); pulse pressure variation in mechanically ventilated patients
- **Avoid fluid overload** — worsens pulmonary oedema, ARDS, abdominal compartment syndrome, and is independently associated with increased mortality (FEAST trial)
- Albumin 4–5% may be added in patients requiring very large volumes of crystalloid

### Management of Specific Shock Types

**Anaphylactic shock:** Adrenaline (epinephrine) 0.5mg IM (0.5 mL of 1:1000) — first and most important drug; repeat every 5 minutes if no improvement. Antihistamines and corticosteroids are adjunctive. Aggressive IV fluids. Remove trigger.

**Obstructive shock — tension pneumothorax:** Clinical diagnosis; immediate needle decompression 2nd ICS MCL then chest drain — do not wait for CXR.

**Obstructive shock — cardiac tamponade:** Beck's triad (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds); pulsus paradoxus; ECG shows electrical alternans and low voltage; echo confirms pericardial fluid. Emergency pericardiocentesis.

**Cardiogenic shock:** Inotropic support (dobutamine, levosimendan); treat cause (primary PCI for cardiogenic shock from STEMI); mechanical circulatory support (intra-aortic balloon pump, Impella) in refractory cases. Avoid excessive IV fluids — these patients are preload-adequate, not deficient.

### Organ Support

- **Lung-protective ventilation** if ARDS develops: tidal volume 6 mL/kg ideal body weight, PEEP ≥5 cmH₂O, plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O, permissive hypercapnia (PaCO₂ up to 60 mmHg tolerated)
- **Prone positioning** for severe ARDS (P/F ratio <150 mmHg) — reduces mortality (PROSEVA trial: 28-day mortality 16% vs 33%)
- **Renal replacement therapy** (CVVHDF or CVVH) — for AKI with fluid overload, hyperkalaemia, acidosis, or uraemia unresponsive to medical management
- **Glucose control** — target 108–180 mg/dL; avoid hypoglycaemia (NICE-SUGAR trial: intensive glucose control increased mortality); monitor glucose 1–2 hourly on insulin infusion
- VTE prophylaxis (LMWH), stress ulcer prophylaxis (PPI), early enteral nutrition

## Complications

- **Multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS)** — AKI, hepatic failure, ARDS, DIC; number of failing organs predicts mortality
- **ARDS** — PaO₂/FiO₂ <300 mmHg with bilateral infiltrates not explained by cardiac failure; ~30–40% mortality in severe ARDS; lung-protective ventilation reduces mortality
- **ICU-acquired weakness** — profound neuromuscular deconditioning from immobility, corticosteroids, and neuromuscular blockade; early physiotherapy is essential
- **Post-intensive care syndrome (PICS)** — cognitive impairment, PTSD, and physical deconditioning after ICU discharge; affects >50% of survivors; long-term rehabilitation required

## Clinical Insight

Blood cultures before antibiotics is an absolute principle — but it must never delay antibiotics. The two actions should be near-simultaneous. Draw cultures from two different sites, do not delay beyond 45 minutes, and start empirical antibiotics based on the most likely source. Every hour of delay in appropriate antibiotic therapy increases mortality by approximately 7%. The cultures are for de-escalation and stewardship; the antibiotics are for survival.

Noradrenaline, not dopamine, is the vasopressor of choice in septic shock. Dopamine caused significantly more arrhythmias and was associated with worse outcomes in the SOAP-II trial. There is no longer a clinical role for dopamine as a first-line vasopressor in septic shock.

Lactate is not just about hypoperfusion — it is a marker of cellular dysfunction in sepsis even when macrocirculatory parameters appear normal. A high lactate in a patient who looks "not that sick" (warm, well-perfused, reasonably maintained BP) is a warning sign. Cytopathic hypoxia means the cells cannot utilise delivered oxygen despite adequate delivery. Serial lactate clearance is a more reliable resuscitation endpoint than any single haemodynamic parameter.
