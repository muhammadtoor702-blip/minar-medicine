## Overview

Vertigo — the illusory sensation of self or environmental movement — is one of the most common presenting complaints in both primary care and A&E, accounting for approximately 4% of all emergency department presentations. The critical distinction in any vertiginous patient is between **peripheral vestibular disorder** (inner ear or vestibular nerve — benign, often self-limiting) and **central vestibular pathology** (brainstem or cerebellum — potentially life-threatening, may mimic benign peripheral conditions). BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo) is the most common cause of vertigo overall and is diagnosed and treated at the bedside. Vestibular neuritis is the most common cause of acute prolonged vertigo. Ménière's disease is the classic triad of episodic vertigo + hearing loss + tinnitus. Missing a posterior circulation stroke presenting as vertigo remains a significant cause of preventable harm in A&E.

## Pathophysiology

### Peripheral Vestibular Anatomy

The vestibular labyrinth comprises:
- **Three semicircular canals** (anterior, posterior, lateral — mutually perpendicular): Detect angular acceleration via endolymph movement → deflection of hair cell cilia in the ampulla
- **Otolith organs** (utricle and saccule): Detect linear acceleration and static head tilt; contain otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals embedded in a gelatinous membrane)

The peripheral vestibular system provides tonic input to brainstem vestibular nuclei. Asymmetric input → nystagmus (corrective eye movement to compensate for perceived rotation) and balance disturbance.

### BPPV — Canalith Repositioning Mechanism

The most common cause of vertigo (~30% of all cases). Otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) detach from the utricular macula and enter a semicircular canal (posterior canal in 85–90% of cases). Head movement in the plane of the affected canal → canalith movement → endolymph displacement → inappropriate cupula deflection → burst of vestibular input → vertigo and nystagmus.

**Why it's positional:** Only occurs when the affected canal is moved into the orientation that allows gravity to act on the displaced canalith. Specific head positions (rolling to affected side, looking up, bending forward) trigger brief (5–30 second) intense vertigo with characteristic nystagmus.

**Canal variant:**
- Posterior canal (85–90%): Triggered by lying to affected side; nystagmus is **upbeat + torsional toward affected ear** on Dix-Hallpike
- Horizontal canal (10%): Triggered by rolling in bed; **direction-changing geotropic (beating toward ground) or apogeotropic nystagmus** on supine roll test

### Vestibular Neuritis

Sudden unilateral loss of vestibular function, most commonly from viral or parainfectious inflammation of the vestibular nerve (typically superior division). The sudden tonic imbalance (one side loses input → contralateral side dominant) → intense spinning vertigo, nausea/vomiting, inability to walk — for days to weeks. **No hearing loss or tinnitus** (cochlear nerve not affected — this is the key distinguishing feature from labyrinthitis, which involves both).

**Central compensation:** Brainstem and cerebellum progressively compensate for the tonic imbalance over days to weeks → symptoms gradually resolve. Vestibular exercises accelerate this compensation.

### Ménière's Disease

Endolymphatic hydrops — pathological distension of the endolymphatic compartment of the labyrinth. Aetiology unclear; proposed mechanisms include: endolymph overproduction, impaired endolymphatic sac resorption, sodium/fluid dysregulation. Distension → episodic rupture of Reissner's membrane → mixing of endolymph and perilymph → hair cell dysfunction → acute attack.

**Classic episodic triad:**
1. Episodic rotational vertigo (20 min–12 hours)
2. Fluctuating low-frequency sensorineural hearing loss
3. Tinnitus (low-pitched, roaring) and aural fullness

Attacks eventually lead to permanent SNHL and burnt-out vestibular function.

## Clinical Presentation

### The HINTS Examination — Peripheral vs Central

The **HINTS exam** (Head Impulse, Nystagmus, Test of Skew) is a bedside tool for distinguishing posterior circulation stroke from peripheral vestibular disorder in patients with acute prolonged vertigo. In experienced hands, it is more sensitive for posterior fossa stroke than early MRI.

**H — Head Impulse Test (HIT):**
Examiner rapidly rotates patient's head ~20° while patient fixates on examiner's nose:
- **Positive HIT (corrective saccade present):** After rapid head thrust, eyes lag behind head → visible catch-up saccade to refixate. Indicates peripheral pathology (reduced vestibulo-ocular reflex gain from affected labyrinth) → **reassuring — peripheral cause likely**
- **Negative HIT (no corrective saccade):** Eyes stay on target throughout head thrust. Indicates intact brainstem VOR → **concerning for central pathology (stroke, cerebellar lesion)**

**N — Nystagmus pattern:**
- **Unidirectional nystagmus (beating in one direction regardless of gaze direction):** Peripheral (e.g., beating left in all positions in left vestibular neuritis) — **benign pattern**
- **Direction-changing nystagmus (gaze-evoked — direction changes with gaze direction):** Central → **concerning for brainstem/cerebellar lesion**
- **Purely vertical nystagmus (up or downbeat):** Always central

**T — Test of Skew (Cover-uncover test):**
Cover one eye, then uncover:
- **Vertical corrective movement (skew deviation):** Brainstem interruption of vertical vestibulo-ocular pathways → **central — concerning**
- **No vertical movement:** Peripheral

**HINTS interpretation:**
- **Benign HINTS (peripheral):** Positive HIT + unidirectional nystagmus + no skew → peripheral vestibular disorder
- **Concerning HINTS (central):** Negative HIT OR direction-changing nystagmus OR skew deviation → **posterior circulation stroke until proven otherwise → urgent MRI brain**

### BPPV — Dix-Hallpike Manoeuvre

Patient seated at the end of the bed, head rotated 45° to the tested side → rapidly lay supine with head hanging ~30° below horizontal. Wait 30 seconds.

**Positive result (posterior canal BPPV):**
- **Upbeat-torsional nystagmus** (fast phase upward + torsional toward lower ear) — typically after 5–20s latency
- Nystagmus fatigues (disappears) within 30–60 seconds
- Reversal of nystagmus on sitting up
- **Vertigo accompanies nystagmus** — if nystagmus without vertigo, question validity

**Features distinguishing BPPV from central positional nystagmus:**
- BPPV: latency (5–20s); fatigable; torsional component; reversed on sitting
- Central: no latency; non-fatigable; purely vertical or direction-changing; no reversal

### Ménière's Disease — Diagnostic Criteria

AAO-HNS criteria (2015):
1. Two or more spontaneous episodic vertigo attacks of 20 min–12 hours
2. Audiometrically documented low-to-mid frequency SNHL in the affected ear on at least one occasion before, during, or after a vertigo episode
3. Fluctuating aural symptoms (hearing loss, tinnitus, or fullness) in the affected ear
4. Not better accounted for by another vestibular diagnosis

## Diagnosis

### Investigations

**Audiogram (PTA):** Low-frequency SNHL in Ménière's; normal in BPPV and vestibular neuritis; asymmetric SNHL in acoustic neuroma.

**Tympanometry:** Excludes middle ear pathology contributing to vertigo.

**MRI brain with contrast (DWI sequences):** Indicated when HINTS exam is concerning for central pathology, when symptoms are atypical for peripheral disorder, when headache accompanies vertigo, or when there are additional neurological signs. **DWI-MRI may be falsely negative in early posterior fossa stroke (<24–48h)** — if clinical suspicion high, MRI can be repeated or patient observed.

**MRI IAM:** For suspected acoustic neuroma (unilateral SNHL + tinnitus).

**ECG and cardiac monitoring:** Orthostatic hypotension and cardiac arrhythmias can cause pre-syncopal "dizziness" — distinguish from true vertigo (spinning sensation).

**Electronystagmography (ENG) / Videonystagmography (VNG):** Records eye movements during caloric testing and positional manoeuvres. Caloric testing: warm/cold water or air instillation into ear canal → thermal current in lateral semicircular canal → nystagmus. Canal paresis = reduced response on affected side. Used in complex or atypical cases.

**Caloric testing:** Mnemonic COWS (Cold-Opposite, Warm-Same): Cold water → nystagmus to opposite side; warm → nystagmus to same side. Canal paresis (reduced caloric response) indicates unilateral peripheral vestibular hypofunction.

## Management

### BPPV — Canalith Repositioning

**Epley manoeuvre (posterior canal BPPV):** Highly effective (80–90% resolution in single treatment):
1. Start in position that provoked nystagmus (Dix-Hallpike to affected side)
2. Rotate head 90° to opposite side (still supine, head now 45° to unaffected side)
3. Roll body and head to face down (head now 90° further, facing floor)
4. Sit up slowly with chin tucked down

Repeat if nystagmus still present. Patient advised to avoid lying flat for 48h (controversial but widely practiced).

**Brandt-Daroff exercises:** Self-administered positional exercises for patients who cannot access clinic; less effective than Epley but useful if unable to attend.

**Horizontal canal BPPV — Barbecue roll (360° rotation):** Series of sequential 90° head rotations to ipsilateral then contralateral sides to roll the canalith out of the horizontal canal.

### Vestibular Neuritis

**Acute phase (first 72h):**
- **Vestibular sedatives/antiemetics:** Prochlorperazine buccal (Buccastem) 3 mg BD; cyclizine 50 mg TID; ondansetron — short-term symptomatic relief
- **Oral prednisolone:** 100 mg/day reducing over 3 weeks (Strupp trial) — accelerates vestibular function recovery; improves long-term outcome
- IV fluids and IV antiemetics if unable to tolerate oral intake

**Recovery phase:**
- **Stop vestibular suppressants after 72h** — prolonged use impairs central compensation; this is a critical teaching point
- **Vestibular rehabilitation exercises (Cawthorne-Cooksey, brand-Daroff-like gaze stability exercises):** Essential for promoting CNS compensation — proven to accelerate recovery
- Most patients recover within 6–8 weeks; some have persistent imbalance

### Ménière's Disease

**Acute attack:** Vestibular sedatives (prochlorperazine), antiemetics; rest in quiet dark environment.

**Long-term — reducing attack frequency:**
- **Low-salt diet** (<1,500 mg sodium/day): Reduces endolymphatic pressure fluctuations — standard first-line advice
- **Betahistine** 16 mg TID (or 24 mg BD): Histamine analogue; improves cochlear blood flow; reduces endolymphatic pressure; reduces attack frequency in RCTs; widely used
- **Thiazide diuretic** (bendroflumethiazide): Reduces endolymph production
- **Intratympanic dexamethasone:** Reduces attack frequency; preserves hearing; preferred when hearing preservation important
- **Intratympanic gentamicin** (chemical labyrinthectomy): Destroys vestibular hair cells on affected side → eliminates attacks; significant risk of SNHL (~20–30%); reserved for refractory disease when hearing is already severely affected
- **Vestibular nerve section:** Surgical; eliminates vertigo attacks while preserving hearing; reserved for refractory cases

### Posterior Fossa Stroke (Central Vertigo)

- Urgent CT head: may be normal initially (posterior fossa poorly visualised on CT)
- **MRI brain with DWI:** Gold standard — identifies posterior circulation infarction
- If cerebellar haemorrhage: neurosurgical assessment (large haematomas → obstructive hydrocephalus + herniation → emergency evacuation)
- If ischaemic stroke: antiplatelet therapy; thrombolysis/thrombectomy if within window; secondary prevention

## Complications

- **BPPV:** Usually resolves with Epley; recurrence rate ~30–50% at 1 year; chronic BPPV may indicate underlying otolith pathology (head trauma, Ménière's, osteoporosis → vitamin D supplementation may reduce recurrence)
- **Vestibular neuritis:** Chronic vestibular hypofunction and imbalance in 10–20% despite rehabilitation; post-BPPV more common after vestibular neuritis
- **Ménière's disease:** Progressive bilateral involvement in 30–50% over 15 years; profound bilateral SNHL ultimately; psychological impact (unpredictable attacks → anxiety, agoraphobia, social isolation)
- **Missed posterior fossa stroke:** AICA stroke mimics peripheral labyrinthitis (hearing loss + vertigo — AICA supplies the inner ear + cerebellum); PICA stroke (Wallenberg syndrome) mimics peripheral vertigo; mortality and morbidity from missed cerebellar stroke are severe

## Clinical Insight

The HINTS examination has transformed the approach to acute vestibular syndrome in emergency medicine. Before HINTS, the reflex was "CT head" — but CT has approximately 0% sensitivity for posterior fossa ischaemic stroke in the first 24–48 hours due to bone artefact and the inherent limitations of CT in the posterior fossa. A properly performed HINTS examination (head impulse + nystagmus type + skew) is more sensitive than early MRI for posterior fossa stroke in patients with acute vestibular syndrome. The critical finding is the "negative HIT" — if the head impulse test shows no corrective saccade in a patient with acute vertigo and nystagmus, the VOR is intact and the origin is central, not peripheral. This should trigger urgent MRI, not discharge.

Vestibular suppressants (prochlorperazine, betahistine for the acute phase) are one of the most over-prescribed classes of drugs in primary care. The paradox is that while they are symptomatically useful in the acute phase (first 24–72h), they actively impair the central compensation process that produces recovery from vestibular neuritis. The brain needs vestibular mismatch signals to recalibrate — suppress them for weeks with prochlorperazine and you have a patient with chronic imbalance who relies on medication, instead of a patient who has undergone rapid compensation through exercises. The rule is: use suppressants for acute distress, stop at 72h, start vestibular rehabilitation.

The Epley manoeuvre is one of the most satisfying procedures in all of medicine — a patient arrives unable to turn their head without violent vertigo, and after 5 minutes of head positioning, 80–90% have resolution. The mechanism (repositioning canalith from posterior semicircular canal to utricle where it can no longer deflect the cupula) is a triumph of applied anatomy. The common error is performing it on the wrong side — always confirm the affected side by Dix-Hallpike before performing Epley.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 52F, sudden-onset severe vertigo from sleep, no hearing loss or tinnitus, severe nausea/vomiting, unsteady, constant horizontal nystagmus (left-beating in all gaze directions), corrective saccade on rightward head thrust (positive HIT to the right).

**Step 1 — HINTS assessment:**
- **Head Impulse Test:** Corrective saccade on thrust to the RIGHT → positive HIT on right → peripheral vestibular hypofunction on the RIGHT side
- **Nystagmus:** Unidirectional (left-beating in all gaze directions) → peripheral pattern
- **Skew:** Examine carefully — if no vertical skew → benign HINTS
- **Conclusion: HINTS consistent with RIGHT-sided peripheral vestibular disorder**

**Step 2 — Diagnosis:**
- Acute prolonged vertigo (12h), no hearing loss/tinnitus, positive HIT (peripheral pattern), unidirectional nystagmus → **RIGHT VESTIBULAR NEURITIS**

**Step 3 — Immediate management:**
- IV/IM prochlorperazine 12.5 mg or ondansetron 4 mg for acute vomiting
- IV fluids (dehydrated from vomiting)
- **Oral prednisolone 100 mg reducing over 3 weeks** — improves vestibular recovery
- Reassure: most common cause of this presentation; not a stroke; full recovery expected

**Step 4 — Discharge planning:**
- Prochlorperazine buccal (Buccastem) for home — maximum 72h use, then STOP
- **Vestibular rehabilitation exercises** — prescribe Cawthorne-Cooksey exercises; follow-up with physiotherapy
- Advise: symptoms will improve over 6–8 weeks; avoid driving until walking confidently without imbalance
- Return if: new hearing loss/tinnitus, new neurological symptoms, worsening despite 1 week

**Step 5 — If HINTS were concerning:**
- Negative HIT (no corrective saccade) OR direction-changing nystagmus OR vertical skew → **urgent MRI brain with DWI** → exclude posterior fossa stroke

**Exam trigger:** *HINTS examination in acute vertigo* — positive HIT + unidirectional nystagmus + no skew = peripheral (safe to discharge with treatment). Negative HIT = central (urgent MRI). The distractor is CT head — insensitive for posterior fossa stroke; HINTS is superior.
