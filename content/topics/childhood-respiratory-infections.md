## Overview

Respiratory infections are the most common reason for paediatric hospital admission in the developed world. They range from the self-limiting (mild bronchiolitis, viral croup) to the immediately life-threatening (epiglottitis, severe pertussis in young infants). The clinical challenge is not making the diagnosis — the presentations are usually characteristic — but determining which children need oxygen, airway intervention, or intensive care, and which can safely go home. The key principle is that respiratory reserve in infants is limited: a child working hard to breathe will tire, and a tired infant progresses rapidly from distress to apnoea.

---

## Pathophysiology

### Bronchiolitis

Bronchiolitis is the most common lower respiratory tract infection (LRTI) in infants. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) accounts for 70–80% of cases; other causes include rhinovirus, metapneumovirus, parainfluenza, and adenovirus. The virus infects bronchiolar epithelial cells → epithelial necrosis + oedema + excessive mucus secretion → partial airway obstruction → air trapping (wheeze on expiration as airways narrow further on expiration) + V/Q mismatch → hypoxia. The small diameter of infant airways means even modest oedema causes marked airway resistance (resistance ∝ 1/radius⁴).

### Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis)

Parainfluenza virus type 1 (most common), also RSV, influenza, and adenovirus. The virus infects the subglottic mucosa — the narrowest part of the paediatric airway, which has a different anatomy than the adult airway (the child's subglottis is the narrowest point, surrounded by the non-distensible cricoid cartilage). Mucosal oedema reduces the subglottic lumen dramatically — a 1 mm of oedema in a 4 mm diameter infant subglottis reduces cross-sectional area by 75% and increases resistance 16-fold (resistance ∝ 1/radius⁴). The characteristic barking cough and inspiratory stridor result from turbulent airflow through the narrowed subglottis.

### Epiglottitis

Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) caused the majority of cases historically. The Hib vaccine has made epiglottitis rare but not extinct — now also caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, Staphylococcus aureus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Bacterial superinfection of the epiglottis causes rapid, fulminant swelling that can occlude the entire glottic aperture within hours. Unlike croup, which is gradual and manageable, epiglottitis can progress to complete airway obstruction in minutes once the child becomes distressed.

### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

Bordetella pertussis attaches to ciliated respiratory epithelium via filamentous haemagglutinin, secretes pertussis toxin (which ADP-ribosylates Gi proteins, impairing immune cell recruitment), and produces tracheal cytotoxin (destroys ciliated epithelial cells → mucociliary clearance failure → mucus accumulation). The toxin-mediated neurological effects explain why lymphocytosis is pathognomonic — pertussis toxin prevents lymphocyte recirculation out of blood.

## Clinical Presentation

### Bronchiolitis

Peak season: October–March (RSV season in the Northern Hemisphere). Age: predominantly <2 years, most severe in infants <3 months. Progression: coryzal symptoms (1–3 days) → cough + progressive respiratory distress → wheeze + crackles.

**Clinical signs:** Tachypnoea (RR >50/min in infants), subcostal/intercostal/sternal recession, nasal flaring, widespread fine inspiratory crackles AND expiratory wheeze (both are present — this is the key clinical feature distinguishing bronchiolitis from isolated wheeze of asthma), prolonged expiration, hyperinflated chest (palpable liver displaced down by overinflated lungs), cyanosis in severe cases.

**Severity assessment for bronchiolitis:**
| Feature | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---------|------|---------|--------|
| SpO₂ | ≥95% | 92–94% | <92% |
| Feeding | >50% normal | <50% normal | Refusing or minimal |
| RR | <50 | 50-70 | >70 |
| Work of breathing | Mild recession | Moderate recession | Severe recession/grunting |

### Croup

Age: 6 months to 3 years. Peak: autumn. The classic presentation is unmistakeable: sudden onset in the early hours of the morning of a **barking seal-like cough**, **inspiratory stridor**, and **hoarse voice**, often preceded by a coryzal prodrome. Worse at night (when the child is recumbent). The child is typically not toxic, not drooling, and not in the "sniffing position."

**Westley Croup Score** (0–17): Inspiratory stridor (0–2) + recession (0–3) + air entry (0–2) + cyanosis (0–4) + level of consciousness (0–5). Score 0–2 = mild; 3–5 = moderate; ≥6 = severe. A score ≥6 suggests impending airway compromise.

### Epiglottitis

The clinical triad is drooling, dysphagia, and distress — the **"3 Ds"**. The child is **toxic-looking** (high fever 39–40°C, systemically unwell), sits upright leaning forward in the **"sniffing position"** to maximally open the airway. Muffled/hot-potato voice (not the barking cough of croup). Stridor is present but quiet — the child cannot generate turbulent flow through the nearly obstructed airway. **Drooling** is pathognomonic (cannot swallow due to pain/obstruction). No cough.

**Do not examine the oropharynx.** Forcing the mouth open in epiglottitis can precipitate acute complete laryngospasm and cardiac arrest. The diagnosis is clinical.

### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

Three clinical phases:
1. **Catarrhal** (1–2 weeks): coryza, mild fever, mild cough — clinically indistinguishable from a viral URTI, but maximally infectious
2. **Paroxysmal** (2–8 weeks): **paroxysmal coughing bouts** (10–30 rapid coughs in succession) followed by the characteristic **inspiratory whoop** (sharp inspiratory noise as air rushes past a partially obstructed glottis) and post-tussive vomiting. In infants under 6 months, there may be no whoop — instead, **apnoeic episodes** are the presenting feature, and pertussis carries significant mortality in this age group.
3. **Convalescent** (weeks to months): gradually diminishing cough — "the 100-day cough."

**FBC: lymphocytosis** (white cell count can reach 50–100 × 10⁹/L, predominantly lymphocytes) is pathognomonic of pertussis toxin-induced lymphocyte sequestration.

## Diagnosis

**Bronchiolitis:** Clinical diagnosis — investigations rarely change management. SpO₂ monitoring. CXR: hyperinflation ± atelectasis (NOT routinely indicated unless diagnosis uncertain or severe). Nasal swab for RSV (useful for infection control cohorting, not for treatment decisions).

**Croup:** Clinical diagnosis. AP neck X-ray: **"steeple sign"** (subglottic narrowing giving the appearance of a church steeple) — present in ~50%, absent X-ray does not exclude croup. Do not delay treatment for X-ray.

**Epiglottitis:** Lateral neck X-ray (only if the child is absolutely stable and accompanied by someone who can intubate): **"thumb sign"** — swollen epiglottis resembles a thumb rather than a thin sliver. But: do not attempt to obtain X-ray in an unstable child. The diagnosis is confirmed — and the airway is secured — in theatre by direct laryngoscopy under anaesthesia.

**Pertussis:** Nasopharyngeal swab for culture (best in first 3 weeks) or PCR (faster, preferred). Serology (anti-pertussis toxin IgA) useful later in disease. **FBC: leucocytosis with lymphocytosis** is highly characteristic.

## Management

### Bronchiolitis

**Supportive care only — there are no disease-modifying treatments:**
- **Supplemental oxygen:** SpO₂ target ≥92% (some centres ≥94%); high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) or heated humidified high-flow (HHHF) for moderate-severe disease — reduces work of breathing and requirement for intubation
- **Feeding support:** Nasogastric tube feeding if unable to take >50% of normal feeds (tachypnoea + coordination of sucking/swallowing/breathing is impossible at RR >60)
- **CPAP:** For severe disease or apnoea
- **Intubation and mechanical ventilation:** Reserve for refractory respiratory failure or apnoea

**Do NOT use the following — evidence of no benefit:**
- Bronchodilators (salbutamol/ipratropium): multiple RCTs show no benefit; mechanism of bronchiolitis is mucosal oedema + plugging, not smooth muscle spasm
- Nebulised adrenaline (outside ICU/high-dependency setting): some benefit in moderate disease but not justified for routine use
- Corticosteroids: multiple systematic reviews show no benefit
- Antibiotics: viral illness — do not use unless secondary bacterial infection suspected (rare)

**Palivizumab** (anti-RSV monoclonal antibody): given monthly throughout RSV season (October–March) as prophylaxis to high-risk infants — ex-preterm <29 weeks, congenital heart disease, immunocompromised, chronic lung disease of prematurity. Not a treatment — prophylaxis only.

### Croup

**Single dose oral dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg** (or 0.6 mg/kg for moderate-severe) — evidence: reduces return to healthcare and admission by ~50%. Can be given as a single oral dose even in mild croup. Onset of benefit within 2–4 hours; effect lasts 12–72 hours.

**Nebulised adrenaline** (1:1000 adrenaline, 0.4 mL/kg up to 5 mL) — for moderate-severe croup or impending airway compromise. Onset of action in minutes; effect lasts 1–2 hours. The child must be observed for 2–4 hours after nebulised adrenaline (rebound swelling can occur as vasoconstriction wears off).

**Intubation:** Only for severe, refractory croup unresponsive to nebulised adrenaline — by the most experienced person available, in a controlled environment. Use an ETT 0.5–1 mm smaller than calculated (due to subglottic oedema). Extubate when leak develops around the tube (oedema resolving) — usually 2–3 days.

**Do NOT:** Examine the throat. Distress the child. Lie the child flat.

### Epiglottitis

**This is an airway emergency — treat it as one:**
1. Do not examine the oropharynx
2. Keep the child calm and upright — any distress may precipitate complete obstruction
3. Call anaesthetics, ENT, and the senior paediatrician immediately
4. Transfer to theatre for controlled intubation by the most experienced anaesthetist available under inhalational induction (sevoflurane)
5. Once intubated and airway secured: IV ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg/day
6. Extubate when clinical improvement (usually 24–48 hours)
7. Rifampicin prophylaxis to household contacts

### Pertussis

**Azithromycin** (5 days) or clarithromycin (7 days): eliminates organism from nasopharynx within 5 days, reducing infectivity. Clinical benefit greatest if started in the catarrhal phase; minimal clinical benefit in paroxysmal phase (but still given to reduce transmission). Post-exposure prophylaxis: azithromycin to household contacts within 21 days of cough onset.

**Hospitalisation** for infants <6 months with apnoea — ICU if apnoea is severe or frequent. Feeding support. Monitor SpO₂ continuously. There is no effective treatment for the paroxysmal cough itself.

**Vaccination:** UK schedule (DTaP-IPV-Hib at 8, 12, and 16 weeks + 4-in-1 preschool booster). Maternal Tdap vaccine in each pregnancy (from 16 weeks) confers passive immunity to neonate in the most vulnerable period before primary series begins.

## Complications

- **Bronchiolitis**: apnoea (especially in ex-preterm infants and those under 6 weeks); respiratory failure requiring intubation; secondary bacterial pneumonia; post-bronchiolitis recurrent wheeze
- **Croup**: respiratory failure requiring intubation; secondary bacterial tracheitis (rare but severe — toxic appearance + purulent secretions in a child initially diagnosed as croup)
- **Epiglottitis**: complete airway obstruction → cardiac arrest; bacteraemia → septic arthritis, pericarditis, meningitis; subglottic stenosis after prolonged intubation
- **Pertussis**: death in infants <6 months from apnoea or pulmonary hypertension; pertussis encephalopathy; pneumothorax; rectal prolapse from coughing

## Clinical Insight

Bronchiolitis is one of the conditions in medicine where doctors are most tempted to treat with drugs that do nothing — and the evidence is clear that they do nothing. Bronchodilators (salbutamol) do not work because the obstruction is mucosal oedema and mucus, not smooth muscle spasm. Steroids do not work because the pathology is not eosinophilic inflammation. Antibiotics do not work because this is a viral illness. The treatment is oxygen, fluids, and time. The discipline of withholding ineffective treatment — especially under parental pressure — is one of the most important clinical skills in paediatrics.

Epiglottitis is rare in the post-Hib vaccine era, which means it is now easy to miss. Any child with the 3 Ds (drooling, dysphagia, distress) and toxic appearance should be treated as epiglottitis. The sequence that kills is: examining the throat in the ED → laryngospasm → complete airway obstruction before the anaesthetist arrives. The only safe place to examine the throat is the theatre with equipment for immediate intubation and tracheostomy at hand.

Pertussis in young infants does not look like pertussis. Infants under 3 months may not generate the inspiratory whoop — they lack the inspiratory muscle strength. Instead, they present with apnoea, cyanosis, and poor feeding. Any infant under 6 months with recurrent apnoeic episodes during winter months should have a nasopharyngeal swab for pertussis PCR. The FBC is the clue: a white cell count of 30–80 × 10⁹/L that is predominantly lymphocytes is essentially diagnostic.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 10-month-old in December, 3 days of coryza → respiratory distress. Poor feeding 24h. RR 58, SpO₂ 91%, bilateral wheeze + crackles, recession + nasal flaring.

**Step 1 — Diagnose bronchiolitis:** The clinical picture is textbook:
- RSV season (December)
- Age <12 months
- Coryzal prodrome → LRTI
- **Bilateral wheeze AND crackles** (the hallmark — bronchiolitis produces both, unlike asthma which produces wheeze predominantly)
- Recession + nasal flaring = moderate-severe work of breathing
- SpO₂ 91% = requires oxygen

**Step 2 — Assess severity and need for admission:** SpO₂ 91% (<92%) = severe by NICE bronchiolitis criteria. Poor feeding >50% normal for >24h = hospital admission indicated. This child needs admission for oxygen therapy and feeding support.

**Step 3 — Treatment — supportive only:**
- **High-flow nasal oxygen** (target SpO₂ ≥92%) — HFNO via age-appropriate nasal cannula
- If unable to maintain SpO₂ ≥92% on HFNO → escalate to CPAP
- **Nasogastric tube** for feeds (tachypnoea at RR 58 makes safe oral feeding impossible)
- Do NOT give salbutamol or steroids — no evidence of benefit, risk of adverse effects

**Step 4 — Monitor for deterioration:**
- Continuous SpO₂ monitoring
- Reassess work of breathing and feeding regularly
- If apnoea → CPAP/PICU
- If RR >70 + severe recession + SpO₂ <90% despite HFNO → CPAP or intubation

**Step 5 — Family counselling:** Most infants recover over 7–10 days without specific treatment. The parents need to understand that the absence of "medicine" does not mean nothing is being done — supportive care is the treatment. Safety-netting: return if breathing worsens at home, apnoea is witnessed, or feeding further deteriorates.

**Exam trigger:** *Infant + RSV season + wheeze + crackles + recession + SpO₂ 91%* → bronchiolitis requiring admission. The distractor is giving salbutamol or oral prednisolone — both are routinely used and both are ineffective. Another common error is giving antibiotics for viral bronchiolitis. The correct answer is: oxygen, feeds, and time.
