## Overview

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is the spectrum of clinical presentations caused by acute myocardial ischaemia, ranging from unstable angina (no myocardial necrosis) through non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI, with necrosis but no STEMI pattern on ECG) to ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI — transmural ischaemia with a time-critical coronary artery occlusion requiring emergency reperfusion). The unifying mechanism is rupture or erosion of an atherosclerotic plaque in a coronary artery, triggering thrombus formation and occlusion. In STEMI — the most time-critical presentation — every minute of vessel occlusion results in additional myocardial cell death ("time is muscle"), and the survival benefit of reperfusion therapy is directly proportional to the speed with which the infarct-related artery is reopened.

## Pathophysiology

### Plaque Rupture and Thrombosis

Atherosclerotic plaques develop in epicardial coronary arteries over decades. The "vulnerable plaque" — a lipid-rich core with a thin fibrous cap and large inflammatory infiltrate — is susceptible to rupture or erosion triggered by mechanical stress, sympathetic surges (physical/emotional exertion), or circadian variation in cortisol and catecholamine levels (peak STEMI incidence in the early morning).

**Plaque rupture sequence:**
1. Fibrous cap rupture → exposure of the lipid core and subendothelial collagen to circulating blood
2. Collagen → platelet adhesion (GP Ib–vWF interaction) → platelet activation → GP IIb/IIIa receptor expression → platelet-to-platelet cross-linking via fibrinogen → platelet thrombus (white clot)
3. Tissue factor exposure → coagulation cascade activation → fibrin generation → red fibrin clot reinforcing the platelet thrombus
4. Degrees of occlusion determine the ACS subtype:
   - **Partial occlusion + intact microcirculation** → NSTEMI (or unstable angina if no troponin rise)
   - **Complete, sustained occlusion** → STEMI (transmural ischaemia; ST elevation from injury current)

### Why STEMI is Different From NSTEMI

In STEMI, the infarct-related artery (IRA) is completely and persistently occluded. The entire territory downstream of the occlusion — without collateral supply — is critically ischaemic. Without reperfusion, the transmural wavefront of necrosis propagates from endocardium to epicardium within 4–6 hours (the "wavefront phenomenon"), converting reversibly ischaemic myocardium to irreversible scar. The goal of primary PCI is to reopen the artery before this wavefront completes.

In NSTEMI, the occlusion is subtotal, intermittent, or rapidly partially recanalised — sufficient to cause myocardial necrosis (troponin rise) but not complete transmural infarction. The ECG shows ST depression or T-wave changes, but not the persistent ST elevation of STEMI. NSTEMI is managed urgently but typically not with the same emergency reperfusion pathway as STEMI.

### Troponin — Mechanism and High-Sensitivity Assays

Troponins I and T are structural proteins of the myocardial contractile apparatus (thin filament regulatory proteins). They leak into the bloodstream from necrotic cardiomyocytes within 2–4 hours of cell death (high-sensitivity assays detect this earlier). Peak at 12–24h, remain elevated for 5–14 days. **High-sensitivity troponin T (hsTnT) or I (hsTnI):** Can detect troponin elevations far below the 99th percentile of the normal population, with CV <10%. The 0/1h or 0/2h algorithm (ESC 2020) uses the absolute value + rate of change (delta) to rule in or rule out MI rapidly.

**ESC 0/1h algorithm (hsTnT):**
- Rule out: hsTnT <5 ng/L at 0h (very low); OR hsTnT <12 ng/L at 0h + delta <3 ng/L at 1h
- Rule in: hsTnT ≥52 ng/L at 0h; OR delta ≥5 ng/L at 1h
- Observe: Neither rule-in nor rule-out criteria met — further testing at 3h or later

## Clinical Presentation

### Classic STEMI Presentation

**Symptoms:** Severe, crushing/heavy/tight central chest pain, radiation to the left arm, jaw, or interscapular area; nausea, diaphoresis, sense of doom; unrelieved by GTN.

**Atypical presentations (particularly in diabetics, elderly, women):**
- Epigastric pain (inferior STEMI mimics indigestion — inferior MI causes vagal stimulation → nausea, epigastric discomfort)
- Dyspnoea only (acute pulmonary oedema from acute LV failure)
- Syncope (Stokes-Adams attack from high-degree AV block in inferior MI)
- Painless MI: up to 30% of MI are "silent" in diabetics (autonomic neuropathy impairs pain perception)

### ECG Interpretation in ACS

**STEMI criteria:**
- ST elevation in ≥2 contiguous leads: ≥1 mm in standard limb leads; ≥2 mm in V1–V4; ≥2 mm in V5–V6 (some guides use 1.5 mm in females for V2–V3)
- New LBBB (left bundle branch block): Sgarbossa criteria or modified Sgarbossa to identify underlying MI; treat as STEMI equivalent

**Localisation from ECG to coronary artery:**

| ECG Territory | Leads | Coronary Artery | Complications |
|--------------|-------|----------------|--------------|
| Inferior | II, III, aVF | RCA (85%) or LCx | AV block, RV infarction |
| Anterior | V1–V4 | LAD (proximal) | Pump failure, VT, heart block |
| Lateral | I, aVL, V5–V6 | LCx or diagonal LAD branch | Papillary muscle rupture |
| Posterior | ST depression V1–V3 (reciprocal) | RCA/LCx | Missed if posterior leads not used |
| RV infarction | ST elevation V3R–V4R | RCA proximal | Hypotension with clear lungs |

**Right ventricular (RV) infarction:** Complicates 30–50% of inferior STEMIs. Critical to recognise because treatment is diametrically opposite to LV failure: **RV infarction requires aggressive fluid loading** (RV needs preload to maintain output); nitrates (GTN) and diuretics cause catastrophic hypotension (reduce preload → RV empties into a non-contracting ventricle). **Check right-sided leads (V3R–V4R) in all inferior STEMI** — ST elevation ≥1 mm = RV involvement.

### NSTEMI and Unstable Angina Risk Stratification — GRACE Score

**GRACE score (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events):** Calculates in-hospital and 6-month mortality based on: age, HR, systolic BP, creatinine, Killip class (heart failure severity), cardiac arrest at presentation, ST-segment changes, troponin elevation.
- GRACE score >140 = high risk → invasive angiography within 24h
- GRACE score 109–140 = intermediate → angiography within 72h
- GRACE score <109 = low risk → non-invasive assessment ± elective angiography

## Diagnosis

**12-lead ECG within 10 minutes** of presentation — the single most important immediate investigation. Diagnoses STEMI and directs reperfusion pathway. Repeat ECG if initial non-diagnostic (posterior MI, LBBB, early changes).

**High-sensitivity troponin:** Baseline (0h) on arrival + repeat at 1–2 hours. Trends (delta) as important as absolute value.

**Other bloods:** FBC, U&E, creatinine (contrast nephropathy risk for PCI), glucose (HbA1c), lipid profile, coagulation (if thrombolysis planned), BNP/NT-proBNP (LV function/heart failure severity).

**Echocardiography:** New wall motion abnormality confirms MI territory; assess LV function, pericardial effusion, valvular complications (acute MR from papillary muscle rupture).

**CXR (portable):** After acute management — not before. Pulmonary oedema, cardiomegaly.

## Management

### STEMI — Emergency Reperfusion

**Primary PCI (PPCI) — standard of care when available within 120 minutes of first medical contact:**
- **Door-to-balloon time target: <90 minutes** (first medical contact to coronary guidewire crossing)
- Optimal: <60 minutes if presenting directly to a PPCI centre
- Superior to thrombolysis in all outcomes (NEJM GUSTO trial and subsequent meta-analyses): lower mortality, fewer reinfarctions, fewer strokes, less haemorrhage
- Procedure: femoral or radial arterial access → coronary catheterisation → balloon angioplasty ± drug-eluting stent (DES) to the IRA

**Thrombolysis (fibrinolysis):** If PPCI not achievable within 120 minutes:
- **Tenecteplase** (single weight-adjusted IV bolus, most convenient) or alteplase; all are plasminogen activators that dissolve fibrin
- **Absolute contraindications:** Prior intracranial haemorrhage ever; ischaemic stroke within 3 months; structural intracranial vascular lesion; malignant intracranial tumour; significant closed-head or facial trauma within 3 months; aortic dissection; active bleeding or bleeding diathesis; severe hypertension (SBP >185 or DBP >110 mmHg uncontrolled)
- **Adjunct:** Tenecteplase + enoxaparin (ASSENT-3 trial)
- **Pharmacoinvasive strategy:** After successful thrombolysis (50% ST resolution at 90 min = reperfusion criterion) → coronary angiography ± PCI within 3–24 hours at PCI centre

**Antiplatelet therapy:**
- **Aspirin 300 mg loading (chewed)** — immediately on diagnosis, before reperfusion
- **P2Y12 receptor antagonist (DAPT):**
  - **Ticagrelor 180 mg loading** (then 90 mg BD for 12 months): preferred over clopidogrel for ACS/STEMI (PLATO trial: ticagrelor vs. clopidogrel — absolute 1.9% reduction in CV death/MI/stroke at 12 months); contraindicated if prior haemorrhagic stroke or active pathological bleeding
  - **Clopidogrel 600 mg loading** (if ticagrelor contraindicated or patient is on OAC)
  - **Prasugrel 60 mg loading** (alternative for STEMI going to PCI — faster onset than clopidogrel; contraindicated in prior stroke/TIA, age >75, weight <60 kg)

**Anticoagulation:**
- **Unfractionated heparin (UFH) 70–100 units/kg IV** at PCI — reduces thrombus propagation during procedure
- Bivalirudin: alternative for patients with heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

**Other STEMI medical therapy:**
- **GTN sublingual** (if SBP >90 mmHg) — symptomatic; no mortality benefit; avoid in RV infarction or PDE5 inhibitor use
- **Beta-blocker** (oral): Start within 24h if no signs of HF, cardiogenic shock, or PR >240 ms; reduces arrhythmia and reinfarction
- **ACE inhibitor/ARB:** Start within 24h, especially with anterior MI, EF <40%, or heart failure — reduces remodelling and mortality (SAVE, AIRE, TRACE trials)
- **Statin:** High-intensity (atorvastatin 80 mg) immediately — plaque-stabilising, anti-inflammatory effect independent of cholesterol lowering
- **Morphine:** IV titrated for pain relief (CAUTION: opioids delay absorption of oral P2Y12 inhibitors — use IV ticagrelor or IV cangrelor if available for PPCI patients needing rapid platelet inhibition)
- **Oxygen:** Only if SpO₂ <94% — hyperoxia may worsen ischaemic injury (AVOID oxygen trial; DETO2X trial showed routine O₂ did not reduce MI size or mortality in normoxic patients)

### NSTEMI/Unstable Angina Management

**Immediate:**
- Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + ticagrelor or clopidogrel)
- Anticoagulation (fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC daily — OASIS-5 trial: superior to enoxaparin with less major bleeding; or enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC BD if fondaparinux unavailable)
- Beta-blocker + statin
- Risk stratify (GRACE score) → timing of angiography

**Invasive angiography timing:**
- High risk (GRACE >140, refractory ischaemia, haemodynamic instability): within 24h
- Intermediate risk (GRACE 109–140): within 72h
- Low risk: non-invasive imaging ± elective angiography

## Complications

- **Mechanical complications (days 1–7):** Free wall rupture (sudden death, tamponade), interventricular septal rupture (new loud pansystolic murmur + biventricular failure), papillary muscle rupture → acute severe MR (pulmonary oedema + hypotension). All require emergency cardiac surgery (high mortality).
- **Arrhythmias:** VF (most common cause of early death in STEMI — treated with CPR + defibrillation); VT; AF; heart block (inferior MI + AV node involvement — may require temporary pacing); idioventricular rhythm (reperfusion arrhythmia — benign, self-limiting)
- **Heart failure/cardiogenic shock:** Loss of ≥40% LV myocardium → cardiogenic shock (mortality 50%); IABP (intra-aortic balloon pump) or Impella for haemodynamic support
- **Pericarditis:** Dressler syndrome (2–12 weeks post-MI; autoimmune; treat with aspirin/NSAIDs/colchicine)
- **LV aneurysm:** Dyskinetic segment (scar bulges outward during systole); risk of thrombus formation → stroke/emboli; anticoagulate if large

## Clinical Insight

The ECG in inferior STEMI must always be accompanied by right-sided leads. The clinical significance is pharmacological: a patient with inferior STEMI and haemodynamic instability may have RV infarction. Giving GTN or morphine (which reduce venous preload) to a patient with RV infarction can precipitate cardiovascular collapse within seconds — the failing right ventricle depends on high preload to maintain output into the pulmonary circulation. The rule is: never give GTN to a patient with inferior STEMI without first checking right-sided leads. ST elevation in V4R = RV involvement = GTN absolutely contraindicated = aggressive IV fluid loading required.

The troponin rise in STEMI begins at 2–4 hours, peaks at 12–24 hours, and remains elevated for up to 14 days. A "normal troponin" at 1 hour of chest pain onset does not exclude MI — the patient may simply be presenting early in the rise. This is why the 0/1h or 0/2h algorithm uses the delta (change) — a small absolute value combined with a rapidly rising delta is highly predictive of MI. A truly normal troponin at 0h + 1h with a low delta effectively excludes STEMI or NSTEMI in patients without ongoing ECG changes.

DAPT duration has been refined by multiple trials. After DES implantation for ACS: 12 months of aspirin + ticagrelor (or clopidogrel). After BMS: 1 month minimum. The PEGASUS-TIMI 54 trial showed that extending ticagrelor beyond 12 months in high-risk patients (prior MI, diabetes, renal disease) continued to reduce MACE but at the cost of increased major bleeding. The individualised decision requires balancing ischaemic risk against haemorrhagic risk.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 58-year-old, 40 minutes of crushing chest pain radiating to jaw/arm. Diaphoresis, nausea. HR 96, BP 154/92. ECG: ST elevation in II, III, aVF (inferior STEMI) + reciprocal depression in I, aVL. hsTnT 842 ng/L.

**Diagnosis: Inferior STEMI**

**Step 1 — Activate PPCI pathway immediately:**
- "Cath lab activation" — notify the interventional cardiology team and PPCI centre
- Target: door-to-balloon time <90 minutes

**Step 2 — Right-sided ECG before ANY drugs:**
- V3R, V4R, V5R: ST elevation ≥1 mm = RV infarction
- **If RV infarction confirmed: NO NITRATES — IV fluids 500 mL 0.9% NaCl first**

**Step 3 — Antiplatelet loading (assuming no RV infarction):**
- **Aspirin 300 mg chewed immediately**
- **Ticagrelor 180 mg orally** (loading dose; then 90 mg BD for 12 months)

**Step 4 — Anticoagulation:**
- UFH 70 units/kg IV (administered at PCI time by cath lab)

**Step 5 — Symptom management:**
- IV morphine 2.5–5 mg titrated (noting oral absorption of P2Y12 inhibitor may be delayed — acceptable if only oral form available)
- GTN sublingual 400 mcg IF BP allows AND no RV involvement confirmed
- O₂ only if SpO₂ <94%

**Step 6 — Transfer to cath lab:**
- Primary PCI: balloon angioplasty + DES to the right coronary artery (RCA — infarct-related artery for inferior territory)
- Assess LV function on angiography table (echo if not done)

**Step 7 — Post-PCI:** 
- Continue aspirin + ticagrelor (12 months DAPT)
- ACE inhibitor from day 1 (inferior MI + risk of LV remodelling)
- Beta-blocker when haemodynamically stable
- High-dose statin (atorvastatin 80 mg)
- Serial ECGs + echo at discharge

**Exam trigger:** *Crushing chest pain + inferior ST elevation (II, III, aVF) + reciprocal changes in I and aVL* → inferior STEMI; RCA occlusion; activate PPCI. Critical next step before any drugs: right-sided leads to exclude RV infarction. The distractor is giving GTN to an inferior STEMI patient without right-sided leads — if there is RV involvement, GTN causes haemodynamic collapse.
