## Overview

Pulmonary embolism (PE) occurs when a thrombus — most commonly arising from the deep veins of the legs or pelvis — embolises to the pulmonary arterial tree, obstructing blood flow to part of the lung. It represents the most severe end of the venous thromboembolism (VTE) spectrum, which includes deep vein thrombosis (DVT). PE ranges from small, incidentally detected subsegmental clots to massive bilateral occlusion causing cardiovascular collapse and death. Without treatment, PE carries a mortality of approximately 30% — making prompt diagnosis and anticoagulation life-saving.

---

## Pathophysiology

**Virchow's triad** describes the three conditions predisposing to venous thrombosis:

| Component | Examples |
|-----------|---------|
| **Venous stasis** | Immobility (long flights, bed rest, paralysis), venous obstruction from tumour or compression, right heart failure |
| **Hypercoagulability** | OCP/HRT, pregnancy/postpartum, malignancy, thrombophilia (Factor V Leiden, antiphospholipid syndrome, protein C/S deficiency, antithrombin deficiency), dehydration, polycythaemia |
| **Endothelial injury** | Major surgery (especially abdominal/pelvic and hip/knee replacement), trauma, central venous catheters |

Once thrombus forms, it may embolise to the pulmonary arteries. The haemodynamic consequences depend on the extent of vascular obstruction and the patient's cardiopulmonary reserve:

- **Small PE:** V/Q mismatch → hypoxaemia. Pleuritic pain and haemoptysis if peripheral (infarction near pleura). The classic triad of **pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, and a peripheral wedge-shaped opacity** on CXR occurs when a peripheral clot causes pulmonary infarction.
- **Submassive PE:** RV pressure overload → RV dilation and dysfunction → leftward septal shift → LV underfilling → reduced cardiac output. Haemodynamically stable but RV strain evident on echo and biomarkers.
- **Massive PE:** Complete RV failure → cardiogenic shock → cardiovascular collapse. This is a life-threatening emergency requiring immediate intervention.

**Pulmonary infarction** occurs in approximately 30% — particularly with peripheral clots occluding smaller vessels supplying the pleural surface. It produces the classic triad of pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, and a wedge-shaped pleural-based opacity on CXR (Hampton's hump).

**Rare causes of PE beyond DVT:** Right ventricular thrombus (post-MI mural thrombus), septic emboli (from right-sided infective endocarditis — multifocal cavitating pulmonary opacities), **fat embolism** (long bone fractures or orthopedic surgery — petechial rash, confusion, hypoxia), **air embolism** (central line placement, laparoscopy — mill-wheel murmur), **amniotic fluid embolism** (obstetric emergency — cardiovascular collapse + DIC at delivery), and tumour embolism.

## Clinical Presentation

PE is highly variable in presentation. The classical triad of pleuritic pain, haemoptysis, and dyspnoea occurs in only a minority. More commonly, the presentation is dominated by one feature — isolated unexplained dyspnoea, tachycardia, or presyncope.

**Always consider PE in:**
- Unexplained tachycardia or hypoxia with a normal CXR
- Pleuritic chest pain in a young person
- Post-operative dyspnoea
- Dyspnoea with risk factors for VTE but no alternative explanation
- Haemoptysis with fever and pleuritic chest pain (mimics pneumonia)

**Signs:** Pyrexia (low-grade, common), cyanosis (in large PE), tachypnoea, tachycardia, hypotension (massive PE), raised JVP (RV failure), pleural rub (infarction near pleura), pleural effusion. Signs of DVT (unilateral leg swelling, erythema, tenderness) in ~50% of PE cases — always examine the legs.

## Diagnosis

### Pre-Test Probability Assessment

**PERC rule** (Pulmonary Embolism Rule-Out Criteria): If clinical suspicion is low AND all 8 criteria are absent, the probability of PE is <2% and no further testing is required:
- Age <50 years
- HR <100 bpm
- SpO₂ ≥95%
- No unilateral leg swelling
- No haemoptysis
- No recent surgery or trauma (within 4 weeks)
- No prior DVT/PE
- No exogenous oestrogen (OCP, HRT)

If any PERC criterion is positive, proceed to Wells scoring.

**Wells PE Score:**

| Criterion | Points |
|---------|--------|
| Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT (leg swelling, pain on deep palpation of veins) | 3 |
| PE is the most likely diagnosis (or equally likely as other diagnoses) | 3 |
| HR >100 bpm | 1.5 |
| Immobilisation (≥3 days) or major surgery within 4 weeks | 1.5 |
| Previous DVT or PE | 1.5 |
| Haemoptysis | 1 |
| Active malignancy (treatment within 6 months, or palliative) | 1 |

Score ≤4 = PE unlikely → D-dimer. Score >4 = PE likely → CTPA.

### Investigations

**D-dimer:**
- High sensitivity (>95%), low specificity. Elevated in infection, malignancy, pregnancy, surgery, AF, increasing age, and many other conditions.
- Use only to **rule out** PE in patients with **low pre-test probability** (Wells ≤4). A negative D-dimer in this group safely excludes PE.
- A positive D-dimer in a low-probability patient, or any D-dimer in a high-probability patient, requires CTPA.
- Do not use D-dimer as a rule-in test — a positive result confirms only that you need imaging.
- Age-adjusted D-dimer threshold: age × 10 µg/L (in patients over 50) — increases specificity without significant loss of sensitivity.

**CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA):** Gold standard. Shows filling defects within pulmonary arteries. Also identifies RV dilation (prognostic), alternative diagnoses (pneumonia, aortic dissection, effusion), and complications. Indicated for all patients with Wells >4, or Wells ≤4 with a positive D-dimer.

**ECG:** Sinus tachycardia (most common finding, >50%). Classic S₁Q₃T₃ (deep S in I, Q wave and T-wave inversion in III) is present in only ~20% and not specific. RBBB, right axis deviation, and T-wave inversions V1–V4 indicate RV strain. A normal ECG does not exclude PE.

**ABG:** Type 1 respiratory failure (↓PaO₂, ↓PaCO₂ — from hyperventilation causing a respiratory alkalosis). Normal ABG does not exclude PE — up to 20% of confirmed PEs have a normal PaO₂.

**Echocardiography:** RV dilation and dysfunction, D-shaped septum (septal bowing into LV), tricuspid regurgitation, elevated RVSP. Used when CTPA unavailable or patient too unstable to transport; can also identify right heart thrombus requiring emergency thrombectomy. McConnell's sign (RV free wall hypokinesis with apical sparing) is relatively specific for acute PE.

**Lower limb compression ultrasound:** If DVT is confirmed in a high-probability patient, PE can be assumed and treatment started without waiting for CTPA.

**V/Q scanning:** Alternative to CTPA when contrast allergy, severe renal impairment, or pregnancy. Also more sensitive than CTPA for **chronic thromboembolic disease (CTEPH)** — where organised clot is not seen as filling defects on CTPA.

**Troponin and BNP/NT-proBNP:** Markers of RV strain. Elevated troponin or BNP indicates myocardial injury from RV pressure overload — both are adverse prognostic markers used to risk-stratify haemodynamically stable PE for potential escalation.

## Management

### Massive PE (haemodynamic instability — systolic BP <90 mmHg or fall >40 mmHg)

- **Systemic thrombolysis** — **alteplase 10mg IV bolus over 1 minute, then 90mg IVI over 2 hours** (maximum 1.5mg/kg if body weight <65kg). Reverses RV failure, dramatically improves haemodynamics. First-line if no absolute contraindications.
- Absolute contraindications to thrombolysis: prior haemorrhagic stroke (ever), ischaemic stroke within 3 months, known intracranial neoplasm, active significant internal bleeding (excluding menses), suspected aortic dissection
- Relative contraindications: TIA within 6 months, oral anticoagulation, pregnancy, non-compressible puncture site, traumatic resuscitation, refractory hypertension, active peptic ulcer, recent major surgery
- If thrombolysis fails or is absolutely contraindicated: **surgical embolectomy** or **catheter-directed thrombolysis/thrombectomy** at a specialist centre
- Supportive: cautious IV fluids (excessive volume worsens RV dilation — the RV is afterload-sensitive; fluid overloading a failing RV worsens output), vasopressors (**noradrenaline** is the vasopressor of choice), oxygen, intubation only if unavoidable (intubation eliminates the Valsalva effect and can precipitate cardiac arrest in massive PE)

### Submassive PE (RV dysfunction, haemodynamically stable)

- Anticoagulation as primary treatment
- Close monitoring for haemodynamic deterioration — if it occurs, consider escalation to thrombolysis
- Decision to thrombolyse requires careful individualised risk-benefit assessment (PEITHO trial: thrombolysis reduces haemodynamic deterioration but increases intracranial haemorrhage)

### Non-Massive PE (haemodynamically stable, no RV dysfunction)

- **Anticoagulate immediately** — do not wait for imaging confirmation if clinical probability is high and the patient is deteriorating. Anticoagulation carries a lower risk than untreated PE.
- **DOACs are first-line** — rivaroxaban (15mg BD for 3 weeks, then 20mg OD with evening meal) or apixaban (10mg BD for 7 days, then 5mg BD). No bridging with LMWH required.
- LMWH bridging to warfarin — if DOAC contraindicated (severe renal impairment GFR <15 mL/min/1.73m², antiphospholipid syndrome — DOAC inferior to warfarin in APS)
- Cancer-associated VTE: LMWH or edoxaban/rivaroxaban are preferred over warfarin
- **Vena caval filter:** If anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated (active major bleeding) — provides mechanical protection against further pulmonary emboli but does not treat existing thrombus

**Duration of anticoagulation:**

| Context | Duration |
|---------|---------|
| Provoked by major transient risk factor (surgery, trauma, immobilisation) | 3 months |
| Provoked by minor transient risk factor (OCP, long-haul flight, minor injury) | 3–6 months |
| Unprovoked PE or ongoing risk factor | Minimum 6 months; consider indefinite after discussion of risk/benefit |
| Recurrent VTE | Indefinite anticoagulation |
| Malignancy-associated | Continue throughout active cancer course |
| Antiphospholipid syndrome | Indefinite warfarin (not DOAC) |

**Outpatient management:** Low-risk patients (sPESI score = 0, no RV dysfunction, haemodynamically stable) can be managed as outpatients with a DOAC and clear safety-netting. sPESI assigns 1 point each for age >80, cancer, chronic cardiopulmonary disease, HR ≥110, SpO₂ <90%, systolic BP <100 mmHg. Score 0 = low risk.

### Unprovoked PE — Investigation

Any patient with a first unprovoked PE should be investigated for underlying risk factors:
- **Occult malignancy screen:** Full history and physical examination (including breast, lymph nodes, abdomen, PR), CXR, FBC, calcium, LFTs, U&E, urinalysis; consider CT TAP if any suspicious features
- **Antiphospholipid syndrome:** Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin antibodies, anti-β₂-glycoprotein I antibodies. Test in young patients with unprovoked PE or recurrent VTE — if confirmed, lifelong warfarin required (not DOACs)
- **Thrombophilia testing:** Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, protein C/S, antithrombin deficiency — if family history positive or recurrent episode. Test after stopping anticoagulation.

## Complications

- **Death** from massive PE or haemodynamic deterioration
- **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH)** — occurs in ~3–5% of PE survivors; failure to fully resolve clot, progressive pulmonary vascular obliteration; presents with progressive dyspnoea months to years after the original PE; investigate with V/Q scan (more sensitive than CTPA for organised chronic thrombus); potentially curative with **pulmonary endarterectomy** (surgical removal of organised clot)
- **Recurrent VTE** — highest risk in unprovoked PE and cancer-associated PE; indefinite anticoagulation substantially reduces recurrence
- **Bleeding from anticoagulation** — intracranial haemorrhage is the most feared; reversal agents available for all DOACs (andexanet alfa for apixaban/rivaroxaban; idarucizumab for dabigatran)
- **Post-thrombotic syndrome** — DVT-related chronic venous insufficiency

## Clinical Insight

D-dimer is a rule-out test, not a rule-in test. A positive D-dimer in a post-operative patient, someone with active cancer, or an elderly inpatient tells you almost nothing useful — almost every patient on a medical ward will have an elevated D-dimer. The pre-test probability must drive the decision to image — not the D-dimer result alone. An elevated D-dimer in a low-probability patient means you need to image; it does not mean the patient has PE.

Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) is an important consideration in young patients with unprovoked PE, recurrent VTE, or a history of recurrent miscarriage. The DOAC-APS trial and subsequent data showed that rivaroxaban performed significantly worse than warfarin in APS — patients on rivaroxaban had more arterial events. If APS is confirmed, use warfarin (target INR 2–3), not DOACs.

CTEPH is the one complication of PE that is both common enough to matter (3–5% of survivors) and treatable with a potentially curative surgical procedure. Any patient with a history of PE who develops unexplained progressive breathlessness — even years later — should be evaluated for CTEPH with V/Q scanning, not just CTPA. V/Q scan shows perfusion defects in areas where CT shows no filling defect because the organised thrombus is not detected by CT contrast.
