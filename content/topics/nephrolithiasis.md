## Overview

Nephrolithiasis — renal stone disease — affects approximately 10–15% of the population at least once in a lifetime, with a high recurrence rate (50% within 10 years without targeted prevention). Stones form when urine becomes supersaturated with stone-forming salts, either due to increased solute excretion, decreased inhibitors, or reduced urine volume. Understanding stone composition is not academic — it drives targeted metabolic evaluation and specific prevention that can dramatically reduce recurrence. This patient's second episode at age 34 has crossed the threshold for full metabolic workup.

---

## Pathophysiology

Stone formation requires urine supersaturation with crystalline salts beyond their solubility threshold. The process involves nucleation (crystal formation), aggregation, and retention in the tubular lumen or papillary surface. Supersaturation is promoted by:
- **Increased solute concentration**: hypercalciuria (most common metabolic abnormality), hyperoxaluria, hyperuricosuria, cystinuria
- **Reduced urine volume**: dehydration, hot climates, occupational heat exposure
- **Altered urine pH**: acid urine (pH <5.5) favours uric acid and cystine crystallisation; alkaline urine favours calcium phosphate and struvite
- **Reduced inhibitors**: **hypocitraturia** (citrate binds calcium, reducing crystallisation — most important inhibitor), low Tamm-Horsfall protein, low nephrocalcin

**Stone types, associations, and radiopacity:**

| Type | Frequency | Radiopacity | Key Associations |
|------|-----------|------------|-----------------|
| Calcium oxalate | ~75% | **Radiopaque** | Hypercalciuria, hyperoxaluria, hypocitraturia |
| Calcium phosphate | ~10% | **Radiopaque** | Distal RTA type 1, hyperparathyroidism, alkaline urine |
| Uric acid | ~10% | **Radiolucent** (invisible on X-ray) | Gout, DM, metabolic syndrome, acidic urine (pH <5.5) |
| Struvite (triple phosphate) | ~5% | Radiopaque | Urease-producing organisms (*Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*) → staghorn calculi; alkaline urine |
| Cystine | ~1% | Mildly opaque | Cystinuria (AR mutation in dibasic amino acid transporter) — young patients, recurrent bilateral stones |

**Calcium stones:** Most are caused by idiopathic hypercalciuria (increased gut absorption or reduced renal tubular reabsorption of calcium — mechanisms overlap). Pathological hypercalcaemia causing stones: **primary hyperparathyroidism** (PTH drives bone calcium release and increases 1,25-OHD → increased gut absorption → hypercalciuria), sarcoidosis and granulomatous disease (extrarenal 1-alpha-hydroxylase), vitamin D toxicity, immobility.

**Hyperoxaluria** — enteric hyperoxaluria is an important and under-recognised cause: after bowel resection or bariatric surgery, fat malabsorption means unabsorbed fatty acids bind calcium in the gut, leaving oxalate free to be absorbed in large quantities. Even a modest increase in urinary oxalate dramatically increases calcium oxalate supersaturation.

**Uric acid stones** — unique in being radiolucent (invisible on plain X-ray, visible on CT) and **medically dissolvable**. They form only in persistently acid urine (pH <5.5) — because uric acid is far more soluble in alkaline urine. Alkalinising the urine to pH 6.5–7.0 with potassium citrate dissolves existing stones and prevents new ones.

## Clinical Presentation

**Renal colic:** The classic presentation — sudden onset severe, cramping, colicky loin pain radiating to the ipsilateral iliac fossa, groin, scrotum, or labia (following the ureteric course), with nausea, vomiting, and haematuria. The pain is **characteristically restless** — the patient moves constantly, trying every position. This distinguishes it from peritonitis (patient lies still). Renal colic is among the most severe pains in clinical medicine.

Pain occurs in waves as the ureter peristaltically contracts against the obstruction. Intermittent relief between waves is characteristic — continuous, unrelenting pain suggests another diagnosis.

**Pain localisation by stone position:**
- **Pelviureteric junction (PUJ)**: loin pain, costovertebral angle tenderness, no radiation
- **Mid-ureter**: pain radiates to the iliac fossa (mimics appendicitis on right, ovarian pathology on left)
- **Lower ureter / vesicoureteric junction (VUJ)**: pain radiates to the groin, scrotum, or labia; frequency and urgency from ureteric irritation — mimics UTI or testicular pathology

**Haematuria** — macroscopic or microscopic — is present in >90% of cases. Its absence does not exclude a stone (complete obstruction can prevent haematuria by blocking urine flow to the bladder).

**Features requiring emergency urological referral:**
- Fever and rigors — infected obstructed system (**pyonephrosis** — surgical emergency; decompression within hours)
- Bilateral obstruction or single functioning kidney with obstruction (AKI)
- Anuria
- Intractable pain or vomiting requiring IV management

**Differential diagnosis for loin-to-groin pain:**
- **Pyelonephritis** — fever, systemic illness, tenderness without colic, positive urine culture (but can coexist with a stone)
- **Appendicitis** — right iliac fossa, migration of pain from periumbilical, guarding, Rovsing's sign
- **Ruptured ectopic pregnancy** — always consider in women of reproductive age; haemodynamic instability
- **Leaking or ruptured AAA** — older vascular patient, pulsatile mass, haemodynamic compromise — misdiagnosed as renal colic in up to 4% of AAA ruptures; fatal if treated as colic

## Diagnosis

**Urine dipstick:** Haematuria present in >90%. Nitrites/leucocytes if concurrent infection.

**Non-contrast CT KUB (kidney, ureter, bladder):** Investigation of choice. Sensitivity and specificity >95% for all stone types — including radiolucent uric acid stones that are invisible on X-ray. Identifies stone size, location, degree of hydronephrosis, tissue oedema ("stranding"), and alternative diagnoses (appendicitis, ovarian pathology, AAA). Replaces plain X-ray as the initial imaging investigation.

**Plain abdominal X-ray (KUB):** Identifies radiopaque stones (calcium, struvite) in the known renal tract — useful for monitoring known radiopaque stones without repeated CT radiation. Unreliable as an initial investigation: misses uric acid, cystine, and many small calcium stones.

**Ultrasound:** Sensitive for hydronephrosis but poor for direct stone visualisation. **Preferred in pregnancy** (avoids radiation) and in children. May miss small ureteric stones. Appropriate as the initial investigation in a pregnant woman with suspected renal colic.

**Bloods:** U&E (AKI from obstruction), FBC and CRP (infection), serum calcium (hyperparathyroidism), uric acid, PTH (if hypercalcaemia or recurrent stones).

**For recurrent stone-formers — metabolic evaluation:**
- Stone analysis if stone passed or retrieved — identifies composition precisely
- **24-hour urine collection** (after the acute episode resolves): calcium, oxalate, uric acid, citrate, sodium, creatinine, and volume — identifies specific metabolic abnormalities to target
- Urine pH — spot pH (acid in uric acid stones, alkaline in struvite/RTA)

## Management

### Acute Renal Colic

**Analgesia is the priority:**
- **NSAIDs** — first-line (diclofenac IM/IV 75 mg or oral; ketorolac IV). Prostaglandin inhibition reduces ureteral smooth muscle spasm and ureteral oedema, providing superior analgesia to opioids in most patients. Avoid if AKI, single kidney, or pre-existing renal impairment.
- **Paracetamol** — effective adjunct; appropriate when NSAIDs are contraindicated
- **Opioids** (morphine IV/SC) — for severe pain or NSAID contraindication; as effective as NSAIDs but more nausea and dependence risk
- **Antiemetics** — ondansetron or metoclopramide

**Medical expulsive therapy (MET):** **Tamsulosin** 0.4 mg daily (alpha-1 adrenergic receptor blocker) — relaxes distal ureteral smooth muscle, increasing stone passage rates for stones ≤10 mm in the lower ureter. Increases passage rate by ~30% over expectant management for distal ureteric stones. Most effective at the VUJ.

**Expectant management (watchful waiting with safety netting):**
- ≤5 mm: 80–90% pass spontaneously — discharge with analgesia, tamsulosin, and urine straining (to capture the stone for analysis)
- 5–10 mm: ~50% pass spontaneously — tamsulosin increases passage rate; outpatient follow-up with interval CT at 4 weeks
- >10 mm: unlikely to pass spontaneously — plan for urological intervention

### Urological Intervention

| Indication | Preferred Approach |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Stone >10 mm or failing to pass after 4 weeks | Shockwave lithotripsy (SWL) or flexible ureteroscopy + laser lithotripsy |
| Infected obstructed system (pyonephrosis) | Emergency percutaneous nephrostomy or ureteric stent (does not remove stone — drainage only; stone cleared later) |
| Staghorn calculus (struvite) | Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) |
| Bilateral obstruction causing AKI | Emergency bilateral decompression |
| Stone in pregnancy | Conservative first; ureteroscopy if failing — SWL contraindicated in pregnancy |

**Shockwave lithotripsy (SWL):** Non-invasive; focused external shock waves fragment stones. Best for renal stones <20 mm. Not suitable for cystine or calcium oxalate monohydrate stones (resistant to fragmentation). Contraindicated in pregnancy, bleeding diathesis, ipsilateral renal artery aneurysm.

**Flexible ureteroscopy + laser lithotripsy:** Holmium:YAG laser fragments stones under direct vision. Effective for ureteric stones and small renal stones. Higher stone-free rates than SWL for stones in the lower ureter.

**PCNL (percutaneous nephrolithotomy):** Percutaneous access into the renal collecting system under fluoroscopic/ultrasound guidance. For large (>20 mm) or complex renal stones and staghorn calculi. Requires general anaesthesia; highest complication rate of all stone procedures but necessary for stones that SWL and ureteroscopy cannot clear.

### Prevention of Recurrence

**Universal measures for all stone types:**
- High fluid intake — target urine output >2.5 L/day (the single most effective prevention)
- Reduce sodium intake (reduces urinary calcium excretion)
- Reduce animal protein (reduces urinary uric acid and calcium excretion)
- **Maintain normal dietary calcium** — dietary calcium restriction paradoxically increases oxalate absorption (less gut calcium to bind oxalate), increasing stone risk. Do NOT restrict calcium.

**Type-specific prevention:**

| Stone Type | Targeted Prevention | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------|
| Calcium oxalate | **Thiazide diuretics** (reduce urinary calcium excretion); **potassium citrate** (citrate binds urinary calcium, raising its solubility; alkalinises urine); reduce oxalate-rich foods (spinach, rhubarb, nuts, chocolate) | Attack calcium and oxalate excretion; raise the inhibitor |
| Calcium phosphate | Treat underlying cause (hyperparathyroidism surgery; RTA management with bicarbonate) | Correct the metabolic driver |
| Uric acid | **Allopurinol** (reduces uric acid production); **potassium citrate** (alkalinises urine to pH 6.5–7.0 — dissolves existing stones AND prevents new ones) | Only stone type treatable medically; uric acid dissolves in alkaline urine |
| Struvite | Eradicate urinary infection; long-term antibiotic prophylaxis; complete surgical clearance (living bacteria in biofilm of the stone will cause rapid reformation) | Stone cannot be prevented without eliminating urease-producing organisms |
| Cystine | Aggressive fluid intake (urine output >3 L/day); urinary alkalinisation to pH >7.5; D-penicillamine or tiopronin (chelating agents that increase cystine solubility) | Cystine is poorly soluble; volume and alkalinisation are the foundation |

**Primary hyperparathyroidism:** All patients with calcium stones and hypercalcaemia need PTH measured. Primary hyperparathyroidism (usually single parathyroid adenoma) is curable with parathyroidectomy — normalising calcium and citrate excretion and preventing further stones. This is one of the most important reversible causes of recurrent nephrolithiasis.

## Complications

- **Infected obstructed system (pyonephrosis)** — life-threatening septicaemia; emergency decompression required within hours
- **AKI** — particularly with bilateral obstruction or obstruction of a solitary kidney
- **Chronic hydronephrosis** — untreated obstruction causes irreversible pressure atrophy of the renal parenchyma
- **Recurrence** — 50% within 10 years without targeted prevention; up to 80% with specific metabolic abnormalities left untreated
- **Post-SWL complications** — haematuria, perinephric haematoma, steinstrasse (stone fragments obstructing the ureter) — requires ureteroscopy

## Clinical Insight

Fever in the context of renal colic is a surgical emergency until proven otherwise. A ureteric stone causing obstruction proximal to a urinary tract infection creates a closed space infection — pyonephrosis — with bacteraemia that progresses to septic shock within hours. These patients may appear surprisingly well at initial assessment, then deteriorate catastrophically. Emergency decompression — ureteric stent or percutaneous nephrostomy — must be performed regardless of how unwell the patient looks. The stone is dealt with later, after the sepsis is controlled.

Uric acid stones are the only stones that can be dissolved medically. Alkalinising the urine to pH 6.5–7.0 with potassium citrate increases urate solubility by orders of magnitude — existing stones dissolve over weeks and new stones do not form. They are also entirely invisible on plain X-ray; CT is always required. A patient with known hyperuricaemia, metabolic syndrome, or gout presenting with renal colic and a negative plain KUB likely has a uric acid stone — get CT before concluding the stone has passed.

Investigate early. A first stone in a child, a woman, or anyone under 25 mandates full metabolic evaluation — primary hyperparathyroidism, distal RTA, cystinuria, and primary hyperoxaluria are important, treatable causes that present in young patients and cause progressive renal damage if missed. The 34-year-old in this scenario has had two episodes and warrants 24-hour urine studies, serum calcium, PTH, and stone analysis — not just a "drink more water" discharge.
