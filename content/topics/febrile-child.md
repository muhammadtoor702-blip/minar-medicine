## Overview

Fever is the most common reason for a child to attend an emergency department, accounting for approximately 20% of all paediatric attendances. In most cases fever represents a self-limiting viral illness, but in a subset of children — particularly those under 5 years — it signals a serious bacterial infection (SBI): meningitis, septicaemia, urinary tract infection, pneumonia, or bacteraemia. The central challenge is stratifying this risk accurately and efficiently. Missing meningococcal disease or sepsis is catastrophic; overtreating every febrile child with antibiotics is harmful. The NICE traffic light system provides a validated framework for this decision, but clinical gestalt — the experienced eye of a doctor or nurse assessing whether a child "looks well" — remains irreplaceable.

---

## Pathophysiology

Fever (core temperature >38.0°C) is an active physiological response to infection — not a malfunction. Pyrogens (bacterial LPS, viral proteins) stimulate macrophages to release IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α, which act on the anterior hypothalamus to upregulate prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) production. PGE2 resets the hypothalamic thermostat upward, triggering heat conservation (vasoconstriction, rigors) and increased heat production (shivering). The elevated temperature impairs microbial replication (many pathogens are temperature-sensitive) and enhances immune effector function.

The physiological cost of fever includes increased metabolic rate (~10% per 1°C elevation), increased oxygen consumption, fluid losses from sweating and hyperventilation, and in susceptible children, a lowered seizure threshold.

### Why Young Children Are at Particular Risk

Children under 3 months are the most vulnerable: maternal IgG has waned but active antibody production is immature; the complement system is functionally impaired; opsonisation of encapsulated bacteria (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae) is poor; and the blood-brain barrier is more permeable. The risk of SBI in a febrile infant under 3 months is substantially higher than in older children, and the clinical signs of SBI may be absent even with life-threatening infection.

## Clinical Presentation

### NICE Traffic Light System (CG160)

The NICE traffic light system categorises children by clinical features into green (low risk), amber (intermediate risk), and red (high risk) for SBI:

**Green — low risk (all green features, no amber or red):**
- Normal colour (pink), normal activity, responds normally to social cues, content or mildly distressed
- Moist mucous membranes, normal skin turgor, normal eyes
- No tachycardia, CRT <2s
- Fever: any duration in vaccinated child aged >6 months without other concerns

**Amber — intermediate risk (any amber feature, no red):**
- Pallor reported by carer; wakes only with prolonged stimulation; decreased activity
- Dry mucous membranes, poor feeding (infants), CRT 3 seconds
- Tachycardia: HR >160 in <1y, >150 in 1-2y, >140 in 2-5y
- Tachypnoea: RR >60 in <5y
- Fever >5 days; swelling of a limb or joint; new lump >2cm

**Red — high risk (any red feature):**
- Pale/mottled/ashen/blue appearance
- No response to social cues; does not wake or stays awake only briefly; weak/high-pitched/continuous cry; grunting; severe respiratory distress
- CRT ≥3 seconds; HR >160 in <1y (persistent); tachycardia with poor perfusion
- **Non-blanching rash** — meningococcal disease until proven otherwise
- Bulging fontanelle (in infants); neck stiffness; meningeal signs; seizure with fever in a child who remains ill
- Fever in infant <3 months

### Fever Without Identifiable Source

In a child aged 3–36 months who is otherwise well with no identifiable focus after thorough examination, the most likely diagnosis is a viral illness. However, occult bacteraemia (most commonly pneumococcal) can occur without localising signs. The introduction of pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV13, Prevnar) has dramatically reduced occult bacteraemia rates in vaccinated children.

### Kawasaki Disease

Kawasaki disease (KD) is a medium-vessel vasculitis of unknown aetiology (likely immune dysregulation after microbial trigger), predominantly affecting children aged 6 months to 5 years. It is the most common cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries. The diagnostic criteria are **fever ≥5 days** plus **at least 4 of 5 principal features:**

1. **Bilateral non-exudative conjunctival injection** (limbic sparing — unlike infectious conjunctivitis)
2. **Oral changes**: erythema/cracking of lips, strawberry tongue, diffuse oral erythema
3. **Changes of extremities**: erythema/oedema of hands and feet (acute phase), periungual desquamation (subacute phase, days 10-21)
4. **Polymorphous rash**: trunk predominant, maculopapular, not vesicular
5. **Cervical lymphadenopathy**: ≥1.5 cm, usually unilateral

Incomplete KD (fever ≥5 days + 2–3 features) occurs in infants and requires echocardiography to diagnose. Kawasaki disease should be considered in any child with unexplained fever lasting ≥5 days — it is underdiagnosed and the window for effective treatment is narrow.

### Febrile Seizures

A febrile seizure is a seizure associated with fever in a child aged 6 months to 5 years without intracranial infection or previous afebrile seizures. **Simple febrile seizure**: generalised, <15 minutes, single episode in 24h — no increased risk of epilepsy beyond the baseline. **Complex febrile seizure**: focal onset, duration >15 minutes, or multiple seizures within 24h — warrants investigation (LP, neuroimaging).

## Diagnosis

**Investigations guided by traffic light category:**

**Green**: No investigations required unless fever >5 days

**Amber**: Urine MC&S (most important investigation — UTI is the most common SBI in children), FBC, CRP, blood cultures, consider CXR if respiratory symptoms or unexplained tachycardia; LP if meningism signs

**Red**: Full sepsis workup — blood cultures, FBC, CRP, U&E, LFTs, lactate, blood glucose (hypoglycaemia precipitates seizures), clotting; urine MC&S; LP if not contraindicated; CXR; LP should not delay antibiotics in suspected meningococcal disease

**UTI in children (NICE CG54 revised):** Urine must be properly collected — clean catch is preferred in all ages, suprapubic aspiration (SPA) is the gold standard for sterility. Catheter specimen is acceptable. Urine pad specimens are not acceptable for culture (high contamination rate). UTI is confirmed by significant growth on culture (>10⁵ cfu/mL single organism). Imaging: first febrile UTI in children <6 months → USS within 6 weeks; recurrent UTI → DMSA scan at 4-6 months (detects renal scarring); abnormal USS or atypical/recurrent → MCUG to exclude vesicoureteric reflux.

**Kawasaki disease investigations:** FBC (anaemia, leucocytosis, thrombocytosis — platelets >450 × 10⁹/L in subacute phase), CRP/ESR markedly elevated, sterile pyuria (urinalysis), mildly elevated transaminases, hypoalbuminaemia; **echocardiogram** — coronary artery z-scores, pericardial effusion.

## Management

### General Approach to the Febrile Child

**Antipyretics:** Paracetamol or ibuprofen — for comfort, not to reduce infection risk or prevent febrile seizures. Do not routinely alternate antipyretics; do not give ibuprofen to dehydrated children (NSAID nephrotoxicity risk).

**Fluids:** Ensure adequate hydration — oral fluids preferred; IV fluids for red features/inability to drink.

**Antibiotics:** Not routinely indicated for green/amber category without identified bacterial source. For red category or any clinical concern — treat immediately.

### Kawasaki Disease

**High-dose aspirin** 30–50 mg/kg/day in 4 divided doses (anti-inflammatory) + **IVIG 2 g/kg single infusion over 12 hours** — must be given within 10 days of fever onset. IVIG reduces coronary artery aneurysm incidence from 25% (untreated) to <5%. Once afebrile, switch to low-dose aspirin 3–5 mg/kg/day for 6–8 weeks (antiplatelet). Echo at diagnosis, 6 weeks, and 1 year. Giant coronary artery aneurysms (z-score >10) require long-term anticoagulation and specialist cardiology follow-up.

### Meningococcal Disease

**Do not delay — give antibiotics before any other investigation:**
- **IV/IM benzylpenicillin** (or ceftriaxone if penicillin allergy) immediately in the community or A&E
- Add IV dexamethasone (0.15 mg/kg QDS × 4 days) if bacterial meningitis suspected
- Chemoprophylaxis to household contacts: ciprofloxacin 500 mg PO single dose (adults); public health notification

### Febrile Seizures

During a seizure: recovery position, protect from injury, time the seizure. If >5 minutes: buccal midazolam 0.3 mg/kg (or PR diazepam) — treat as status epilepticus if ongoing. After: reassure parents (90% of simple febrile seizures have an excellent prognosis), educate on first-aid management, no routine anticonvulsant prophylaxis. LP in any child with complex febrile seizure or who remains unwell after seizure resolves.

## Complications

- **Meningococcal septicaemia**: DIC, limb ischaemia and gangrene (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome), adrenal haemorrhage, death
- **Bacterial meningitis**: sensorineural hearing loss (10–15%), cognitive impairment, hydrocephalus, epilepsy
- **Kawasaki disease**: coronary artery aneurysms → thrombosis → myocardial infarction; in the acute phase, myocarditis; rare: macrophage activation syndrome
- **UTI**: renal scarring (reflux nephropathy) → hypertension and chronic kidney disease in adulthood
- **Febrile seizures**: status epilepticus; small increased risk of epilepsy with complex febrile seizures
- **Occult bacteraemia**: meningitis, septicaemia, pneumonia if not detected

## Clinical Insight

The non-blanching rash is the most important physical sign in all of paediatrics. A petechial or purpuric rash in a febrile child is meningococcal disease until proven otherwise — no investigation should delay treatment. Give benzylpenicillin. Press a glass against the rash — if it does not blanch, treat. If you are wrong and it turns out to be viral, you have given an unnecessary antibiotic to a well child. If you are right and you hesitate, the child may die within hours.

Kawasaki disease is the diagnosis most commonly missed in febrile children, and the consequences of missing it can be catastrophic. Coronary artery aneurysms — the principal danger — are irreversible and can lead to myocardial infarction in childhood or young adulthood. The diagnostic criteria require only fever ≥5 days plus 4 of 5 features, but incomplete KD occurs (particularly in infants), and any child with unexplained fever lasting ≥5 days should have an echocardiogram. The treatment window is tight — IVIG is less effective after 10 days of fever.

Do not be falsely reassured by a "normal" CRP or WBC in a febrile infant. Neonates and young infants (<3 months) can have life-threatening bacterial infection with entirely normal inflammatory markers early in the course. A febrile infant under 3 months is red-category by definition and requires a full septic screen regardless of how well they look.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 2-year-old girl, 2-day fever to 39.2°C, irritable, reduced oral intake, no focus. HR 148, RR 28, CRT 2 seconds. Alert but miserable.

**Step 1 — Apply the NICE traffic light:**
- Pallor? Not mentioned — but irritable and miserable (amber activity)
- CRT 2 seconds (borderline amber)
- HR 148 for a 2-year-old (amber threshold: >150 in 1-2y) — borderline
- No red features (no mottling/cyanosis, no non-blanching rash, no bulging fontanelle)
- **Amber category** — intermediate risk for SBI

**Step 2 — No focus found: the most important investigation is urine:**
- UTI is the most common SBI in young children — no localising symptoms in toddlers (can't verbalise dysuria/frequency)
- Clean catch urine sample — do not use dipstick alone; send for MC&S
- Other investigations in this amber child: FBC, CRP, blood cultures (if CRP >20 mg/L or clinical concern worsens)

**Step 3 — Consider Kawasaki disease:** This child has had fever for 2 days — too early to meet the ≥5-day criterion yet. However, if fever persists beyond 5 days: reassess for KD features (conjunctival injection, lip changes, rash, hand/foot changes, lymphadenopathy). Echo is warranted at 5+ days of unexplained fever in a child this age. KD is the diagnosis most commonly missed in this clinical scenario.

**Step 4 — Management of the amber child without identified source:**
- Observe: assess hydration and fluid intake; encourage oral fluids
- Antipyretics for comfort (paracetamol 15 mg/kg QDS + ibuprofen 5 mg/kg TDS in children over 3 months)
- Safety-netting: return immediately if rash develops, becomes difficult to rouse, develops breathing difficulty, or develops a red-traffic-light feature
- Review urine culture result at 48 hours

**Step 5 — If child deteriorates to red features:**
- Immediate blood cultures → IV ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg
- If non-blanching rash appears → IM/IV benzylpenicillin immediately
- LP when safe (not in haemodynamically unstable child)

**Exam trigger:** *2-year-old + fever + irritability + no focus + amber features* → investigate for UTI (most common SBI in this age group). Urine culture is the most important investigation. The distractor is starting oral amoxicillin empirically without sending urine first — contaminating the sample and making culture impossible, and treating empirically without knowing the organism or sensitivities.
