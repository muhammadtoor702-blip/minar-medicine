## Overview

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, classified as acute or chronic based on whether pancreatic function and morphology return to normal after the episode. Acute pancreatitis ranges from mild, self-limiting disease (~80%) to fulminant necrotising pancreatitis with multi-organ failure and up to 30% mortality in the most severe cases. Chronic pancreatitis is progressive and irreversible. The most common causes in the UK are gallstones (~45%) and alcohol (~35%), together accounting for ~80% of cases.

## Acute Pancreatitis

### Pathophysiology

The normal pancreas protects itself from autodigestion by secreting inactive zymogens — trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, pro-elastase, pro-phospholipase — that are activated only after entering the duodenum. Acute pancreatitis begins with **premature, intracellular activation of trypsinogen to trypsin** within acinar cells → autocatalytic activation cascade of all pancreatic enzymes → autodigestion of the gland. The trigger for this premature activation varies by aetiology:

**Gallstones (~45%)**: A gallstone impacting at the ampulla of Vater causes biliary-pancreatic reflux (bile into the pancreatic duct) or elevated intraductal pressure → acinar cell membrane disruption → intracellular trypsin activation. Gallstone pancreatitis is the most common cause in women.

**Alcohol (~35%)**: Ethanol and its metabolite acetaldehyde are directly toxic to acinar cells; alcohol alters zymogen secretion patterns and promotes intracellular trypsinogen activation. Typically requires years of heavy drinking; a single binge is an uncommon trigger in a naive pancreas.

**Other causes — GET SMASHED mnemonic:**

| Letter | Cause |
|--------|-------|
| G | Gallstones |
| E | Ethanol (alcohol) |
| T | Trauma (blunt abdominal trauma) |
| S | Steroids |
| M | Mumps (and other viral infections — coxsackievirus, CMV) |
| A | Autoimmune (IgG4-related pancreatitis — responds to steroids) |
| S | Scorpion sting (tropical medicine), hypercalcaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia (>975 mg/dL, i.e., >11 mmol/L) |
| H | Hypertriglyceridaemia, Hypercalcaemia |
| E | ERCP (post-procedural, ~3–5% risk) |
| D | Drugs: azathioprine, thiazides, furosemide, valproate, tetracyclines, didanosine |

Once established, the local inflammatory cascade releases cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) that can trigger a systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS), leading to ARDS, AKI, and circulatory failure — the hallmarks of severe pancreatitis. The clinical course is determined by whether this systemic response is transient or persistent.

### Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms**: Sudden onset severe epigastric or left hypochondrial pain, typically radiating to the back ("boring through" to the back is the classic description), relieved by leaning forward. Nausea and vomiting are almost universal. The pain is constant (not colicky — distinguishing it from biliary colic). The patient cannot find a comfortable position.

**Signs**: Epigastric and left hypochondrial tenderness, guarding. Bowel sounds reduced or absent (paralytic ileus). In severe pancreatitis: tachycardia, hypotension, fever, tachypnoea.

**Late signs of retroperitoneal haemorrhage** (severe necrotising pancreatitis, present in <5%):
- **Grey-Turner's sign** — flank bruising from retroperitoneal haemorrhage tracking laterally
- **Cullen's sign** — periumbilical bruising from tracking anteriorly
Both indicate severe haemorrhagic necrotising pancreatitis with poor prognosis when present.

**Differential diagnosis for acute severe epigastric pain:**
- Peptic ulcer perforation — board-like rigidity, free air under diaphragm on erect CXR
- Inferior MI — always obtain ECG; diabetics and elderly can present with epigastric pain
- Aortic dissection — tearing pain, BP asymmetry
- Mesenteric ischaemia — pain out of proportion to signs, AF risk factor, bowel sounds typically absent
- Cholecystitis — right upper quadrant, positive Murphy's sign, fever

### Diagnosis

**Serum amylase**: Rises within 2–12 hours, peaks at 24 hours, normalises by 3–5 days. A level >3× upper limit of normal (>300 U/L) in the appropriate clinical context is diagnostic. Limitations: can be elevated in perforated peptic ulcer, mesenteric ischaemia, and salivary disease; may be falsely normal in severe or late presentation pancreatitis (the amylase-secreting acinar cells are destroyed).

**Serum lipase**: More sensitive and specific than amylase; remains elevated for 7–14 days. Increasingly preferred, particularly in delayed presentations (>24 hours after pain onset). Serum lipase is not elevated in salivary disease.

**Bloods**: FBC (leucocytosis), U&E (AKI, watch potassium), LFTs (elevated bilirubin and ALP suggest gallstone pancreatitis with biliary obstruction), calcium (hypocalcaemia from fat saponification — a severity marker), glucose (hyperglycaemia from islet damage), triglycerides (if cause uncertain), CRP (rises over 24–48 hours — most useful severity marker at 48 hours; CRP >150 mg/L at 48h suggests severe pancreatitis).

**USS abdomen**: Mandatory in all acute pancreatitis to identify gallstones and biliary duct dilation. Bowel gas frequently limits pancreatic visualisation — absence of stones on USS does not exclude gallstone pancreatitis; MRCP is more sensitive for microlithiasis.

**CT abdomen with contrast (CECT)**: Not required in mild pancreatitis (does not change management). Indicated for: diagnostic uncertainty, failure to improve after 48–72 hours, or suspected complications (necrosis, abscess, pseudocyst). Best performed at 48–72 hours (early scanning underestimates necrosis extent). The **CT Severity Index (Balthazar CTSI)** grades peripancreatic inflammation (A–E) and percentage necrosis — correlates with morbidity and mortality. Necrosis involving >50% of the gland predicts high complication rate.

**Severity assessment — Modified Glasgow (Imrie) Score** (assess at 48 hours after admission):

| Criterion | Threshold (abnormal) |
|-----------|---------------------|
| Age | >55 years |
| WBC | >15 × 10⁹/L |
| Glucose | >180 mg/dL (in a patient without known diabetes) |
| BUN | >45 mg/dL |
| PaO₂ | <60 mmHg |
| Calcium | <8 mg/dL |
| Albumin | <3.2 g/dL |
| LDH | >600 U/L |

Score ≥3 at 48 hours = **severe pancreatitis** → ICU/HDU admission.

**Revised Atlanta Classification** (2012):
- **Mild**: No organ failure, no local/systemic complications
- **Moderately severe**: Transient organ failure (<48 hours) or local complications without persistent organ failure
- **Severe**: Persistent organ failure (>48 hours) in one or more organ systems (cardiovascular, respiratory, renal) — mortality up to 30–40%

### Management

**Mild acute pancreatitis (majority of cases):**
- **IV fluid resuscitation** — aggressive early hydration critical in the first 24–48 hours (250–500 mL/hour crystalloid initially). Lactated Ringer's/Hartmann's preferred over 0.9% saline (WATERFALL trial and meta-analyses: Hartmann's associated with lower SIRS rates; saline causes hyperchloraemic acidosis in large volumes).
- **Analgesia** — IV opioids (morphine or pethidine); patient-controlled analgesia for severe pain; NSAIDs adjuncts if no contraindication
- **Early oral feeding** as soon as tolerated — not nil-by-mouth as previously taught. Early enteral nutrition maintains gut barrier integrity and reduces bacterial translocation and infectious complications. Clear liquids can begin when nausea subsides.
- **No prophylactic antibiotics** — multiple RCTs have shown no mortality benefit; they increase antibiotic resistance and risk of *C. difficile*. Antibiotics are only indicated for confirmed infected necrosis or concomitant cholangitis.

**Severe acute pancreatitis:**
- ICU/HDU admission with close monitoring and organ support
- **Nasojejunal (NJ) enteral nutrition** within 24–48 hours — superior to TPN in all RCTs; reduces infectious complications, need for surgery, and mortality by maintaining intestinal barrier and preventing bacterial translocation. Nasogastric feeding is also acceptable if NJ placement is difficult.
- TPN reserved for cases where enteral feeding is impossible (e.g., duodenal ileus, persistent vomiting)
- **ERCP within 24–72 hours** if associated **cholangitis** (fever + jaundice + biliary obstruction — Charcot's triad) or persistent biliary obstruction — does not benefit pancreatitis without cholangitis and risks ERCP-induced pancreatitis in already-inflamed tissue

**Infected pancreatic necrosis — step-up approach:**
1. **Antibiotics** (imipenem or meropenem — good pancreatic penetration) — for confirmed infected necrosis; delay intervention for at least 4 weeks if stable (allows demarcation and walling off)
2. **Percutaneous/endoscopic drainage** — first step; minimally invasive; effective in ~40% of cases
3. **Video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement (VARD)** or endoscopic necrosectomy — if drainage alone insufficient
4. **Open necrosectomy** — reserved for failure of minimally invasive approaches; highest morbidity

**Gallstone pancreatitis — cholecystectomy timing is critical**: Perform cholecystectomy during the same admission for mild pancreatitis, or within 2–4 weeks of discharge for moderately severe disease (once inflammation subsides). Without cholecystectomy, ~30% of patients have a second episode of gallstone pancreatitis within 6 weeks. With cholecystectomy, recurrence drops to ~3%. **Every day that surgery is deferred in a fit patient is an unnecessary risk of recurrence.**

## Chronic Pancreatitis

### Pathophysiology

Chronic pancreatitis is progressive, irreversible inflammation leading to fibrosis and destruction of the exocrine and eventually endocrine pancreatic parenchyma. The end result is loss of digestive enzyme secretion (exocrine insufficiency) and loss of insulin and glucagon secretion (endocrine insufficiency).

**Causes**: Alcohol most common (~70–80%); smoking (independent risk factor, synergistic with alcohol); hereditary/genetic (CFTR mutations [cystic fibrosis], PRSS1 [hereditary pancreatitis — trypsinogen gain-of-function mutation, autosomal dominant], SPINK1 [trypsin inhibitor deficiency]); autoimmune (IgG4-related — responds to steroids); obstructive (stones, stricture, pancreas divisum); idiopathic.

### Clinical Presentation

**Pain**: Chronic, debilitating epigastric pain "boring" to the back — the dominant symptom. Relieved by sitting forward or applying a hot water bottle to the epigastrium (erythema ab igne — mottled skin discolouration from heat exposure is a clinical clue). Worse after meals (stimulated secretion against obstructed ducts). Leads to food avoidance, weight loss, and often narcotic dependence. Paradoxically, pain may improve in late ("burnt-out") disease as the gland is completely fibrosed.

**Exocrine insufficiency**: Steatorrhoea (pale, fatty, offensive, bulky, difficult-to-flush stools from malabsorbed fat) once >90% of exocrine function is lost — this represents substantial glandular destruction. Malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K): weight loss, osteoporosis, night blindness, coagulopathy. Faecal elastase is a non-invasive marker of exocrine function — levels <100 µg/g indicate severe insufficiency.

**Endocrine insufficiency**: Type 3c ("pancreatogenic") diabetes mellitus — occurs late in the disease course when islet cells are destroyed. Characteristically brittle, prone to hypoglycaemia (both insulin deficiency and glucagon deficiency impair the hypoglycaemic response), and requires low doses of insulin. Oral hypoglycaemics are generally ineffective.

**Investigations**: Ultrasound or CT may show pancreatic calcifications (diagnostic of chronic pancreatitis — calcified proteinaceous plugs in dilated pancreatic ducts), duct dilation, and atrophy. AXR may show speckled calcification in the epigastrium. MRCP defines ductal anatomy for intervention planning.

### Management

- **Complete alcohol and smoking cessation** — the only interventions that slow progression; continued alcohol use accelerates fibrosis
- High-calorie diet, low fat (reduces stimulation of residual exocrine secretion); small frequent meals; medium-chain triglycerides (MCT oil, if tolerated) do not require pancreatic lipase for absorption
- **Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)**: Creon (lipase + amylase + protease) with every meal and snack, taken mid-meal; titrate dose to normalise stool consistency and prevent steatorrhoea. Supplement fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) — often depleted even before steatorrhoea is clinically apparent.
- **Analgesia**: Stepwise — paracetamol → NSAIDs → weak opioids → strong opioids. Neuropathic agents (pregabalin, amitriptyline) for the neuropathic component. Coeliac plexus block (ultrasound- or CT-guided injection of corticosteroid or neurolytic agent) for refractory pain — effective in ~50%; duration of benefit variable.
- **Endoscopic or surgical drainage** for dilated pancreatic duct with dominant stricture or stones: pancreaticojejunostomy (Puestow procedure) creates a drainage anastomosis; superior to endoscopic stenting for long-term pain control in RCTs.
- **Autoimmune pancreatitis (IgG4-related)**: Responds dramatically to steroids (prednisolone 40 mg/day) — exclude before concluding chronic pancreatitis is from alcohol or idiopathic, especially if no history of alcohol and serum IgG4 is elevated.

## Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) — the most common and most lethal pancreatic malignancy. 5-year survival is ~12% (improved from 3% a decade ago but still dismal). Most present with locally advanced or metastatic disease.

**Risk factors**: Smoking (most important modifiable — doubles risk), obesity, new-onset diabetes in older patients (particularly with weight loss — may be paraneoplastic), chronic pancreatitis (20× risk after 20 years), family history, hereditary syndromes (BRCA2 and PALB2 mutations — ~10× risk; Lynch syndrome, hereditary pancreatitis).

**Presentation**: Painless progressive obstructive jaundice with pale stools and dark urine is the classic presentation for head of pancreas tumours — the only group likely to be resectable. Body/tail tumours present later with epigastric/back pain, weight loss, and anorexia — usually metastatic at diagnosis. **Courvoisier's law**: a palpable, non-tender gallbladder in a jaundiced patient suggests malignant biliary obstruction rather than gallstones (gallstones cause gallbladder fibrosis and contracture, preventing distension). New-onset diabetes in a person over 50 with weight loss warrants pancreatic imaging.

**Investigation**: Triple-phase CT of the pancreas (gold standard for resectability assessment), MRCP (ductal anatomy), endoscopic ultrasound (EUS — best for small tumours <2 cm and tissue acquisition by EUS-guided FNA), CA 19-9 (tumour marker — useful for monitoring response and detecting recurrence, not for diagnosis).

**Management**: Whipple's procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy — resection of head, duodenum, distal common bile duct, gallbladder, and proximal jejunum with pancreaticojejunostomy, hepaticojejunostomy, and gastrojejunostomy reconstruction) for resectable head-of-pancreas tumours. Only ~15–20% are resectable at presentation. Adjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine + capecitabine, or FOLFIRINOX for fit patients) improves 5-year survival. Palliative ERCP + biliary stent for obstructive jaundice. Palliative chemotherapy for advanced disease (FOLFIRINOX for fit patients; gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel as an alternative).

## Complications

**Acute pancreatitis**: Pancreatic necrosis (sterile or infected — infected necrosis requires drainage + antibiotics), pseudocyst (walled-off fluid collection — may resolve spontaneously or require endoscopic drainage), abscess, splenic vein thrombosis (causing gastric varices), ARDS, AKI, DIC, hypocalcaemia (fat saponification consumes calcium), hyperglycaemia.

**Chronic pancreatitis**: Malabsorption, Type 3c diabetes, pseudocyst, splenic vein thrombosis (gastric varices), biliary stricture, pancreatic adenocarcinoma (~20× lifetime risk after 20 years of chronic pancreatitis), narcotic dependence.

## Clinical Insight

Cholecystectomy during the index admission for mild gallstone pancreatitis prevents recurrence. Without it, ~30% of patients will have a second episode within 6 weeks — the next episode may be more severe. Discharge without a plan for cholecystectomy is incomplete management. This is not an elective procedure to schedule at some distant future date: surgical admission before the patient goes home is the standard of care.

Early enteral nutrition in severe pancreatitis is not merely preferred over TPN — it actively prevents complications. The gut becomes the mediator of the systemic inflammatory response in severe pancreatitis through bacterial translocation from a compromised intestinal barrier. Feeding via a nasojejunal tube maintains barrier integrity, reduces infectious complications, and reduces mortality. Nil-by-mouth and TPN should be reserved for the uncommon situation where enteral feeding is technically impossible.

New-onset diabetes in a patient over 50 with weight loss and upper abdominal discomfort requires pancreatic imaging before accepting a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. Type 3c pancreatogenic diabetes is a recognised presenting feature of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. A "new type 2 diabetes" diagnosis in this clinical context without abdominal imaging is incomplete — and potentially delays a diagnosis that, caught earlier, might be resectable.
