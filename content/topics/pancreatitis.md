## Overview

Pancreatitis is inflammation of the pancreas, classified as acute or chronic based on whether pancreatic function and morphology return to normal after the episode. Acute pancreatitis ranges from mild self-limiting disease to fulminant necrotising pancreatitis with multi-organ failure and 30% mortality. The most common causes in the UK are gallstones and alcohol, together accounting for ~80% of cases.

---

## Acute Pancreatitis

### Pathophysiology

The normal pancreas protects itself from autodigestion by secreting inactive zymogens (trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen, pro-elastase) that are activated only in the duodenum. Acute pancreatitis begins with premature, intracellular activation of trypsinogen → trypsin → activation cascade of all pancreatic enzymes → autodigestion of the gland. The trigger for this premature activation varies by aetiology:

**Gallstones** (~45%): A gallstone impacting at the ampulla of Vater causes biliary-pancreatic reflux (bile into the pancreatic duct) or elevated intraductal pressure → acinar cell injury.

**Alcohol** (~35%): Acetaldehyde is directly toxic to acinar cells; alters zymogen secretion patterns; promotes intracellular trypsinogen activation.

| D | Drugs (azathioprine, thiazides, furosemide, valproate, tetracyclines) |

Once established, the local inflammatory cascade releases cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) that can cause a systemic inflammatory response, leading to ARDS, AKI, and circulatory failure — the hallmarks of severe pancreatitis.

### Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms**: Sudden onset severe epigastric pain, typically radiating to the back, relieved by leaning forward (reduces pancreatic duct pressure). Nausea and vomiting are almost universal. The pain is constant (not colicky) — distinguishing it from biliary colic.

**Signs**: Epigastric and left hypochondrial tenderness, guarding. In severe pancreatitis: tachycardia, hypotension, fever, reduced bowel sounds (ileus). Grey-Turner's sign (flank bruising) and Cullen's sign (periumbilical bruising) indicate retroperitoneal haemorrhage — a sign of severe necrotising pancreatitis; present in <5% but carry a grim prognosis.

**Differential diagnosis for acute severe epigastric pain:**
- Peptic ulcer perforation — board-like rigidity, free air under diaphragm on CXR
- Acute MI (inferior) — always do ECG; diabetics and elderly can present with epigastric pain
- Aortic dissection — tearing pain, BP asymmetry
- Mesenteric ischaemia — pain out of proportion to signs, AF risk factor
- Cholecystitis — RUQ rather than epigastric, positive Murphy's sign

### Diagnosis

**Serum amylase**: Rises within 2–12 hours, peaks at 24 hours, normalises by 3–5 days. A level >3× upper limit of normal (usually >300 U/L) in the appropriate clinical context is diagnostic. Amylase can be elevated in other conditions (perforated ulcer, mesenteric ischaemia, salivary disease) and may be falsely normal in severe/late pancreatitis.

**Serum lipase**: More sensitive and specific than amylase; remains elevated for longer (up to 2 weeks). Increasingly preferred, particularly when the patient presents late.

**Bloods**: FBC (raised WBC), U&E (AKI, electrolyte disturbance), LFTs (bilirubin and ALP elevated in gallstone pancreatitis — biliary obstruction), calcium (hypocalcaemia from fat saponification — a severity marker), glucose, triglycerides, CRP (peaks at 48 hours — most useful severity marker after initial presentation).

**USS abdomen**: Mandatory in all acute pancreatitis to identify gallstones and biliary dilation. Often shows a swollen, hypoechoic pancreas. Gas and patient habitus frequently limit pancreatic visualisation.

**CT abdomen with contrast (CECT)**: Not required in mild pancreatitis. Indicated for: diagnostic uncertainty, failure to improve after 48–72 hours, or suspected complications (necrosis, abscess). The CT Severity Index (CTSI) grades peripancreatic inflammation and percentage necrosis — correlates with morbidity and mortality.

**Severity assessment — Modified Glasgow (Imrie) Score** (assess at 48 hours):

| Criterion | Abnormal Value |
|---------|--------------|
| Age | >55 years |
| WBC | >15 × 10⁹/L |
| Glucose | >10 mmol/L (no diabetes) |
| Urea | >16 mmol/L |
| PaO₂ | <8 kPa |
| Calcium | <2 mmol/L |
| Albumin | <32 g/L |
| LDH | >600 U/L |

Score ≥3 at 48 hours = severe pancreatitis → ICU/HDU management.

### Management

**Mild acute pancreatitis (majority of cases):**
- IV fluid resuscitation — aggressive early hydration (250–500 mL/hour crystalloid, Hartmann's preferred over normal saline — less acidotic) for the first 24–48 hours
- Analgesia — IV morphine or pethidine (pain is severe; patient-controlled analgesia)
- Nil by mouth initially; early oral feeding as tolerated (do not withhold nutrition; gut rest is not beneficial)
- No antibiotics unless proven infection

**Severe acute pancreatitis:**
- ICU/HDU admission with organ support
- **Nasojejunal (NJ) enteral nutrition** — early enteral feeding (within 24–48 hours) reduces complications, bacterial translocation, and mortality compared with TPN
- **No prophylactic antibiotics** — not recommended (no mortality benefit, increases resistance)
- **ERCP within 24–72 hours** if associated cholangitis or biliary obstruction (jaundice + dilated CBD on USS)
- Drainage/debridement of infected pancreatic necrosis (if develops) — step-up approach (percutaneous/endoscopic drainage before surgical necrosectomy)

**Gallstone pancreatitis**: **Cholecystectomy during the same admission** (for mild pancreatitis) or within 2–4 weeks of discharge (for moderate-severe) — reduces recurrence from ~30% without surgery to ~3%. Delaying cholecystectomy is the most important preventable cause of recurrent gallstone pancreatitis.

## Chronic Pancreatitis

### Pathophysiology

Chronic pancreatitis is progressive, irreversible inflammation leading to fibrosis and destruction of the exocrine and eventually endocrine pancreatic parenchyma. Alcohol is the most common cause (~70–80%) — sustained pancreatic ductal hypertension and repeated episodes of acute-on-chronic inflammation drive fibrosis. Other causes: hereditary (CFTR, PRSS1, SPINK1 mutations), autoimmune, idiopathic, obstructive.

### Clinical Presentation

**Pain**: Chronic, debilitating epigastric pain radiating to the back — the dominant symptom. Pain is continuous, worse after meals (stimulated secretion against obstruction), and often leads to food avoidance and weight loss. In late disease, pain may paradoxically improve ("burnout") as the gland is completely destroyed.

**Exocrine insufficiency**: Steatorrhoea (pale, fatty, offensive, difficult to flush stools) when >90% of exocrine function is lost. Malabsorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) and fat causes weight loss, osteoporosis, night blindness, and coagulopathy.

**Endocrine insufficiency**: Insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus ("pancreatogenic diabetes" or Type 3c DM) — occurs late, when islets are also destroyed. Characteristically brittle and prone to hypoglycaemia (both insulin and glucagon are deficient).

**Complications**: Pseudocyst (walled-off pancreatic fluid collection — may cause pain, biliary or duodenal obstruction), splenic vein thrombosis (causes gastric varices), and significantly elevated risk of pancreatic adenocarcinoma (20× increased risk after 20 years of chronic pancreatitis).

### Management

- Complete alcohol and smoking cessation — the only interventions that slow progression
- High-calorie, low-fat diet with small frequent meals
- **Pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT)** — creon with all meals and snacks; titrate dose to normalise stool consistency and prevent steatorrhoea; fat-soluble vitamin supplementation
- **Analgesia**: stepwise — paracetamol → NSAIDs → weak opioids → strong opioids. Neuropathic agents (pregabalin, duloxetine) for neuropathic component. Coeliac plexus block for refractory pain.
- **Endoscopic or surgical drainage** for dilated pancreatic duct with obstruction
- **Insulin** for Type 3c diabetes — low doses, very sensitive; risk of hypoglycaemia

## Pancreatic Cancer

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) — the most common and most lethal pancreatic malignancy. 5-year survival is ~12%. Most present with locally advanced or metastatic disease.

**Risk factors**: Smoking (most important modifiable risk factor), obesity, diabetes (new-onset DM in an older patient with weight loss should prompt investigation), chronic pancreatitis, family history, hereditary syndromes (BRCA2, PALB2, Lynch syndrome).

**Presentation**: Painless obstructive jaundice (head of pancreas) is the classic presentation that allows potentially curative surgery. Body/tail tumours present later with epigastric pain, weight loss, and back pain — often metastatic at diagnosis. Courvoisier's sign — palpable, non-tender gallbladder in jaundiced patient — suggests malignant biliary obstruction (stone-induced gallbladder inflammation causes contracture, preventing distension).

**Investigation**: CT pancreas (triple-phase), MRCP, endoscopic USS (EUS — best for small tumours and tissue sampling), CA 19-9 (tumour marker — used for monitoring, not diagnosis).

**Management**: Whipple's procedure (pancreaticoduodenectomy) for resectable head-of-pancreas tumours. Only ~20% are resectable at presentation. Adjuvant chemotherapy (gemcitabine + capecitabine, or FOLFIRINOX). Palliative ERCP stenting for obstructive jaundice. Palliative chemotherapy for advanced disease (FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine + nab-paclitaxel).
## Complications

**Acute pancreatitis**: Pancreatic necrosis (sterile or infected), pseudocyst, abscess, splenic vein thrombosis, ARDS, AKI, DIC, hypocalcaemia (fat saponification), hyperglycaemia (islet damage).

**Chronic pancreatitis**: Malabsorption, diabetes, pseudocyst, splenic vein thrombosis, biliary stricture, and pancreatic adenocarcinoma.

## Clinical Insight

Cholecystectomy during the index admission for mild gallstone pancreatitis prevents recurrence. Without it, ~30% of patients will have a second episode within 6 weeks. With it, recurrence drops to ~3%. Every day that cholecystectomy is delayed in a patient fit for surgery is an unnecessary risk. This is not an elective procedure to schedule at some distant future date — it should be done before the patient goes home.

Early enteral nutrition in severe pancreatitis is not merely preferred over TPN — it actively reduces complications. The gut becomes the mediator of the systemic inflammatory response in severe pancreatitis through bacterial translocation. Feeding via a nasojejunal tube maintains gut barrier integrity and reduces infectious complications. TPN should be reserved for cases where enteral feeding is impossible.

New-onset diabetes in a patient over 50 with weight loss and upper abdominal discomfort requires pancreatic imaging. Type 3c pancreatogenic diabetes can be the presenting feature of pancreatic adenocarcinoma. A diagnosis of new type 2 diabetes in this setting without imaging is an incomplete workup.
