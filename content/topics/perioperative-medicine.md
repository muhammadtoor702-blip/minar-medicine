## Overview

Perioperative medicine is the specialty focused on optimising patients before, during, and after surgery. As the surgical population ages and accumulates comorbidities, the perioperative physician has become the gatekeeper between a patient's medical risk and the surgical team's plan. The core task is not to say "yes" or "no" to surgery, but to quantify the risk, explain it to the patient and surgeon, identify modifiable risk factors, and reduce them before the knife is lifted. The most preventable perioperative deaths are cardiac — and the most effective cardiac risk reduction strategies are identifying patients at high risk, optimising their haemodynamics, and ensuring perioperative medications are handled correctly.

## Pathophysiology

### The Surgical Stress Response

Surgery triggers a systemic neuroendocrine stress response:
- **HPA axis activation:** Cortisol release → catecholamine surge → tachycardia, hypertension, peripheral vasoconstriction, hyperglycaemia (gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance)
- **Inflammatory response:** IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α release → fever, increased metabolic demand, potential systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) in major surgery
- **Coagulation activation:** Surgical trauma activates the clotting cascade → pro-thrombotic state; balance with anti-coagulation management

The magnitude of the stress response scales with surgical trauma — laparoscopic day case vs. major open abdominal surgery represent opposite ends of the spectrum. This is why the **procedure risk** is one of the two key variables in perioperative cardiac risk assessment (the other being the patient's functional capacity and risk factors).

### Mechanisms of Perioperative Cardiac Events

**Demand ischaemia:** Tachycardia + hypertension + fever + anaemia + increased myocardial oxygen demand from catecholamine surge exceeds the fixed supply through a stenosed coronary artery → myocardial infarction (Type 2 MI — not plaque rupture).

**Plaque rupture (Type 1 MI):** Pro-thrombotic and pro-inflammatory state of surgery can destabilise vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques → acute STEMI perioperatively (less common than demand ischaemia).

**Haemodynamic instability:** Hypovolaemia (inadequate preoperative fasting replacement + intraoperative blood loss), vasodilation from anaesthetic agents, and ACE inhibitor/ARB-mediated vasoplegia — the classic cause of intraoperative hypotension.

## Clinical Presentation — Pre-operative Risk Assessment

### Functional Capacity

**METs (metabolic equivalents):** The cornerstone of risk assessment. 1 MET = basal metabolic rate (3.5 mL O₂/kg/min). Poor functional capacity (<4 METs) = high perioperative risk.

| Activity | METs |
|---------|------|
| Can you walk on level ground at 4 mph? | 4 METs |
| Climb a flight of stairs or walk up a hill? | 4–5 METs |
| Run a short distance? | 6 METs |
| Play tennis (singles)? | 8 METs |
| Swim? | 8 METs |

**Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET):** Objective measurement of functional capacity. Key parameter: **anaerobic threshold (AT)** — the point at which aerobic metabolism is supplemented by anaerobic glycolysis (identified by the inflection point in CO₂ production relative to O₂ consumption on the V-slope plot). AT ≥11 mL/kg/min = good functional reserve = low operative risk. AT <8 mL/kg/min = high risk — consider whether the benefit of surgery justifies the risk, or whether risk modification (prehabilitation, medical optimisation) is possible. CPET is increasingly the standard for major elective surgery (colectomy, oesophagectomy, cystectomy).

### Revised Lee Cardiac Risk Index (RCRI)

Six independent predictors of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) perioperatively:
1. High-risk surgery (intraperitoneal, intrathoracic, suprainguinal vascular)
2. Ischaemic heart disease (prior MI, Q waves on ECG, positive exercise test, angina, nitrate use)
3. Congestive heart failure (history of, or signs on examination)
4. Cerebrovascular disease (prior TIA or stroke)
5. Diabetes mellitus requiring insulin
6. Serum creatinine >2 mg/dL (CKD)

**Risk:** 0 factors = ~0.4%; 1 factor = ~1%; 2 factors = ~2.4%; ≥3 factors = ~5.4% risk of MACE (cardiac death, non-fatal MI, non-fatal cardiac arrest).

### Active Cardiac Conditions — Mandatory Investigation and Delay

The ACC/AHA guidelines identify five active cardiac conditions that generally require investigation and management before elective non-cardiac surgery:
1. **Unstable coronary syndromes** (unstable angina, recent MI <60 days)
2. **Decompensated heart failure** (NYHA Class IV or worsening)
3. **Significant arrhythmias** (complete heart block, Mobitz II, ventricular arrhythmias causing symptoms, SVT with rapid rate, symptomatic bradycardia)
4. **Severe valvular disease** (severe aortic stenosis — valve area <1.0 cm², mean gradient >40 mmHg; severe symptomatic mitral stenosis)
5. **Recent stent placement** (drug-eluting stent — dual antiplatelet therapy for minimum 6–12 months before elective surgery; bare metal stent — minimum 1 month)

**Aortic stenosis:** Moderate AS (AVA 1.0–1.5 cm²) requires echocardiography to confirm current status and a careful risk-benefit discussion. Severe symptomatic AS (symptoms: syncope, chest pain, dyspnoea) = very high perioperative mortality — TAVR/SAVR before elective surgery should be considered. Our scenario patient has moderate AS (AVA 1.2 cm²) without symptoms — surgery can proceed with careful monitoring.

## Diagnosis — Pre-operative Investigations

**Routine investigations** should be targeted, not reflexive (NICE guidelines):
- ECG: patients with cardiac disease, hypertension, or age >70 for major surgery
- FBC: anaemia common in elderly, oncology — correct pre-operatively (iron IV if ferritin <30 ng/mL; consider ESA for haemoglobin <8 g/dL in elective surgery with expected blood loss)
- U&E/creatinine: AKI risk assessment; baseline for nephrotoxic anaesthetic drugs; renal failure patients need potassium and acid-base checked
- Coagulation: only if bleeding history, anticoagulant therapy, or liver disease
- Echocardiogram: new murmur, known valvular disease being re-evaluated, unexplained dyspnoea
- Lung function tests: COPD patients before thoracic surgery; not routinely needed for all patients

**Pre-operative haemoglobin optimisation:** Anaemia is an independent risk factor for post-operative complications and blood transfusion. NICE recommends iron therapy for all patients with pre-operative anaemia 4 weeks before elective surgery — IV iron works faster than oral (2–4 weeks vs 3 months) and is preferred when time is limited.

## Management

### Medication Management — The Critical Pre-operative Decisions

**Continue:**
- **Beta-blockers:** Do NOT stop perioperatively — abrupt withdrawal risks rebound tachycardia, hypertension, and MI. Continue at same dose. (Note: POISE trial showed initiating beta-blockers immediately before surgery increased mortality — so start before surgery only with careful titration over weeks, never acutely.)
- **Statins:** Continue — perioperative statin withdrawal is associated with increased cardiac events
- **Aspirin:** For primary prevention — stop 7 days before to reduce bleeding risk. For secondary prevention (prior MI, stent, high CV risk) — continue unless the surgical team has specific reason to stop (neurosurgery, intracranial aneurysm, ophthalmology with risk of sight-threatening haemorrhage)
- **Thyroid medication, Parkinson's medication, psychiatric medications:** Always continue

**Hold on the day of surgery (day of surgery = "day 0"):**
- **ACE inhibitors / ARBs:** Hold on the morning of surgery — these agents blunt the RAAS response to surgical stress, causing refractory intraoperative hypotension that is poorly responsive to vasopressors. Multiple meta-analyses confirm this increases renal injury and MACE. Restart 24–48h post-operatively when the patient is haemodynamically stable and eating/drinking.
- **Diuretics:** Hold on the morning of surgery (hypovolaemia risk)

**Hold before surgery with specific timelines:**
- **Metformin:** Hold 24–48 hours before surgery (risk of lactic acidosis if AKI develops perioperatively — iodinated contrast media and reduced renal perfusion perioperatively increase AKI risk). Restart when eating normally and renal function confirmed stable post-operatively.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin):** Hold **3–5 days before surgery**. Risk: **euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (eu-DKA)** — SGLT2 inhibitors increase ketone production; surgical stress increases glucagon → ketogenesis; the result is DKA at normal or near-normal glucose levels (glucose typically 110–140 mg/dL — deceptively "safe"-looking). The absence of hyperglycaemia means the diagnosis is missed until the patient is severely acidotic. Requires urgent insulin + dextrose. All diabetic patients need glucose monitoring perioperatively.
- **Oral anticoagulants (warfarin, DOACs):** Complex bridging decisions — see below.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide):** Hold 1 week (daily) or 2 weeks (weekly formulations) before surgery — delayed gastric emptying significantly increases aspiration risk despite standard fasting guidelines (undigested food has been found in the stomach of GLP-1 RA-treated patients fasting for >8 hours). If held, use standard ASA fasting guidelines.

**Insulin management:**
- Long-acting insulin (glargine/detemir): Continue at **80% of usual dose** on the evening before surgery (prevents ketoacidosis while reducing hypoglycaemia risk during fasting)
- Short-acting (mealtime) insulin: Omit while NBM
- Monitor glucose 2-hourly perioperatively; target 108–180 mg/dL (NICE)
- Variable rate insulin infusion (VRIII/sliding scale) for poor glycaemic control or prolonged NBM

### DVT Prophylaxis

**NICE risk assessment (Caprini model components):**
- Patient risk factors: age >60, obesity (BMI >30), active cancer, personal/family VTE history, thrombophilia, immobility, pregnancy/postpartum, oestrogen-containing contraception, inflammatory bowel disease, varicose veins
- Surgical risk factors: major surgery, abdominal/pelvic surgery, orthopaedic (hip/knee)

**Mechanical prophylaxis:** TED (thrombo-embolic deterrent) stockings + intermittent pneumatic compression (IPC) devices — start intraoperatively and continue until ambulant.

**Pharmacological prophylaxis:**
- Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH — enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily) for moderate-to-high risk patients from the night before or day of surgery
- **Extended thromboprophylaxis (28 days post-discharge)** is recommended for major abdominal or pelvic cancer surgery (NICE — evidence from multiple RCTs including ENOXACAN II)
- **Spinal/epidural anaesthesia timing:** LMWH must be held 12h before spinal/epidural insertion and 4h before removal (risk of epidural haematoma)

### Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS)

ERAS protocols are evidence-based multimodal perioperative care pathways that reduce complications and length of hospital stay.

**Key ERAS elements:**
- **Pre-operative carbohydrate loading:** Oral carbohydrate drink 400 mL (containing 50 g carbohydrate) up to 2 hours before induction (safe, reduces insulin resistance and protein catabolism). ERAS Society recommendation.
- **Clear fluid fasting: 2 hours; solid food fasting: 6 hours** (ASA/AAGBI consensus — long fasting is unnecessary and harmful)
- **Intra-operative:** Goal-directed fluid therapy (oesophageal Doppler/LiDCO to titrate fluids to cardiac output), normothermia (warm air blankets, warm fluids), short-acting anaesthetic agents, minimally invasive surgery
- **Post-operative:** Early oral nutrition within 4–6 hours (not "bowel rest"), opioid-sparing multimodal analgesia (paracetamol + NSAIDs + regional block), early mobilisation (day 0 post-surgery for laparoscopic colectomy), remove NG tube and urinary catheter within 24–48h

**Anastomotic leak (after colorectal surgery):** The most feared early complication. Typically presents on **post-operative days 3–5** (physiological fall in anastomotic healing — peak leak risk). Features: fever, tachycardia, raised WBC, elevated CRP (>150 mg/L at day 3 post-op), abdominal pain out of proportion, peritonism, change in drain output (faeculent/cloudy). CT abdomen/pelvis with IV and rectal contrast: extraluminal air or contrast = leak. Management: small contained leaks → IV antibiotics + CT-guided drain; large leak with peritonitis → return to theatre (Hartmann's procedure — resect + end colostomy; primary re-anastomosis rarely safe in a contaminated unwell patient).

## Complications

- **Cardiovascular:** MACE (MI, arrhythmia, cardiac arrest) — peak risk intraoperative to day 3; demand ischaemia commonest; Troponin rise in 8–18% after major non-cardiac surgery (MINS — myocardial injury after non-cardiac surgery)
- **Pulmonary:** Pneumonia (aspiration, secretion retention, atelectasis), pulmonary embolism (peak days 7–14), ARDS (after major surgery with sepsis or massive transfusion)
- **Renal:** AKI (commonest after cardiac surgery, nephrotoxic drugs, intraoperative hypotension, contrast media, sepsis); target: hourly urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h
- **Infectious:** Surgical site infection (SSI): superficial (1–3%), deep (0.5–1%), organ/space (anastomotic leak)
- **Thrombotic:** DVT, PE — despite prophylaxis, VTE occurs in 1–3% after major abdominal surgery
- **Glycaemic:** Perioperative hyperglycaemia (surgical stress response) worsens outcomes; perioperative hypoglycaemia (particularly if on insulin while NBM)

## Clinical Insight

The SGLT2 inhibitor euglycaemic DKA trap: a patient on empagliflozin presents 24h after major surgery with tachycardia, Kussmaul breathing, confusion, and a venous blood gas showing pH 7.19, bicarbonate 8 mEq/L, base excess −18 — but glucose is only 130 mg/dL. The nurse checks the glucose, sees "130 mg/dL — normal range," and reassures the team. The anaion gap is elevated at 24 mEq/L, ketones are 4.8 mmol/L, and the patient is in severe DKA. This is the euglycaemic DKA phenomenon: the SGLT2 inhibitor promotes urinary glucose excretion, keeping blood glucose deceptively normal while ketogenesis runs unchecked. Every diabetic patient on an SGLT2 inhibitor who develops metabolic acidosis post-operatively needs a blood gas + ketones, regardless of the blood glucose reading.

The ACE inhibitor on the day of surgery question is one of the most frequently examined perioperative pharmacology questions. The answer is always: **hold it on the morning of surgery**. The mechanism is important: surgical stress causes sympathetic activation and RAAS activation — if RAAS is blocked by an ACEi, the compensatory vasoconstriction cannot occur and profound vasodilation results. The anaesthetist will encounter refractory intraoperative hypotension that doesn't respond to standard vasopressors. Restart ACEi/ARB 24–48h post-operatively, not on day 1 (the patient is usually volume-depleted on day 1).

Prehabilitation — optimising patients before surgery — is an emerging area that genuinely changes outcomes. A patient with AT 7 mL/kg/min on CPET who completes a 6-week supervised exercise programme before elective colorectal surgery can increase AT to 10 mL/kg/min, shifting them from very high risk to moderate risk. Combined with smoking cessation (reduces pulmonary complications by 50% if stopped ≥8 weeks before surgery), nutritional optimisation, and anaemia correction, prehabilitation is the most patient-centred perioperative intervention available.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 71-year-old, CCS II angina, moderate AS (AVA 1.2 cm²), T2DM (metformin + empagliflozin), BMI 34, on bisoprolol, lisinopril. Listed for sigmoid colectomy.

**Step 1 — RCRI:**
- High-risk surgery (intraperitoneal) = 1 point
- Ischaemic heart disease (angina) = 1 point
- Diabetes on oral medication (not insulin) = 0 (insulin only = 1 point)
- RCRI = 2 → estimated MACE risk ~2.4%

**Step 2 — Functional capacity assessment:**
- CCS Class II angina = symptoms with moderate exertion (walking quickly, climbing stairs rapidly, walking uphill) — equivalent to approximately 4–6 METs
- This patient is borderline — CPET is indicated for major colorectal surgery to objectively define AT; if AT <11 mL/kg/min, consider prehabilitation

**Step 3 — Cardiac assessment:**
- Moderate AS (AVA 1.2 cm², asymptomatic): review echocardiogram (6 months ago — acceptable for elective surgery; if 1.0–1.5 cm² and asymptomatic, proceed with monitoring rather than delay)
- Discuss with cardiologist whether current AS severity warrants pre-operative TAVR/SAVR — given AVA 1.2 cm² (moderate, not severe per ACC/AHA criteria of <1.0 cm²) and asymptomatic, generally proceed to surgery with careful haemodynamic monitoring

**Step 4 — Medication management:**
- **Bisoprolol:** Continue at current dose — do NOT stop perioperatively
- **Lisinopril:** HOLD on the morning of surgery — restarted 24–48h post-op when haemodynamically stable
- **Metformin:** Hold 24–48h before surgery — restart when eating and renal function confirmed stable
- **Empagliflozin:** Hold **3–5 days before surgery** — risk of euglycaemic DKA; monitor ketones + gas perioperatively
- Glucose target perioperatively: 108–180 mg/dL (NICE); variable rate insulin infusion if needed

**Step 5 — DVT prophylaxis:**
- Major abdominal cancer surgery risk category: **extended prophylaxis with LMWH for 28 days post-discharge** (NICE/ESMO recommendation)
- TED stockings + IPC devices intraoperatively

**Step 6 — ERAS pathway:**
- Carbohydrate loading 400 mL up to 2h before induction
- Goal-directed fluid therapy intraoperatively (target: cardiac output, SVV)
- Early feeding post-operatively (day 0 or day 1)
- Multimodal opioid-sparing analgesia (paracetamol + NSAID if renal function allows + epidural)

**Exam trigger:** *Diabetic patient on SGLT2 inhibitor + major surgery* → hold empagliflozin 3–5 days pre-operatively. The distractor is missing this and allowing SGLT2 inhibitor continuation, resulting in euglycaemic DKA. A second trap is continuing the ACE inhibitor on the morning of surgery, causing refractory intraoperative hypotension.
