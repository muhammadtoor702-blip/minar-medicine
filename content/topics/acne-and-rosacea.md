## Overview

Acne vulgaris is the most common skin condition worldwide, affecting ~85% of adolescents and persisting into adulthood in ~40% of cases. It causes substantial psychological morbidity — depression and anxiety in acne patients rival those seen in conditions like epilepsy and diabetes. Rosacea is a distinct chronic facial inflammatory condition, affecting predominantly fair-skinned adults, with a different pathophysiology and treatment approach. Both conditions are frequently undertreated, with patients enduring years of avoidable suffering. **First Aid high-yield:** Know acne pathogenesis (4 factors), isotretinoin side effects (especially teratogenicity), and rosacea's key distinguishing features (no comedones, flushing, fair skin, older).

## Acne Vulgaris

### Pathophysiology

Four pathological processes interact to produce acne — **FA mnemonic: "FISH" (Follicular hyperkeratinisation, Inflammation, Sebum excess, Hormonal/bacterial)**:

1. **Sebaceous gland hyperactivity**: Androgen-stimulated sebum overproduction (most important driver of adolescent acne — explains onset at puberty and response to anti-androgens). Sebum provides a substrate for bacterial growth. DHEAS (dehydroepiandrosterone sulphate) from the adrenal gland drives early puberty acne; testosterone drives later adolescent acne.

2. **Follicular hyperkeratinisation**: Abnormal cornification of the follicular epithelium → impaction of desquamated cells and sebum → **microcomedone** formation. This is the **primary event** — all acne lesions begin as microcomedones.

3. ***Cutibacterium acnes*** (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*): Commensal of sebaceous follicles; proliferates in the anaerobic, lipid-rich environment of an obstructed follicle. Triggers innate immune activation via **Toll-like receptors (TLR-2)**, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, TNF-α, IL-8). **FA:** *C. acnes* uses sebum (triglycerides) as substrate; produces lipases that generate free fatty acids → further inflammation.

4. **Inflammation**: Neutrophil recruitment into the follicle → rupture → release of comedone contents into the dermis → intense inflammatory response → papule, pustule, nodule, or cyst.

**Cystic acne** results from deep dermal inflammation, often with foreign body granuloma formation. Scarring (atrophic ice-pick, rolling, boxcar, or hypertrophic/keloidal) reflects dermal destruction.

**Hormonal acne in women**: Adult female acne — worse premenstrually; **distribution along the jawline and chin** (androgen-sensitive area); often treatment-resistant. Suggests androgen excess or sensitivity. Investigate for **PCOS** (polycystic ovarian syndrome) if irregular periods, hirsutism, or hyperandrogenaemia — measure LH/FSH ratio, testosterone, sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), DHEAS.

**Neonatal acne**: Appears in the first weeks of life — caused by **maternal androgen stimulation** of neonatal sebaceous glands. Resolves spontaneously without treatment within weeks. Distinguish from infantile acne (onset 3–6 months, may persist longer, may require treatment).

### Clinical Presentation

**Lesion types:**
- Non-inflammatory: **open comedone** (blackhead — oxidised melanin, not dirt; the pore is open) and **closed comedone** (whitehead — pore is closed; the key to distinguishing acne from rosacea — rosacea has NO comedones)
- Inflammatory: papule (solid, <5 mm), pustule (purulent), nodule (>5 mm, solid, deep), cyst (fluctuant, deep, dermal)

**Severity classification:**
- Mild: comedones ± scattered papules/pustules
- Moderate: papules and pustules ± few nodules
- Severe: nodules, cysts, widespread inflammation; scarring

**Distribution**: Face (cheeks, forehead, nose, chin, jaw), upper chest, upper back — areas with the highest density of sebaceous glands.

**Acne-like eruptions that are NOT acne vulgaris — FA differential:**
- **Drug-induced acneiform eruption** (corticosteroids, lithium, iodides, anabolic steroids, anti-epileptics, gefitinib, mTOR inhibitors): usually **monomorphic papules**, **lacks comedones**, often affects unusual sites (forearms, trunk)
- **Rosacea**: no comedones; flushing; older patient; distinct triggers (see below)
- **Perioral dermatitis**: small papules around the mouth (and sometimes eyes); often from **topical steroid misuse** on the face; treat by withdrawing topical steroids + doxycycline
- **Gram-negative folliculitis**: sudden worsening of acne after prolonged antibiotic use — *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Proteus* folliculitis; treat with isotretinoin
- **Milia**: tiny white cysts from trapped keratin; no inflammatory component; not true acne
- **Sebaceous hyperplasia**: enlarged sebaceous glands in adults (especially on nose) — waxy, doughnut-shaped papules with central depression; associated with ciclosporin use

### Management

**Topical therapies:**

| Agent | Mechanism | Use |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| **Benzoyl peroxide (BPO)** | Antibacterial (bactericidal, not bacteriostatic), keratolytic | All grades; **reduces *C. acnes* resistance** when combined with antibiotics; first-line OTC; can bleach fabrics |
| **Adapalene (retinoid)** | Normalises follicular keratinisation, anti-inflammatory | Mild-moderate comedonal and inflammatory acne; apply at night; teratogenic — avoid in pregnancy |
| **Tretinoin (retinoid)** | More potent than adapalene | Moderate acne; teratogenic |
| **Topical antibiotics** (clindamycin, erythromycin) | Anti-*C. acnes*, anti-inflammatory | **Always combine with BPO to prevent resistance**; do not use as monotherapy |
| **Azelaic acid** | Anti-inflammatory, mild antibacterial, anti-pigmentary | Safe in pregnancy; also treats post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation |
| **Topical dapsone** | Anti-inflammatory | Useful in adult female acne; less UK use |

**Combination therapy**: BPO + adapalene (Epiduo) or BPO + antibiotic + retinoid — more effective than monotherapy.

**Oral antibiotics** (moderate-severe inflammatory acne):
- **Lymecycline 408 mg OD** or **doxycycline 100 mg OD** — first-line. Use for **maximum 3 months** (after which antibiotics provide no further benefit and resistance risk increases). **Always combine with topical BPO** to prevent resistance.
- Never use oral antibiotics as monotherapy.
- Avoid tetracyclines in pregnancy, children <12, and breastfeeding.
- Erythromycin (safer in pregnancy) but high *C. acnes* resistance rates.

**Hormonal therapy (women):**
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP)** — second-line for adult female acne; **anti-androgenic COCPs** (co-cyprindiol/Dianette, or pills containing drospirenone/norgestimate) are most effective. Co-cyprindiol is licensed for acne in the UK.
- **Spironolactone 50–100 mg OD** — off-label but highly effective for adult female acne; anti-androgen (blocks androgen receptors in sebaceous glands); not for pregnancy (teratogenic in males). NICE supports use.

**Isotretinoin (oral retinoid — the most effective treatment for acne):**

Isotretinoin is a vitamin A derivative that targets **all four pathological factors** in acne: reduces sebum production by ~**90%** (most important effect), normalises follicular keratinisation, reduces *C. acnes* colonisation, and has direct anti-inflammatory effects.

**Indications:**
- Severe nodulo-cystic acne
- Moderate acne with scarring
- Acne significantly impacting psychological wellbeing
- Failure of ≥2 courses of oral antibiotics

**Side effects — FA high-yield (must know ALL of these):**
- **Teratogenicity** — absolute contraindication in pregnancy; severe risk of foetal malformations (ear, CNS, cardiovascular). All patients of childbearing potential must use two forms of contraception and participate in the **Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP)**. Monthly pregnancy tests mandatory. **FA:** isotretinoin → retinoic acid embryopathy (microtia, CNS malformations, cardiac defects)
- Dry lips (universal — use lip balm throughout); dry skin, dry eyes (advise against contact lens use)
- Elevated **triglycerides** (monitor monthly initially) — stop if >10 mmol/L (>885 mg/dL)
- Elevated LFTs (monitor monthly)
- Musculoskeletal pain — particularly in high-impact athletes; reduce dose or suspend
- **Mood and depression**: Black box warning (FDA); evidence is contested — but screen for and monitor mood throughout. Acne itself causes depression; isotretinoin-induced depression is controversial but duty of care requires monitoring.
- Photosensitivity — advise high-SPF sunscreen
- Night vision impairment (rare)
- Epistaxis — drying of nasal mucosa
- **Hypercholesterolaemia** (in addition to hypertriglyceridaemia)

**Monitoring on isotretinoin**: Monthly blood tests (FBC, LFTs, lipids, pregnancy test). Clinical review monthly.

### Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation and Scarring

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) — brown discolouration after acne lesions; more prominent in darker skin types; treat with azelaic acid, vitamin C, niacinamide, chemical peels.

Atrophic scars (ice-pick, rolling, boxcar): microneedling, fractional laser, chemical peels, dermal fillers — refer to dermatology.

Hypertrophic/keloid scars: intralesional triamcinolone, silicone gel, compression therapy.

## Hidradenitis Suppurativa

**First Aid and OHCM high-yield:** Often confused with acne but is a distinct chronic inflammatory condition of the apocrine gland-bearing skin.

### Pathophysiology

Chronic, relapsing inflammatory disease of the **apocrine gland-bearing skin** (axillae, groin, perineum, inframammary folds, buttocks). The primary event is follicular occlusion (same as acne) → follicular rupture → intense inflammatory reaction involving apocrine glands → abscess formation, sinus tracts, scarring. **Not primarily an infectious process** (though secondary infection with *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Streptococcus* spp., and anaerobes occurs). Strong association with **smoking** and **obesity**; hormonal influence (worsens perimenstrually); TNF-α and IL-1β are key cytokines.

### Clinical Features

- **Hurley stage I**: Abscess formation, single or multiple — no sinus tracts or scarring
- **Hurley stage II**: Recurrent abscesses with sinus tract formation and scarring — separated by normal-appearing skin
- **Hurley stage III**: Diffuse involvement with multiple interconnected sinus tracts and scarring — "rope-like" indurated cords

Distribution: axillae, groin, perineum, inframammary folds, inner thighs, buttocks.

Painful, tender, fluctuant nodules and abscesses that rupture and drain — then reform. The chronic course with sinus tracts and the specific distribution pattern distinguishes HS from acne.

**Associations**: Crohn's disease (shares TNF-α and IL-12/23 pathways), metabolic syndrome, polycystic ovarian syndrome, inflammatory arthritis.

### Management

- **Lifestyle**: Smoking cessation (most important modifiable factor), weight loss, loose-fitting clothing, avoid shaving or deodorant irritants in affected areas
- **Topical clindamycin** — for mild disease (Hurley I)
- **Oral antibiotics**: Combination clindamycin + rifampicin (the most evidence-based regimen) for 10–12 weeks; tetracyclines for less severe disease
- **Adalimumab** (anti-TNF) — licensed for moderate-severe HS; NICE-approved; significant efficacy
- **Secukinumab** (anti-IL-17) — newer licensed option
- **Surgical**: Wide local excision for advanced disease; CO₂ laser deroofing for sinus tracts; I&D is only a temporary measure

## Rosacea

### Pathophysiology

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory condition of the **central face**. Its pathophysiology involves:
- **Neurovascular dysregulation** — excessive flushing in response to triggers via transient receptor potential (TRP) ion channels and activation of the neuropeptide vasodilatory cascade
- **Innate immune activation** — elevated **cathelicidin antimicrobial peptides** (particularly LL-37) in the skin, driving neutrophil infiltration and angiogenesis
- ***Demodex folliculorum*** — a skin mite; found in higher densities on rosacea skin; role may be as a carrier of bacteria triggering innate immune activation. **FA:** Demodex mite density is increased in rosacea — this is the target of ivermectin cream

### Clinical Presentation

Fair-skinned adults (Fitzpatrick I–II), predominantly women (but **phymatous subtype more severe in men**). Mean age 30–50.

**Four subtypes:**

| Subtype | Features |
|---------|---------|
| Erythematotelangiectatic (ETR) | **Flushing**, persistent central facial erythema, telangiectasiae. No papules/pustules. |
| Papulopustular (PPR) | Central facial erythema + papules and pustules (resembles acne but **NO comedones**). |
| Phymatous | Skin thickening, irregular surface, sebaceous gland hyperplasia. **Rhinophyma** (bulbous nose — predominantly men). W.C. Fields appearance. |
| Ocular | Eyelid inflammation (**blepharitis**), conjunctival injection, foreign body sensation. Affects ~50% of rosacea patients; can precede skin disease. |

**Distinguishing rosacea from acne — FA critical:**
| Feature | Rosacea | Acne |
|---------|---------|------|
| Comedones | **Absent** | **Present** |
| Age | >30 (adults) | Adolescents/young adults |
| Flushing | Yes | No |
| Distribution | Central face only | Face, chest, back |
| Skin type | Fair (Fitzpatrick I–II) | Any |
| Pustule contents | Sterile | Keratin + bacteria |

**Triggers of flushing**: Hot drinks, spicy food, **alcohol (red wine)**, sun exposure, temperature extremes, exercise, emotional stress, certain skincare products (alcohol-based toners).

**FA:** Rosacea that looks like acne — the key to diagnosis is the **absence of comedones**. If there are no blackheads or whiteheads, it's not acne.

### Management

**Trigger avoidance** — central to all subtypes. Sun protection (SPF ≥30 daily) is essential.

**Topical:**
- **Ivermectin 1% cream** (Soolantra) — targets *Demodex*; anti-inflammatory; **first-line for PPR**; apply once daily
- **Metronidazole 0.75–1% cream/gel** (Rozex) — anti-inflammatory; for PPR; apply twice daily
- **Azelaic acid 15% gel** — anti-inflammatory; useful for PPR and erythema
- **Brimonidine 0.33% gel** (Mirvaso) — alpha-adrenergic agonist; reduces erythema via vasoconstriction; works within 30 minutes; effects temporary (rebound erythema with prolonged use)

**Oral:**
- **Doxycycline 40 mg modified-release (Efracea)** — sub-antimicrobial dose; anti-inflammatory without antibiotic activity; **standard of care for moderate-severe PPR**
- Doxycycline 100 mg conventional dose — for severe disease or ocular rosacea

**Laser and light-based therapy:**
- **Pulsed dye laser (PDL)** or intense pulsed light (IPL) — for telangiectasiae and persistent erythema in ETR subtype; **most effective treatment for vascular component**
- CO₂ laser or electrosurgery — rhinophyma (phymatous subtype)

**Ocular rosacea**: Lid hygiene (warm compresses, eyelid scrubs), topical cyclosporin eye drops, oral doxycycline for moderate-severe disease.

## Complications

**Acne**: Psychological morbidity (depression, anxiety, social withdrawal — equal to epilepsy and diabetes in burden studies), permanent scarring (atrophic or hypertrophic), post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

**Isotretinoin-specific**: Teratogenicity, hypertriglyceridaemia (risk of pancreatitis if severe), raised LFTs, mood disturbance, musculoskeletal pain.

**Rosacea**: Rhinophyma (disfiguring nose enlargement — largely irreversible without surgery), ocular complications (corneal scarring, vision loss in severe ocular rosacea), psychological distress.

**Hidradenitis suppurativa**: Chronic pain, social isolation, depression (even higher psychological burden than acne), lymphoedema from scar contracture, squamous cell carcinoma in chronic perineal sinus tracts (rare).

## Clinical Insight

Isotretinoin is often the most appropriate treatment for moderate-severe acne, yet patients frequently experience years of inadequate antibiotic courses before it is offered. The criteria for prescribing are not limited to nodulo-cystic acne — any patient with significant psychological impact, persistent acne despite adequate treatment, or acne causing scarring should be referred for consideration of isotretinoin. Delayed treatment means delayed resolution and more extensive scarring.

The Pregnancy Prevention Programme for isotretinoin is not bureaucracy — it prevents teratogenic catastrophe. Every clinician prescribing isotretinoin must ensure the patient is enrolled, using two forms of contraception, and undergoing monthly pregnancy testing. The teratogenicity operates through retinoic acid receptor signalling in embryonic development — even brief exposure during organogenesis causes severe malformations.

Rosacea is frequently confused with acne, seborrhoeic dermatitis, or contact dermatitis. The **absence of comedones is the single most reliable feature distinguishing rosacea from acne**. A patient with facial spots and no comedones over the age of 30 should prompt consideration of rosacea — particularly if there is flushing, central face distribution, and fair skin. Prescribing a topical steroid for rosacea in mistake for another condition can cause severe steroid rosacea (periorificial/perioral dermatitis variant) that is very difficult to treat.

Hidradenitis suppurativa is chronically under-diagnosed — patients with recurrent "boils" in the axillae, groin, or perineum should be assessed for HS. The average delay from symptom onset to diagnosis is **7–10 years**. Early adalimumab prevents the progression to Hurley stage III, where surgical management is the only effective option.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** An 18-year-old man with 2 years of persistent facial spots despite OTC benzoyl peroxide. Inflammatory papules, pustules, and nodules on cheeks, forehead, and chest. Two large tender cysts on the jaw. Visibly self-conscious and socially withdrawn. Asking about isotretinoin.

**Step 1 — Classify severity: this is severe nodulocystic acne:** The lesion types here tell the story — the patient has all of: comedones (whitehead/blackhead basis), inflammatory papules and pustules, nodules (>5mm solid deep lesions), and **cysts** (fluctuant, deep dermal collections). Nodulo-cystic acne is by definition severe. Distribution across face and chest confirms sebaceous gland-rich areas. Two years of persistence despite OTC therapy = inadequate control. This is not mild acne that needs a topical retinoid — this patient needs escalation.

**Step 2 — Assess psychological impact — this alone justifies isotretinoin:** "Visibly self-conscious, avoiding social situations" describes significant psychological morbidity. Studies consistently show depression and anxiety in acne patients rival those in epilepsy and diabetes. The psychological burden is an independent indication for isotretinoin — this does not require nodulo-cystic acne to be the only criterion. Any patient with acne significantly impacting quality of life, who has failed adequate prior treatment, should be referred for consideration of isotretinoin.

**Step 3 — Is isotretinoin appropriate? Yes — and it should be offered promptly:** He meets multiple indications: severe nodulo-cystic disease, 2 years persistence, failed OTC BPO, significant psychological impact. Isotretinoin targets all 4 pathological factors (reduces sebum by ~90%, normalises follicular keratinisation, reduces *C. acnes*, directly anti-inflammatory). It is the only agent with potential to produce long-term remission. Prior oral antibiotic courses are not a prerequisite — the indications are met now.

**Step 4 — Counsel on side effects before prescribing (mandatory pre-prescribing discussion):** Explain all of the following: Dry lips (universal — use lip balm); dry skin, dry eyes; elevated triglycerides (blood tests monthly); elevated LFTs (blood tests monthly); musculoskeletal pain; photosensitivity (SPF 30+ sunscreen daily); mood monitoring (black box warning; screen at each visit — though evidence of causation is contested, duty of care requires monitoring). This patient is male — no Pregnancy Prevention Programme required, but if female, two forms of contraception + monthly pregnancy tests + PPP enrollment are mandatory before the first prescription.

**Step 5 — Baseline bloods and referral pathway:** Refer to dermatology — isotretinoin is prescribed by dermatologists in most UK settings (or by trained GPs in some areas). Before starting: fasting lipids (TG and cholesterol), LFTs, FBC, urine pregnancy test if female. These are checked monthly throughout the course. Usual course: 0.5 mg/kg/day initially, titrated to 1 mg/kg/day; duration typically 4–6 months (aiming for cumulative dose ~120–150 mg/kg).

**Exam trigger:** *Severe nodulocystic acne + psychological morbidity + failed prior treatment* → **isotretinoin** is the correct answer. Know the mechanism (targets all 4 acne pathological factors), the key side effects (teratogenicity, elevated TG/LFTs, depression monitoring, dry lips universal), and the monitoring protocol (monthly bloods + pregnancy test if female). The distractor is prescribing another course of oral antibiotics — antibiotics have a 3-month maximum; adding more without escalation is inappropriate for severe nodulocystic acne. The distractor for rosacea scenarios: **absence of comedones** = not acne = rosacea.
