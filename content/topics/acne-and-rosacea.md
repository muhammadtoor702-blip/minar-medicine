## Overview

Acne vulgaris is the most common skin condition worldwide, affecting ~85% of adolescents and persisting into adulthood in ~40% of cases. It causes substantial psychological morbidity — depression and anxiety in acne patients rival those seen in conditions like epilepsy and diabetes. Rosacea is a distinct chronic facial inflammatory condition, affecting predominantly fair-skinned adults, with a different pathophysiology and treatment approach. Both conditions are frequently undertreated, with patients enduring years of avoidable suffering.

## Acne Vulgaris

### Pathophysiology

Four pathological processes interact to produce acne:

1. **Sebaceous gland hyperactivity**: Androgen-stimulated sebum overproduction (most important driver of adolescent acne — explains onset at puberty and response to anti-androgens). Sebum provides a substrate for bacterial growth.

2. **Follicular hyperkeratinisation**: Abnormal cornification of the follicular epithelium → impaction of desquamated cells and sebum → microcomedone formation. This is the primary event — all acne lesions begin as microcomedones.

3. **Cutibacterium acnes** (formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*): Commensal of sebaceous follicles; proliferates in the anaerobic, lipid-rich environment of an obstructed follicle. Triggers innate immune activation via Toll-like receptors, releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, TNF-α).

4. **Inflammation**: Neutrophil recruitment into the follicle → rupture → release of comedone contents into the dermis → intense inflammatory response → papule, pustule, nodule, or cyst.

**Cystic acne** results from deep dermal inflammation, often with foreign body granuloma formation. Scarring (atrophic ice-pick, rolling, boxcar, or hypertrophic/keloidal) reflects dermal destruction.

**Hormonal acne**: Adult female acne — worse premenstrually; distribution along the jawline and chin; often treatment-resistant. Suggests androgen excess or sensitivity. Investigate for PCOS (polycystic ovarian syndrome) if irregular periods, hirsutism, or hyperandrogenaemia.

### Clinical Presentation

**Lesion types:**
- Non-inflammatory: open comedone (blackhead — oxidised melanin, not dirt) and closed comedone (whitehead)
- Inflammatory: papule (solid, <5 mm), pustule (purulent), nodule (>5 mm, solid, deep), cyst (fluctuant, deep, dermal)

**Severity classification (Global Acne Grading System or GAGS):**
- Mild: comedones ± scattered papules/pustules
- Moderate: papules and pustules ± few nodules
- Severe: nodules, cysts, widespread inflammation; scarring

**Distribution**: Face (cheeks, forehead, nose, chin, jaw), upper chest, upper back — the areas with the highest density of sebaceous glands.

**Acne-like eruptions that are NOT acne vulgaris:**
- Drug-induced acneiform eruption (corticosteroids, lithium, iodides, anabolic steroids, anti-epileptics, gefitinib): usually monomorphic papules, lacks comedones
- Rosacea: no comedones; flushing; older patient; distinct triggers
- Perioral dermatitis: small papules around the mouth; often from topical steroid misuse
- Gram-negative folliculitis: sudden worsening of acne after prolonged antibiotic use — *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Proteus* folliculitis; treat with isotretinoin

### Management

**Topical therapies:**

| Agent | Mechanism | Use |
|-------|-----------|-----|
| **Benzoyl peroxide (BPO)** | Antibacterial, keratolytic | All grades; reduces *C. acnes* resistance when combined with antibiotics; first-line OTC |
| **Adapalene (retinoid)** | Normalises follicular keratinisation | Mild-moderate comedonal and inflammatory acne; apply at night; avoid in pregnancy |
| **Tretinoin (retinoid)** | More potent than adapalene | Moderate acne; teratogenic |
| **Topical antibiotics** (clindamycin, erythromycin) | Anti-*C. acnes*, anti-inflammatory | Always combine with BPO to prevent resistance; do not use as monotherapy |
| **Azelaic acid** | Anti-inflammatory, mild antibacterial, anti-pigmentary | Safe in pregnancy; also treats post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation |
| **Topical dapsone** | Anti-inflammatory | Limited UK use |

**Combination therapy**: BPO + adapalene (Epiduo) or BPO + antibiotic + retinoid — more effective than monotherapy.

**Oral antibiotics** (moderate-severe inflammatory acne):
- **Lymecycline 408 mg OD** or **doxycycline 100 mg OD** — first-line. Use for maximum 3 months. Always combine with topical BPO to prevent resistance.
- Never use oral antibiotics as monotherapy.
- Avoid tetracyclines in pregnancy (<18 weeks), children <12, and breastfeeding.
- Erythromycin (safer in pregnancy) but high *C. acnes* resistance rates.

**Hormonal therapy (women):**
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill** — second-line for adult female acne; anti-androgenic COCPs (co-cyprindiol/Dianette or pills containing drospirenone/norgestimate) are most effective. Not a substitute for acne treatment but effective adjunct.
- **Spironolactone** 50–100 mg OD — off-label but highly effective for adult female acne; anti-androgen; not for pregnancy.

**Isotretinoin (oral retinoid — the most effective treatment for acne):**

Isotretinoin is a vitamin A derivative that targets all four pathological factors in acne: reduces sebum production by ~90%, normalises follicular keratinisation, reduces *C. acnes* colonisation, and has direct anti-inflammatory effects.

**Indications:**
- Severe nodulo-cystic acne
- Moderate acne with scarring
- Acne significantly impacting psychological wellbeing
- Failure of ≥2 courses of oral antibiotics

**Side effects:**
- **Teratogenicity** — absolute contraindication in pregnancy; severe risk of foetal malformations. All patients of childbearing potential must use two forms of contraception and participate in the Pregnancy Prevention Programme (PPP). Monthly pregnancy tests mandatory.
- Dry lips and skin (universal — use lip balm and emollient throughout)
- Dry eyes (advise against contact lens use)
- Elevated triglycerides (monitor monthly initially)
- Elevated LFTs (monitor monthly)
- Musculoskeletal pain — particularly in high-impact athletes; reduce dose
- **Mood and depression**: Black box warning; evidence is contested — but screen for and monitor mood throughout. Acne itself causes depression; isotretinoin-induced depression is controversial but the duty of care is to monitor.
- Photosensitivity — advise high-SPF sunscreen

**Monitoring on isotretinoin**: Monthly blood tests (FBC, LFTs, lipids, pregnancy test). Clinical review monthly.

### Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation and Scarring

Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH) — brown discolouration after acne lesions; more prominent in darker skin types; treat with azelaic acid, vitamin C, niacinamide, chemical peels.

Atrophic scars: microneedling, fractional laser, chemical peels, dermal fillers — refer to dermatology.

## Rosacea

### Pathophysiology

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory condition of the central face. Its pathophysiology involves:
- **Neurovascular dysregulation** — excessive flushing in response to triggers via transient receptor potential (TRP) ion channels and activation of the neuropeptide vasodilatory cascade
- **Innate immune activation** — elevated cathelicidin antimicrobial peptides (particularly LL-37) in the skin, driving neutrophil infiltration and angiogenesis
- **Demodex folliculorum** — a skin mite; found in higher densities on rosacea skin; role may be as a carrier of bacteria triggering innate immune activation

### Clinical Presentation

Fair-skinned adults (Fitzpatrick I–II), predominantly women (but more severe in men). Mean age 30–50.

**Four subtypes:**

| Subtype | Features |
|---------|---------|
| Erythematotelangiectatic (ETR) | Flushing, persistent central facial erythema, telangiectasiae. No papules/pustules. |
| Papulopustular (PPR) | Central facial erythema + papules and pustules (resembles acne but NO comedones). |
| Phymatous | Skin thickening, irregular surface, sebaceous gland hyperplasia. Rhinophyma (bulbous nose — predominantly men). |
| Ocular | Eyelid inflammation (blepharitis), conjunctival injection, foreign body sensation. Affects ~50% of rosacea patients. |

**Distinguishing rosacea from acne:**
- Rosacea: older patient, fair skin, no comedones, flushing is a feature, central face (cheeks, nose, forehead, chin)
- Acne: any age, comedones present, extends to chest and back, no flushing

**Triggers of flushing**: Hot drinks, spicy food, alcohol (red wine), sun exposure, temperature extremes, exercise, emotional stress, certain skincare products.

### Management

**Trigger avoidance** — central to all subtypes. Sun protection (SPF ≥30 daily) is essential.

**Topical:**
- **Ivermectin 1% cream** (Soolantra) — targets *Demodex*; anti-inflammatory; first-line for PPR; apply once daily
- **Metronidazole 0.75–1% cream/gel** (Rozex) — anti-inflammatory; for PPR; apply twice daily
- **Azelaic acid 15% gel** — anti-inflammatory; useful for PPR and erythema
- **Brimonidine 0.33% gel** (Mirvaso) — alpha-adrenergic agonist; reduces erythema via vasoconstriction; works within 30 minutes; effects temporary

**Oral:**
- **Doxycycline 40 mg modified-release (Efracea)** — sub-antimicrobial dose; anti-inflammatory without antibiotic activity; standard of care for moderate-severe PPR
- Doxycycline 100 mg conventional dose — for severe disease or ocular rosacea

**Laser and light-based therapy:**
- **Pulsed dye laser (PDL)** or intense pulsed light (IPL) — for telangiectasiae and persistent erythema in ETR subtype; most effective treatment for vascular component
- CO₂ laser or electrosurgery — rhinophyma (phymatous subtype)

**Ocular rosacea**: Lid hygiene (warm compresses, eyelid scrubs), topical cyclosporin eye drops, oral doxycycline for moderate-severe disease.
## Complications

**Acne**: Psychological morbidity (depression, anxiety, social withdrawal), permanent scarring (atrophic or hypertrophic), post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation.

**Isotretinoin-specific**: Teratogenicity, hypertriglyceridaemia, raised LFTs, mood disturbance.

**Rosacea**: Rhinophyma (disfiguring nose enlargement — largely irreversible without surgery), ocular complications (corneal scarring, vision loss in severe ocular rosacea), psychological distress.

## Clinical Insight

Isotretinoin is often the most appropriate treatment for moderate-severe acne, yet patients frequently experience years of inadequate antibiotic courses before it is offered. The criteria for prescribing are not limited to nodulo-cystic acne — any patient with significant psychological impact, persistent acne despite adequate treatment, or acne causing scarring should be referred for consideration of isotretinoin. Delayed treatment means delayed resolution and more extensive scarring.

The Pregnancy Prevention Programme for isotretinoin is not bureaucracy — it prevents teratogenic catastrophe. Every clinician prescribing isotretinoin must ensure the patient is enrolled, using two forms of contraception, and undergoing monthly pregnancy testing. Male patients also require counselling (though isotretinoin is not teratogenic through male seminal transmission, this is often misunderstood).

Rosacea is frequently confused with acne, seborrhoeic dermatitis, or contact dermatitis. The absence of comedones is the single most reliable feature distinguishing rosacea from acne. A patient with facial spots and no comedones over the age of 30 should prompt consideration of rosacea — particularly if there is flushing, central face distribution, and fair skin.
