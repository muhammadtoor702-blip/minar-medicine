## Overview

Poisoning accounts for over 200,000 emergency department attendances per year in the UK and is among the most common reasons for ICU admission in young adults. Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose is the most common cause of acute liver failure in the developed world and the paradigmatic example of a delayed-toxicity poisoning: the patient appears well in the first 24 hours while irreversible hepatocellular destruction proceeds, and by the time jaundice and coagulopathy appear, the window for treatment may have passed. The overarching principle in toxicology is that time-to-antidote determines outcome — N-acetylcysteine (NAC) given early is almost completely protective; given late, it may reduce but cannot reverse hepatic necrosis.

## Pathophysiology

### Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) Toxicity

Normal metabolism: paracetamol is 90% conjugated in the liver by glucuronyl transferase and sulfotransferase to non-toxic glucuronide and sulfate conjugates. A small fraction (~5%) is oxidised by CYP2E1 (and CYP3A4) to the reactive intermediate **NAPQI (N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine)**, which is immediately detoxified by conjugation with **glutathione** to a non-toxic mercapturic acid.

**Toxic doses:** NAPQI production overwhelms glutathione stores (typically at >7.5–10 g in adults, though threshold is lower in malnourished individuals, alcoholics, those on enzyme-inducing drugs). Depleted glutathione → NAPQI accumulates → binds covalently to hepatocyte proteins → mitochondrial dysfunction + oxidative stress → zone 3 (centrilobular) hepatocyte necrosis. Hepatotoxicity is evident biochemically at 24–36h, peaks at 72–96h. Kidney tubular damage (acute tubular necrosis) may co-occur.

**Risk-modifying factors (lower the treatment threshold):**
- Chronic alcohol use (induces CYP2E1 → more NAPQI; depletes glutathione)
- Enzyme-inducing drugs: rifampicin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St John's Wort (more NAPQI)
- Malnutrition, anorexia nervosa, chronic illness (depleted glutathione stores)

### Salicylate Toxicity

**Mechanism:** Salicylates uncouple oxidative phosphorylation in mitochondria → impaired ATP production + increased heat production. They directly stimulate the medullary respiratory centre → hyperventilation → **respiratory alkalosis** (the first and most prominent acid-base disturbance). Simultaneously: salicylate itself is an acid, and the metabolic derangement from uncoupled phosphorylation generates organic acids → **high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis** overlays. The classic result: **mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis** on ABG.

In children, the respiratory alkalosis is often obscured by the metabolic acidosis → net metabolic acidosis can predominate.

### Opioid Toxicity

Mu-opioid receptor agonism at supraspinal and spinal levels:
- **Respiratory depression** (principal cause of death): decreased respiratory rate and tidal volume; hypercapnia and hypoxia
- **CNS depression:** Sedation, coma
- **Miosis:** Pontine, not cortical — the **pinpoint pupils** + **respiratory depression** + **coma** triad is diagnostic
- **Peripheral vasodilation and bradycardia**

### Tricyclic Antidepressant (TCA) Toxicity

TCAs inhibit multiple cardiac and neurological channels:
1. **Sodium channel blockade (quinidine-like):** Slows phase 0 depolarisation → **prolonged QRS** (>100 ms = toxic, >160 ms = ventricular arrhythmia risk); risk of VT/VF
2. **Anticholinergic effects:** Tachycardia, urinary retention, ileus, dry skin, confusion, delirium
3. **Alpha-1 blockade:** Peripheral vasodilation → hypotension
4. **Antihistamine/GABA-A antagonism:** Sedation, seizures
TCA toxicity can progress from mild anticholinergic symptoms to cardiac arrest within 1–2 hours — patients who are alert on arrival can deteriorate rapidly.

### Beta-Blocker and Calcium Channel Blocker Toxicity

**Beta-blocker overdose:** Cardiac beta-1 receptor blockade → bradycardia, AV block, hypotension, heart failure. Insulin resistance (hypoglycaemia). CNS effects with lipid-soluble agents (propranolol, metoprolol): seizures, coma.

**CCB overdose:** Non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) → direct cardiac depression: bradycardia, AV block, hypotension. Dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine) → peripheral vasodilation predominates. Hypoinsulinaemia from reduced calcium-dependent insulin secretion → hyperglycaemia + hypo-insulinaemia (paradoxically, high-dose insulin is therapeutic).

## Clinical Presentation

### Toxidrome Recognition

| Toxidrome | Agent | Pupils | HR | BP | Temp | Key Features |
|-----------|-------|--------|----|----|------|-------------|
| Opioid | Morphine, heroin, oxycodone | Pinpoint | ↓ | ↓ | ↓ | Respiratory depression, coma |
| Anticholinergic | TCA, antihistamines, atropine | Dilated | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | Dry flushed skin, urinary retention, delirium, ileus |
| Cholinergic | Organophosphates, nerve agents | Pinpoint | ↓/↑ | ↓ | — | SLUDGE/DUMBELS |
| Sympathomimetic | Cocaine, amphetamine, MDMA | Dilated | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | Diaphoresis, agitation, seizures |
| Serotonin syndrome | SSRI + MAOi, tramadol | Dilated | ↑ | ↑ | ↑ | Triad: clonus + agitation + hyperthermia |

### Paracetamol Overdose — Stages

- **Phase I (0–24h):** Nausea, vomiting, malaise, or completely asymptomatic — a false sense of safety
- **Phase II (24–72h):** RUQ pain and tenderness (hepatic necrosis begins), rising ALT/AST, PT prolonging
- **Phase III (72–96h):** Peak hepatotoxicity — jaundice, coagulopathy, encephalopathy, AKI; the patient appears to be "getting worse" as the hepatocyte necrosis is maximal
- **Phase IV (4 days–2 weeks):** Recovery (if survived) or progression to acute liver failure requiring transplantation

### Salicylate Toxicity — Features

Mild-moderate (blood level 300–500 mg/L): tinnitus (earliest symptom), deafness, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis.
Severe (>700 mg/L or symptomatic): confusion, seizures, pulmonary oedema, coma. The fever from uncoupled phosphorylation can be prominent.
ABG: respiratory alkalosis + high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. In children: net metabolic acidosis.

### Organophosphate Toxicity

Irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase → accumulation of acetylcholine at all synapses.

**Muscarinic effects (SLUDGE/DUMBELS):** Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defaecation, GI cramps, Emesis; Diarrhoea, Urination, Miosis, Bradycardia/Bronchospasm/Bronchorrhoea, Emesis, Lacrimation, Salivation.

**Nicotinic effects (skeletal muscle):** Fasciculations, muscle weakness, paralysis (respiratory muscle paralysis is the cause of death).

**CNS effects:** Anxiety, seizures, coma.

## Diagnosis

**Paracetamol level at 4 hours post-ingestion:** The Rumack-Matthew nomogram plots paracetamol blood level against time post-ingestion. Treatment threshold: **≥150 mg/L at 4h** (or the equivalent on the 4h-to-treatment line at later time points). The "treatment line" descends from 200 mg/L at 4h to 6.25 mg/L at 15h. In high-risk patients (enzyme inducers, malnourished), some guidelines use a lower threshold (100 mg/L at 4h, i.e., the 50% lower line).

- Level cannot be interpreted before **4 hours** post-ingestion (absorption not complete)
- If time of ingestion is uncertain, treat if any possibility of toxic level

**Bloods (for paracetamol overdose):** LFTs (ALT, AST, bilirubin — rising from 24–36h), PT (first sign of synthetic dysfunction — rises before bilirubin in acute liver failure), creatinine (AKI occurs in severe cases), U&E, glucose (hypoglycaemia in fulminant hepatic failure), FBC, venous blood gas (lactate — >3 mmol/L in severe cases indicates poor outcome).

**Salicylate levels:** Obtain at presentation and at 2–4h (absorption can be delayed with enteric-coated formulations). Serum level >300 mg/L = significant toxicity; >700 mg/L = severe toxicity requiring haemodialysis. **Do not use the Done nomogram** (inaccurate) — base decisions on clinical status + serial levels.

**ECG:** Essential for TCA, beta-blocker, CCB overdoses. QRS >100 ms in TCA = high risk of arrhythmia/seizure.

## Management

### Paracetamol Overdose — N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)

NAC replenishes hepatic glutathione stores, allowing NAPQI detoxification. It is the only proven treatment for paracetamol hepatotoxicity and is almost completely protective if given within 8 hours.

**SNAP protocol (current UK standard, 2021):**
- **Bag 1:** 200 mg/kg NAC in 200 mL 5% dextrose over **60 minutes**
- **Bag 2:** 100 mg/kg NAC in 500 mL 5% dextrose over **16 hours**
- Total dose: 300 mg/kg over 17 hours (versus the old 3-bag regimen which was 300 mg/kg over 21 hours)

**Indications for NAC:**
- Paracetamol level at or above the treatment line on the Rumack-Matthew nomogram
- Staggered overdose (multiple doses over time) — treat if total dose >75 mg/kg regardless of level
- Unknown time of ingestion with any plasma paracetamol detected
- Severe overdose (>150 mg/kg or 75 g) regardless of level/time — start immediately while awaiting level

**NAC side effects:** Anaphylactoid reaction (urticaria, flushing, wheeze — occurs in ~10%, more common with initial high-rate infusion). Management: stop infusion, give chlorphenamine ± salbutamol nebuliser, then restart at slower rate. True anaphylaxis is rare. Note: this is NOT IgE-mediated — it is complement activation from the first rapid bag; hence the reason for giving the first bag slower (over 60 minutes instead of the original 15 minutes).

**King's College Criteria for liver transplantation referral (paracetamol):**
- **pH <7.3** (after resuscitation) — single criterion sufficient
- OR all three of: PT >100s (INR >6.5) + creatinine >3.4 mg/dL + grade III–IV hepatic encephalopathy

### Salicylate Poisoning

1. **Activated charcoal** 50 g if within 1–2 hours of ingestion (and airway protected)
2. **Urinary alkalinisation:** IV sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg bolus then infusion targeting urinary pH >7.5. Alkaline urine traps ionised salicylate in the tubular lumen, preventing reabsorption and increasing elimination by up to tenfold ("ion trapping").
3. **Fluid replacement** (large volumes needed — massive diaphoresis)
4. **Monitor potassium** (hypokalaemia worsens with bicarbonate; replace aggressively — urine alkalinisation requires adequate potassium)
5. **Haemodialysis:** Indicated for salicylate level >700 mg/L, renal failure, pulmonary oedema, confusion/coma, or clinical deterioration despite treatment. Haemodialysis removes salicylate directly and corrects acid-base and fluid abnormalities simultaneously.

### Opioid Overdose

**Naloxone (opioid antagonist — competitive mu receptor):**
- IV/IM/IN route: **0.4 mg IV, titrate to adequate respiration** (not full reversal — excessive naloxone precipitates acute opioid withdrawal: vomiting, aspiration, hypertension, pulmonary oedema)
- Repeat every 2–3 minutes until RR >12 and SpO₂ improving
- **Half-life of naloxone (~60–90 min) is shorter than most opioids** — re-narcotisation is common; monitor 4–6h minimum after reversal
- Naloxone infusion: if the patient required a large bolus dose, start a maintenance infusion at **2/3 of the effective bolus dose per hour**

### Tricyclic Antidepressant Overdose

1. **Sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg IV bolus** — the cornerstone of treatment:
   - Alkalinisation increases the protein-bound (inactive) fraction of TCAs
   - Sodium loading reverses sodium channel blockade
   - Target: serum pH 7.45–7.55, QRS narrowing
   - Continue boluses until QRS <100 ms
2. **Benzodiazepines for seizures** (diazepam or lorazepam)
3. **Intubation and mechanical ventilation** for GCS <8 or respiratory compromise
4. **Lipid emulsion therapy (Intralipid 20%)** for refractory cardiovascular toxicity
5. **AVOID:** Class Ia (quinidine, procainamide) or Class Ic antiarrhythmics — these worsen sodium channel blockade. Physostigmine — risk of seizures and cardiac arrest. Flumazenil — lowers seizure threshold.
6. Continuous cardiac monitoring; VT/VF may occur up to 24h after ingestion

### Beta-Blocker and CCB Overdose

**Medical management sequence:**
1. **Atropine** 0.5–1 mg IV for bradycardia (often insufficient but try first)
2. **IV calcium** (for CCB — and some beta-blocker toxicity): calcium gluconate 3 g IV bolus — reverses CCB-induced cardiac depression; less effective for the dihydropyridine CCBs
3. **High-dose insulin euglycaemic therapy (HIET):** Insulin 1 unit/kg IV bolus + 50% dextrose (50 mL) + potassium supplementation; maintenance infusion at 0.5–1 unit/kg/hour. Insulin improves myocardial glucose utilisation and calcium channel function — arguably the most evidence-based therapy for severe CCB toxicity. Mechanism: insulin promotes cellular glucose uptake, overriding the calcium-dependent impaired glucose utilisation in overdosed cardiac myocytes.
4. **IV lipid emulsion (Intralipid 20%):** 1.5 mL/kg bolus over 1 minute, then 0.25 mL/kg/min infusion — "lipid sink" hypothesis: lipid-soluble drugs partition into the lipid phase, reducing free drug available to bind cardiac channels
5. **Vasopressors** (noradrenaline, adrenaline) for refractory hypotension
6. **Extracorporeal support** (VA-ECMO) for refractory cardiovascular collapse

### Organophosphate Poisoning

1. **Decontamination:** Remove clothing, wash skin (protective gloves — secondary contamination risk to healthcare workers)
2. **Atropine (muscarinic antagonist):** Titrate until secretions dry up (SLUD symptoms resolve): starting 2–4 mg IV, doubling every 5–10 minutes — patients may require hundreds of milligrams. Atropine treats muscarinic effects (bronchospasm, bronchorrhoea, bradycardia) but NOT muscle paralysis.
3. **Pralidoxime (oxime — reactivates acetylcholinesterase):** 1–2 g IV over 15–30 minutes within the first 24–48 hours. **Ageing** occurs when the organophosphate-enzyme bond undergoes irreversible structural change (typically within 24–48h); after ageing, pralidoxime cannot reactivate the enzyme — administer early. Pralidoxime treats nicotinic effects (weakness, paralysis).
4. **Benzodiazepines** for seizures
5. **Ventilatory support** for respiratory muscle paralysis (the principal cause of death)

## Complications

- **Paracetamol:** Acute liver failure (requiring transplantation), AKI, cerebral oedema from hyperammonaemia, hypoglycaemia, metabolic acidosis, death
- **Salicylate:** Non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (capillary leak), cerebral oedema, hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia, metabolic encephalopathy
- **Opioid:** Aspiration pneumonia (obtunded patient with vomiting), hypoxic brain injury, rhabdomyolysis, non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema (particularly heroin)
- **TCA:** VT/VF, status epilepticus, aspiration pneumonia, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, prolonged QTc (risk of Torsades de Pointes after QRS prolongation resolves)
- **Organophosphate:** Intermediate syndrome (delayed muscle weakness at 24–96h from persistent acetylcholinesterase inhibition, distinct from acute cholinergic crisis — does NOT respond to atropine); OPIDN (organophosphate-induced delayed neuropathy, weeks later)

## Clinical Insight

The single most important concept in paracetamol overdose is that the absence of symptoms in the first 24 hours is NOT reassuring. Hepatocellular necrosis proceeds silently while the patient feels relatively well. The level at 4h, not the symptoms at presentation, determines treatment. A patient who says "I feel fine, I only took a few tablets" has a paracetamol level that tells you the truth. Do not discharge until you have the 4-hour level in hand and applied the Rumack-Matthew nomogram.

The SNAP protocol reduced the loading infusion duration from 15 minutes (which caused most anaphylactoid reactions) to 60 minutes — the rate of anaphylactoid reactions fell from ~20% to ~10%. The total dose is identical (300 mg/kg); only the infusion speed of the first bag changed. This illustrates a general principle: the concentration and rate of drug delivery matters as much as the total dose, particularly for drugs that activate complement or trigger mast cell degranulation.

In TCA overdose, the QRS width is your guide. QRS >100 ms = sodium bicarbonate now. QRS >160 ms = imminent VT/VF, get the crash trolley and give bicarbonate aggressively. The QRS narrows with alkalaemia and sodium loading. Do not waste time trying to identify the specific TCA — the management is the same for all.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 19-year-old, 32 × 500 mg paracetamol tablets (~16 g) + alcohol, 5 hours ago. GCS 12, HR 98, BP 108/72, SpO₂ 97%. Paracetamol level 180 mg/L at 5 hours.

**Step 1 — Calculate the dose and apply the nomogram:**
- Estimated dose: 32 × 500 mg = 16,000 mg = 16 g in a 58 kg woman = **276 mg/kg**
- This exceeds the toxic threshold (150 mg/kg) on dose alone — NAC indicated regardless of level
- Paracetamol level at 5 hours: 180 mg/L
- On the Rumack-Matthew nomogram at 5h: the treatment line is ~125 mg/L — **180 mg/L is above the treatment line → NAC indicated**

**Step 2 — Bloods:**
- Paracetamol level (done — 180 mg/L)
- LFTs, PT/INR, U&E, creatinine, glucose, FBC, venous blood gas (lactate, pH)
- At 5h, LFTs are likely still normal (hepatotoxicity peaks at 72–96h) — a normal ALT at 5h does NOT mean safe

**Step 3 — Start NAC immediately (SNAP regimen):**
- Bag 1: 200 mg/kg = 11,600 mg NAC in 200 mL 5% dextrose over 60 minutes
- Bag 2: 100 mg/kg = 5,800 mg NAC in 500 mL 5% dextrose over 16 hours
- Monitor for anaphylactoid reaction during Bag 1 — have chlorphenamine ready

**Step 4 — No role for activated charcoal:**
- 5 hours post-ingestion = absorption is complete; activated charcoal is only useful within 1–2 hours (before absorption)
- Alcohol co-ingestion increases GI motility — further reduces charcoal benefit

**Step 5 — Risk factor assessment:**
- Alcohol use: yes (co-ingestion) — alcohol induces CYP2E1 and depletes glutathione → treat even more urgently
- This patient is NOT low-risk; continue full NAC course
- Repeat LFTs + PT at 12h and 24h after starting NAC

**Step 6 — Psychiatric review before discharge:**
- All self-harm overdoses require formal psychiatric assessment before discharge
- Safe discharge only when: medically cleared (normal LFTs, normal PT at end of NAC) + psychiatric review completed + safety plan in place

**Exam trigger:** *Young woman + paracetamol level 180 mg/L at 5h + alcohol co-ingestion* → above treatment line → start NAC immediately using the SNAP regimen. The distractor is reassurance based on normal LFTs and near-normal observations — hepatotoxicity has not yet manifested and will peak at 72–96h. The level, not the symptoms, determines treatment.
