## Overview

Heart failure is a clinical syndrome in which the heart cannot pump sufficient blood to meet the body's metabolic demands, or can only do so at the cost of elevated filling pressures. It is not a diagnosis — it is the final common pathway of many cardiac conditions including ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, valvular disease, and cardiomyopathy.

Prevalence is 1–3% of the general population, rising to ~10% among patients over 70. Prognosis is poor — approximately 25–50% of patients die within 5 years of diagnosis, worse than most common cancers. When admission is required, 5-year mortality reaches 75%.

**Classification by ejection fraction** — this is the single most important distinction, because it drives treatment:

| Type | EF | Predominant Mechanism | Typical Cause |
|------|----|----------------------|--------------|
| HFrEF (reduced) | <40% | Systolic dysfunction — ventricle cannot contract adequately | MI, dilated cardiomyopathy |
| HFmrEF (mildly reduced) | 40–49% | Mixed or recovering | Variable |
| HFpEF (preserved) | ≥50% | Diastolic dysfunction — ventricle cannot relax and fill | Hypertension, diabetes, obesity, age |

**NYHA Functional Classification** — grades symptom severity and guides therapy intensity:

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Heart disease present but no symptoms from ordinary activity |
| II | Comfortable at rest; dyspnoea or fatigue on ordinary activity |
| III | Marked limitation; comfortable at rest but symptomatic on less than ordinary activity |
| IV | Symptoms at rest; any activity causes discomfort; bed-bound |

**High-output heart failure** — a distinct entity where cardiac output is normal or elevated but cannot meet massively increased metabolic demands. Causes: severe anaemia, hyperthyroidism, arteriovenous fistula, wet beriberi (thiamine deficiency), Paget's disease, pregnancy.

## Pathophysiology

When cardiac output falls, compensatory neurohormonal mechanisms are activated:

1. **RAAS** — reduced renal perfusion triggers renin → angiotensin II (vasoconstriction) → aldosterone (sodium and water retention → increased preload)
2. **Sympathetic nervous system** — increased heart rate and peripheral vasoconstriction (increased afterload); catecholamine release causes direct myocardial toxicity over time
3. **Ventricular remodelling** — myocyte hypertrophy, fibrosis, and chamber dilation in response to pressure and volume overload

These responses initially maintain cardiac output but are ultimately maladaptive. Progressive volume and pressure overload leads to further ventricular dilation, reduced contractility, and a self-perpetuating cycle of deterioration. This is the biological rationale for every pillar of heart failure pharmacotherapy — ACEi blocks RAAS, beta-blockers attenuate sympathetic activation, MRAs block aldosterone, SGLT2 inhibitors reduce volume and afterload through osmotic diuresis.

**Left heart failure** elevates pulmonary venous pressure → transudation → pulmonary oedema → dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND.

**Right heart failure** (from LVF, cor pulmonale, or pulmonary hypertension) elevates systemic venous pressure → peripheral oedema, raised JVP, hepatomegaly, ascites, hepatic congestion.

## Clinical Presentation

**LVF symptoms:** Exertional dyspnoea (earliest and most sensitive symptom), orthopnoea (quantify by number of pillows), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, nocturnal cough (may be productive of pink frothy sputum), wheeze ("cardiac asthma"), fatigue, cold peripheries, reduced exercise tolerance, nocturia.

**RVF symptoms:** Ankle and leg oedema (may extend to thighs, sacrum, and abdominal wall in severe cases), abdominal distension (ascites), nausea and anorexia (hepatic congestion, gut oedema), weight gain from fluid retention.

**Examination findings:**

| Finding | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| Elevated JVP | Elevated venous pressure; most reliable objective sign of fluid overload |
| Third heart sound (S3) | Ventricular volume overload; low-pitched, best heard with bell at apex |
| Fourth heart sound (S4) | Stiff, non-compliant ventricle; more common in HFpEF and hypertensive heart disease |
| Displaced, diffuse apex | LV dilatation (eccentric hypertrophy) |
| Bibasal crepitations | Pulmonary oedema; may be absent in chronic compensated HF |
| Pulsus alternans | Alternating strong and weak beats; indicates severe LV dysfunction |
| Peripheral oedema | Pitting; distribution depends on posture (ambulant: ankles; bed-bound: sacrum) |

**CXR — ABCDE mnemonic for LVF:**
- **A** — Alveolar oedema (bat-wing perihilar shadowing in acute, severe HF)
- **B** — Kerley B lines (septal lines — interstitial oedema in peripheral lymphatics)
- **C** — Cardiomegaly (cardiothoracic ratio >0.5 on PA film)
- **D** — Dilated prominent upper lobe vessels (upper lobe diversion — early sign of venous hypertension)
- **E** — Effusions (pleural; bilateral, right > left)

## Diagnosis

**NT-proBNP** is the key biomarker. Released by ventricular myocytes under pressure and volume stress. Highly sensitive — a normal NT-proBNP in an untreated patient makes significant heart failure very unlikely.

NICE thresholds:
- NT-proBNP **<400 pg/mL** — HF is unlikely; consider alternative diagnosis
- NT-proBNP **400–2000 pg/mL** — refer for specialist assessment and TTE within 6 weeks
- NT-proBNP **>2000 pg/mL** — refer urgently; specialist assessment and TTE within 2 weeks

Note: NT-proBNP is elevated by AF, AKI, sepsis, and age; it is reduced by obesity and nephrotic syndrome. Interpret in context.

**Echocardiography** is the essential investigation — confirms the diagnosis, determines EF, identifies the underlying cause (wall motion abnormalities, valvular disease, pericardial pathology), and guides treatment. No evidence-based pharmacological treatment for HFrEF should be started without a confirmed EF.

**Bloods:** NT-proBNP, FBC (anaemia worsens HF; target Hb >12 g/dL), U&E and creatinine (baseline before ACEi and diuretics; monitor closely throughout), LFTs (hepatic congestion), TFTs (hypothyroidism is a reversible cause), HbA1c, fasting lipids.

**ECG:** LVH, AF (common precipitant and complication), ischaemic changes, LBBB (predicts CRT benefit; QRS >120ms is the key threshold).

## Management

### Acute Decompensated Heart Failure (Medical Emergency)

- Sit the patient upright — reduces venous return and improves respiratory mechanics
- Oxygen to maintain SpO₂ >94%
- **IV furosemide** — the cornerstone of acute treatment. Give 2.5× the patient's usual oral dose IV (much better bioavailability acutely). Monitor urine output hourly and electrolytes daily.
- **GTN infusion** — if systolic BP >90 mmHg. Reduces preload (venodilation) and afterload. Reduces dyspnoea rapidly. Withhold if patient has taken PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil) within 24–48 hours.
- **CPAP or BiPAP** — for persistent hypoxia or respiratory distress despite oxygen and diuretics. CPAP reduces afterload, improves oxygenation, and reduces intubation rates.
- **Inotropes** (dobutamine) — only for cardiogenic shock or end-stage HFrEF refractory to all other measures. Have no mortality benefit and may cause arrhythmias.
- **Do not start or continue beta-blockers** during acute decompensation — negative inotropy can worsen cardiogenic shock. If the patient is already on a beta-blocker and is haemodynamically stable, continue it at a reduced dose; do not abruptly stop.

### Chronic HFrEF — Four Pillars of Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy

Evidence from landmark trials supports four drug classes that independently reduce mortality. All eligible patients should receive all four:

| Pillar | Drug Class | Example | Key Trial | Mortality Reduction |
|--------|-----------|---------|-----------|-------------------|
| 1 | ACEi or **ARNI** | Ramipril; sacubitril/valsartan | CONSENSUS; PARADIGM-HF | 16–40% |
| 2 | Beta-blocker | Bisoprolol 2.5–10mg OD; carvedilol; metoprolol succinate | MERIT-HF; COPERNICUS | 34% |
| 3 | Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA) | Spironolactone 25mg OD; eplerenone | RALES; EPHESUS | 30% |
| 4 | SGLT2 inhibitor | Dapagliflozin 10mg OD; empagliflozin | DAPA-HF; EMPEROR-Reduced | 25% |

**ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan):** Superior to ACEi monotherapy in PARADIGM-HF (sacubitril/valsartan reduced CV death and HF hospitalisation by 20% vs enalapril). Use in NYHA II–III who remain symptomatic on ACEi. Allow a **36-hour washout** from ACEi before switching — the combination risks severe angioedema. Start only when BP ≥100/60 mmHg and eGFR ≥30.

**Beta-blockers:** Start low and go slow — begin bisoprolol 1.25mg OD and increase every 2 weeks toward target dose (10mg OD). Only initiate when patient is euvolaemic (not acutely decompensated). The mortality benefit is proven for bisoprolol, carvedilol, and metoprolol succinate specifically — do not assume class effect.

**MRA:** Spironolactone 25mg OD reduces mortality by 30% in NYHA III–IV HFrEF (RALES). Eplerenone for post-MI HFrEF (EPHESUS). Monitor K⁺ and creatinine — stop or reduce if K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L or creatinine rises significantly. Avoid if eGFR <30. SE of spironolactone: gynaecomastia (switch to eplerenone).

**SGLT2 inhibitors:** Dapagliflozin 10mg OD reduces HF hospitalisation and cardiovascular death in HFrEF regardless of diabetes status (DAPA-HF). Works via osmotic diuresis, afterload reduction, and direct myocardial metabolic effects. Now a first-line recommendation in all EF <40% patients.

**Diuretics:** Furosemide 40–80mg/24h PO (increase as needed for symptom control; can use bumetanide 1–2mg if furosemide response is insufficient). Relieve congestion and improve symptoms but have **no proven mortality benefit** — use the lowest effective dose. SE: hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia, AKI, ototoxicity with rapid IV infusion. Add K⁺-sparing diuretic if K⁺ <3.5 mEq/L.

**Ivabradine:** If sinus rhythm with resting HR >70 bpm despite maximum tolerated beta-blocker, add ivabradine 5mg BD (reduces HR via If channel inhibition without affecting BP). Modest reduction in HF hospitalisation (SHIFT trial). Not for AF.

**Digoxin:** 125–250 mcg OD PO. Reduces HF hospitalisation in symptomatic HFrEF; no mortality benefit. Most useful for additional rate control in HFrEF + AF. Narrow therapeutic window — toxicity risk increases with hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, and renal impairment. Check levels >6h post-dose.

### Device Therapy

- **ICD** — for EF <35% after ≥3 months of optimised medical therapy; primary prevention of sudden cardiac death
- **CRT (cardiac resynchronization therapy)** — biventricular pacing for EF <35% + LBBB + QRS >130ms. Resynchronises LV contraction, improves EF, symptoms, and survival. Can be combined with ICD (CRT-D).
- **LVAD (left ventricular assist device)** — bridge to transplantation or "destination therapy" for refractory end-stage HFrEF. Blood is pumped continuously from LV apex to aorta; patient has no palpable pulse.
- **Cardiac transplantation** — for persistent NYHA III–IV despite all above. Contraindications include infection, PVD, eGFR <40 mL/min/1.73m², active malignancy, severe pulmonary hypertension, and significant comorbidities. Median survival >10 years with lifelong immunosuppression.

### HFpEF

No therapy has yet demonstrated mortality reduction in HFpEF. SGLT2 inhibitors show the strongest emerging evidence (EMPEROR-Preserved) and are increasingly used. Management focuses on:
- Controlling underlying causes: treat hypertension aggressively, control AF, manage obesity and diabetes
- Relieve congestion with diuretics
- Avoid beta-blockers unless there is another indication (coexisting CAD, rate control for AF)

### Monitoring on Treatment

- U&E, creatinine, and K⁺ 1–2 weeks after each dose increase of ACEi, ARNI, ARB, or MRA; at every clinical review
- A creatinine rise up to 30% after ACEi/ARNI is acceptable and expected. Rise >50% suggests renal artery stenosis or excessive diuresis — investigate.
- K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L: reduce or stop MRA and/or ACEi; recheck in 3–5 days
- Serial NT-proBNP at outpatient visits — a persistently elevated or rising level suggests residual congestion; adjust diuretics
- Repeat echo after 3–6 months of optimised GDMT — some patients recover EF substantially (especially tachycardia-induced, peripartum, or alcohol-related DCM)

**Common precipitants of acute decompensation (always ask):** medication non-compliance, excess dietary sodium or fluid, new AF or worsening arrhythmia, chest infection, new MI or ischaemia, NSAID use (cause sodium and water retention), anaemia, uncontrolled hypertension, new valvular disease, anaesthetic agents.

## Complications

- **Arrhythmias** — AF is both a cause and consequence of HF; ventricular arrhythmias cause sudden cardiac death in HFrEF
- **Cardiorenal syndrome** — progressive renal impairment from reduced perfusion and venous congestion backpressure on kidneys; diuresis can precipitate AKI
- **Hepatic congestion and cardiac cirrhosis** — chronic passive congestion causes hepatomegaly, elevated LFTs, and eventually cardiac cirrhosis in right HF
- **Thromboembolic events** — mural thrombus in severely dilated LV (especially if EF <25%); pulmonary and systemic embolism
- **Cardiac cachexia** — chronic hypermetabolism and gut oedema causing malnutrition; worsens prognosis independently
- **Sudden cardiac death** — from ventricular arrhythmias; accounts for ~50% of HFrEF deaths

## Clinical Insight

The most common precipitant of acute decompensation is medication non-compliance or dietary sodium excess. Ask about this before attributing any admission to disease progression — you cannot optimise therapy in someone who is not taking it.

Never start a beta-blocker in an acutely decompensated patient. Wait until euvolaemic — the negative inotropy of a beta-blocker in a patient whose sympathetic drive is the only thing maintaining cardiac output can precipitate cardiogenic shock. Stabilise first, then introduce bisoprolol at 1.25mg with very slow up-titration.

NT-proBNP is actionable. Titrating diuretics and neurohormonal blockade toward a target NT-proBNP — rather than managing symptoms alone — is associated with better outcomes and gives you an objective endpoint to work toward at each clinic visit.
