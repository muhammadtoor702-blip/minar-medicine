## Overview

Crystal arthropathies result from the deposition of crystals in joint spaces and periarticular tissues, producing acute inflammatory arthritis. Gout (monosodium urate crystals) and pseudogout (calcium pyrophosphate crystals) are the most clinically important. Vasculitides are inflammatory diseases of blood vessel walls, classified by vessel size, with distinct clinical presentations, autoantibody associations, and treatment approaches. Both groups are included here as high-yield rheumatological emergencies.

## Gout

### Pathophysiology

Gout results from hyperuricaemia (serum urate >6.0 mg/dL in women, >7.0 mg/dL in men) leading to monosodium urate (MSU) crystal deposition in joints, tendons, and soft tissues. Urate is the end-product of purine metabolism — humans lack uricase, the enzyme that would further metabolise urate to the more soluble allantoin (present in most other mammals).

**Causes of hyperuricaemia:**

| Mechanism | Causes |
|-----------|--------|
| Reduced renal excretion (~90%) | CKD, thiazide/loop diuretics, cyclosporin, low-dose aspirin, lead nephropathy, hypertension, metabolic syndrome |
| Increased production (~10%) | High purine diet (red meat, shellfish, offal), alcohol (beer — purines + fructose), fructose, myeloproliferative disorders, haemolysis, tumour lysis syndrome |

**Pathogenesis of the acute attack**: Supersaturated urate crystals deposit in cartilage and synovium. Physical disturbance (trauma, dehydration, alcohol — the latter also decreases urate excretion acutely) dislodges crystals into the joint space → macrophages phagocytose crystals → NLRP3 inflammasome activation → IL-1β release → acute inflammation → neutrophil influx → the most painful joint known to medicine.

**Why the first MTP joint?** The "podagra" of gout at the hallux MTP reflects the lower temperature at the extremities (cooler temperature reduces urate solubility → lower crystallisation threshold at the feet) and the mechanical trauma and pressure this joint endures.

**FA high-yield — Lesch-Nyhan syndrome**: X-linked recessive deficiency of **HGPRT (hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyl transferase)** — the salvage pathway enzyme that recycles purines. Without HGPRT, purines are degraded to urate → severe hyperuricaemia from childhood. Features: gout (early onset), **self-mutilation behaviour** (biting fingers and lips — pathognomonic), intellectual disability, spasticity, choreoathetosis. Treatment: allopurinol reduces urate but does not correct the neurological features. The FA buzzword is: young male + gout + self-mutilation = Lesch-Nyhan.

### Clinical Presentation

**Acute gout**: Abrupt onset (often waking from sleep), monoarthritis (first MTP most commonly — "podagra"), escalating to maximum intensity within hours. Exquisite tenderness — the weight of a bedsheet is unbearable. Periarticular erythema, warmth, and swelling. Fever may accompany severe attacks. Resolves spontaneously within 7–14 days.

Other commonly affected joints: ankle, midfoot, knee, wrist, small hand joints. Polyarticular gout occurs in established disease.

**Chronic tophaceous gout**: Untreated longstanding hyperuricaemia → tophi (deposits of MSU crystals in soft tissue) — in the helix of the ear, fingers, Achilles tendon, olecranon. Tophi are chalk-white subcutaneous deposits visible through the skin or as firm nodules. Chronic polyarthritis with joint destruction.

**Inter-critical gout**: Asymptomatic intervals between attacks — shortening with increasing urate burden.

### Diagnosis

**Synovial fluid analysis** is the gold standard. Aspirate during an acute attack → negatively birefringent, needle-shaped crystals under polarised light microscopy (yellow when parallel to the slow vibration axis of the compensator). This is pathognomonic — do not diagnose gout without it when possible.

**Serum urate**: Elevated in most cases but may be **paradoxically low during an acute attack** (inflammation reduces urate reabsorption and increases excretion acutely). A normal urate during an acute attack does not exclude gout — measure 2–4 weeks later.

**X-ray (chronic gout)**: Characteristic "rat bite" erosions with overhanging edges ("punched-out" erosions with sclerotic margins), preservation of joint space until late (distinguishes from RA), tophi calcification.

**MSU crystal deposition can be identified non-invasively** with dual-energy CT (DECT — detects urate by differential X-ray attenuation) or ultrasound ("double contour sign" — urate deposition on the surface of hyaline cartilage).

### Management

**Acute attack:**

- **Colchicine** (first-line if tolerated): 500 µg BD–TDS for 5–7 days. Inhibits microtubule polymerisation → impairs neutrophil migration into the joint. Diarrhoea is the dose-limiting side effect. **Low-dose regimen (500 µg BD) is as effective as high-dose with fewer GI side effects.** Avoid in eGFR <30.
- **NSAIDs** (naproxen, indomethacin, diclofenac): Alternative first-line — highly effective. Avoid in CKD, peptic ulcer disease, cardiac failure. Add PPI cover.
- **Prednisolone** (oral 30–35 mg/day for 5 days): When NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated — CKD, anticoagulation. Also intra-articular methylprednisolone for single joint involvement.
- **Anakinra** (IL-1 receptor antagonist) or **canakinumab** (anti-IL-1β): For refractory attacks or when all other options are contraindicated.

**Do not start or stop urate-lowering therapy during an acute attack** — changes in urate level mobilise crystals and can prolong or precipitate attacks.

**Long-term urate-lowering therapy (ULT):**

Indicated after: ≥2 attacks per year, tophi, renal stone, urate nephropathy, or any attack in CKD stage ≥2 or on diuretics.

- **Allopurinol**: First-line. Xanthine oxidase inhibitor — reduces uric acid production. Start at 50–100 mg daily; titrate monthly (maximum 900 mg). Target serum urate <6.0 mg/dL (or <5.0 mg/dL in tophaceous gout). Screen for **HLA-B*5801** before starting in South/East Asian populations (severe hypersensitivity — SJS/TEN risk).
- **Febuxostat**: More potent XO inhibitor. Second-line (cardiovascular safety concerns — FAST trial; caution in established CVD).
- **Prophylaxis during ULT initiation**: Start colchicine 500 µg OD or naproxen for 6 months — mobilised crystals cause acute attacks during the initial urate-lowering period.
- **Lifestyle**: Reduce alcohol, avoid purine-rich foods, increase hydration, switch thiazide to an alternative antihypertensive. **Losartan** is uricosuric — the antihypertensive of choice in gout with hypertension.

## Pseudogout (Calcium Pyrophosphate Deposition — CPPD)

Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPP) crystals deposit in articular cartilage (chondrocalcinosis) and synovium. On synovial fluid polarised microscopy: **positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped crystals** (blue when parallel to the slow vibration axis — the opposite of gout).

**Acute CPPD ("pseudogout")**: Most commonly affects the **knee** (most common joint), wrist, and MCP joints. Clinically resembles gout — acute monoarthritis, fever, high WBC in joint fluid. X-ray shows chondrocalcinosis (calcification of fibrocartilage — knee menisci, triangular fibrocartilage of the wrist, symphysis pubis).

**Associations**: Hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, haemochromatosis, hypomagnesaemia, hypophosphataemia, Wilson's disease, ageing. Screen for metabolic causes in any patient with CPPD under 55 or with multiple joint involvement.

**Management**: Same as gout for acute attacks — colchicine, NSAIDs, or intra-articular/systemic steroids. No equivalent of ULT — no treatment removes existing CPP deposits. Hydroxychloroquine or methotrexate for recurrent inflammatory CPPD.

### Gout vs Pseudogout — FA Crystal Comparison

| Feature | Gout (MSU) | Pseudogout (CPPD) |
|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| Crystal type | Monosodium urate | Calcium pyrophosphate |
| Shape | Needle-shaped | Rhomboid-shaped |
| Birefringence | **Negatively birefringent** | **Positively birefringent** |
| Colour (parallel to compensator) | **Yellow** | **Blue** |
| Most common joint | First MTP (podagra) | Knee (most common) |
| X-ray | Rat bite erosions, punched-out lesions | Chondrocalcinosis (menisci, wrist) |
| Serum marker | Elevated urate | No serum marker |
| ULT available? | Yes (allopurinol, febuxostat) | No |
| Metabolic associations | Obesity, alcohol, diuretics, CKD | Hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, haemochromatosis |

**FA mnemonic**: **"N**egative for **G**out" — gout crystals are **N**egatively birefringent. Pseudogout is the **P**ositive one.

## Vasculitis

### Classification by Vessel Size

| Size | Vasculitides |
|------|-------------|
| Large vessel | Giant cell arteritis (GCA), Takayasu arteritis |
| Medium vessel | Polyarteritis nodosa (PAN), Kawasaki disease |
| Small vessel (ANCA-associated) | GPA (Wegener's), MPA, EGPA (Churg-Strauss) |
| Small vessel (immune complex) | IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein Purpura), cryoglobulinaemic vasculitis |

### Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)

Granulomatous inflammation of large and medium vessels — predominantly the branches of the external carotid artery. Almost exclusively in patients >50 (mean age 70). Women > men.

**Clinical features**: New temporal headache (throbbing, unilateral or bilateral), scalp tenderness (pain on combing hair), **jaw claudication** (pathognomonic — pain on chewing from masseter ischaemia), visual loss (from anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy — AION), diplopia, amaurosis fugax.

**The most feared complication is permanent blindness.** The ischaemic optic neuropathy can be sudden and is irreversible once established.

**Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)** co-exists in ~50% of GCA: aching and stiffness in the shoulder and pelvic girdles; profound morning stiffness; elderly patient; ESR >50; excellent response to prednisolone.

**Investigations**: ESR typically >50 mm/hr (often >100); CRP markedly elevated; elevated platelets; normocytic anaemia. **Temporal artery biopsy** — gold standard; shows granulomatous inflammation with giant cells and fragmentation of the internal elastic lamina. Biopsy within 2 weeks of starting steroids (histological changes persist for 4 weeks).

**Management**: Start **prednisolone 40–60 mg daily immediately** upon clinical suspicion — do not wait for biopsy. Every day without treatment risks permanent blindness. Taper slowly (over 12–18 months minimum); relapse is common on rapid taper. IV methylprednisolone (500–1000 mg) if visual symptoms present — emergency.

**Tocilizumab** (IL-6 receptor inhibitor): Added to steroids for GCA — dramatically reduces relapse rate and cumulative steroid dose. Now standard of care for GCA alongside prednisolone.

### ANCA-Associated Vasculitis (AAV)

Small vessel vasculitis with pauci-immune necrotising inflammation (minimal immune complex deposition on immunofluorescence). ANCA (anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies) are the serological hallmark.

**cANCA/anti-PR3**: Granulomatosis with Polyangiitis (GPA, formerly Wegener's)
**pANCA/anti-MPO**: Microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) and eosinophilic GPA (EGPA)

**GPA**: Upper airway disease (sinusitis, nasal crusting, saddle-nose deformity, subglottic stenosis), lower airway disease (pulmonary cavities, haemoptysis), and **pauci-immune crescentic GN** (rapidly progressive — haematuria, proteinuria, AKI). The clinical triad: upper airways + lungs + kidneys.

**MPA**: Predominantly renal (pauci-immune GN) and pulmonary (alveolar haemorrhage). Less upper airway involvement than GPA.

**EGPA**: Asthma (often long-standing) + blood eosinophilia + vasculitis. Cardiac involvement (eosinophilic myocarditis — major cause of death), mononeuritis multiplex, skin purpura, renal disease.

**Investigation**: ANCA (cANCA/PR3, pANCA/MPO), renal function and urinalysis (proteinuria + haematuria + casts), CXR/CT chest, renal biopsy (fibrinoid necrosis and crescents — confirms GN; pauci-immune on IF).

**Management (AAV)**:
- **Induction**: Rituximab + high-dose prednisolone — now preferred over cyclophosphamide for most patients (RAVE and RITUXVAS trials). Cyclophosphamide + prednisolone — alternative, particularly for severe renal disease
- **Maintenance**: Rituximab 6-monthly or azathioprine — for 24 months minimum
- **Plasma exchange**: For severe renal failure (creatinine >5.7 mg/dL) or pulmonary haemorrhage — evidence remains debated (PEXIVAS trial: plasma exchange did not reduce death/ESRD)
- **Prophylaxis**: Co-trimoxazole — prevents PCP during immunosuppression; reduces relapse in GPA (trimethoprim reduces nasal *Staphylococcus* carriage — a relapse trigger)
## Complications

**Gout**: Tophi with skin ulceration, urate nephropathy, renal calculi, disability from chronic arthropathy, drug side effects (allopurinol hypersensitivity).

**GCA**: Permanent visual loss (anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy), aortic aneurysm (large vessel involvement in ~20%), stroke, iatrogenic complications from prolonged high-dose steroids.

**AAV**: ESRD from crescentic GN (requires dialysis and consideration for transplantation), relapse (~50% within 5 years), complications of immunosuppression (infection, malignancy — particularly bladder cancer with cyclophosphamide).

## Clinical Insight

The serum urate is unreliable during an acute gout attack. IL-1β drives acute-phase proteins, increases renal urate excretion, and reduces tubular urate reabsorption — causing a significant drop in serum urate during the acute episode. A normal urate on day 1 of a painful red first MTP joint does not exclude gout. Measure 2–4 weeks after the attack settles. If synovial fluid aspiration is performed during the attack, the crystals are there regardless of the blood result.

Giant cell arteritis is a visual emergency managed on clinical suspicion, not biopsy confirmation. The temporal artery biopsy is performed to confirm the diagnosis but must never delay treatment. Prednisolone started today will not negate the biopsy result — granulomatous changes persist histologically for up to 4 weeks after steroid initiation. In a patient with jaw claudication and visual symptoms, starting steroids tomorrow instead of today may be the difference between sight and blindness.

Rituximab has replaced cyclophosphamide as the preferred induction agent for ANCA-associated vasculitis in most patients. This matters clinically because cyclophosphamide carries substantial toxicity — haemorrhagic cystitis (mesna prophylaxis and adequate hydration required), bladder cancer (lifetime risk ~5%), gonadal failure, and secondary malignancy. In a young woman with GPA, rituximab is strongly preferred for fertility preservation. This is a shared decision-making conversation that should happen before induction begins.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 58-year-old man with hypertension, CKD stage 3, BMI 33. Twenty-four hours of excruciating pain, swelling, and erythema of the right first MTP joint — cannot bear the weight of a bedsheet. On bendroflumethiazide for 10 years. Drinks 30 units of alcohol weekly. Serum urate 9.7 mg/dL.

**Step 1 — Diagnose gout:** The clinical picture is classic. **Podagra** (acute monoarthritis of the first MTP joint) + urate 9.7 mg/dL (markedly elevated) = gout until proven otherwise. The bedsheet sign — exquisite allodynia where even the lightest touch is unbearable — is gout until synovial fluid analysis proves otherwise. Three independent precipitants are present:
- **Bendroflumethiazide** — reduces renal urate excretion (the most common drug cause of hyperuricaemia)
- **30 units of alcohol weekly** — alcohol (especially beer) increases purine production and acutely inhibits renal urate excretion
- **CKD stage 3** — impaired renal urate clearance

**Step 2 — Treat the acute attack (mind the comorbidities):**
- **NSAIDs are contraindicated** — CKD stage 3 makes NSAIDs high-risk for acute kidney injury
- **Colchicine** — check eGFR first. If eGFR ≥30: colchicine 500 µg BD for 5–7 days is safe. Diarrhoea is the dose-limiting side effect. If eGFR <30: avoid colchicine.
- **Prednisolone 30–35 mg/day for 5 days** — the preferred option if both NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated; also appropriate as first-line given CKD. Safe and highly effective.
- Do NOT start allopurinol during an acute attack — changing urate levels mobilises crystals and can prolong the attack.

**Step 3 — After the acute episode resolves, start urate-lowering therapy:**
- **Switch bendroflumethiazide to losartan** — losartan is uricosuric (increases renal urate excretion), provides equivalent antihypertensive effect, and does not worsen gout. An essential drug switch.
- **Allopurinol** — start at 50–100 mg daily, titrate monthly. Target serum urate **<6.0 mg/dL**. Dose-adjust for CKD (reduced starting dose, slower titration).
- **Prophylaxis** during ULT initiation: colchicine 500 µg OD for 6 months (or longer until urate is stable at target) — prevents mobilisation flares.
- Reduce alcohol and increase hydration.

**Exam trigger:** *Excruciating first MTP joint pain + bendroflumethiazide + CKD + elevated urate* → gout. Avoid NSAIDs in CKD. Use prednisolone for the acute attack. Switch thiazide to **losartan** (uricosuric antihypertensive) and start allopurinol once the acute attack has fully resolved. The distractor is starting allopurinol immediately or using NSAIDs in the context of CKD.
