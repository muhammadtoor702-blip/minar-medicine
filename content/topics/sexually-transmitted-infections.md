## Overview

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) represent a major public health burden — over 1 million new STIs are acquired globally every day. Many are asymptomatic, facilitating silent transmission and complications that may only manifest years later (infertility, ectopic pregnancy, cervical cancer). A systematic, non-judgemental approach to sexual health, including partner notification, contact tracing, and regular STI screening, is essential to controlling transmission.

## Chlamydia

### Pathophysiology

*Chlamydia trachomatis* is an obligate intracellular bacterium. Serovars D–K cause genital tract infection; serovars A–C cause trachoma (ocular); serovars L1–L3 cause lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV).

It infects columnar and squamo-columnar junction epithelium — cervix, urethra, rectum, pharynx, conjunctiva. Because it is intracellular, treatment requires agents that penetrate cells (azithromycin, doxycycline).

### Clinical Presentation

**Men**: Urethral discharge (mucopurulent or clear), dysuria, urethral discomfort. Epididymo-orchitis (unilateral scrotal pain and swelling — most common cause in men under 35). Reactive arthritis (formerly Reiter's syndrome: urethritis + arthritis + conjunctivitis, classically post-chlamydial).

**Women**: Most commonly **asymptomatic** (up to 70%). Mucopurulent cervical discharge, post-coital or intermenstrual bleeding, dysuria. Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID — ascending infection to endometrium, fallopian tubes, ovaries). Perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome — RUQ pain from perihepatic inflammation).

**Both genders**: Pharyngitis (usually asymptomatic), proctitis (anorectal pain, discharge, tenesmus — particularly in MSM with LGV).

### Diagnosis and Management

NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test) — gold standard; can be performed on self-taken vaginal swabs, urine (first-catch), rectal swabs, or pharyngeal swabs.

**Treatment**: **Doxycycline 100 mg BD for 7 days** (BASHH first-line — superior to single-dose azithromycin for genital chlamydia). Azithromycin 1 g single dose — alternative if compliance uncertain. LGV requires doxycycline for 21 days.

## Gonorrhoea

### Pathophysiology

*Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — Gram-negative diplococcus. Infects columnar epithelium at the cervix, urethra, rectum, pharynx, and conjunctiva. Fimbriae and outer membrane proteins (Opa proteins) mediate attachment and invasion. Produces beta-lactamase (penicillinase) — widespread penicillin resistance. Now also increasing ciprofloxacin and azithromycin resistance globally — **ceftriaxone is the only reliably effective agent in many settings.**

### Clinical Presentation

**Men**: Profuse purulent urethral discharge, marked dysuria. Epididymo-orchitis (less common than chlamydia in men <35, but gonococcal epididymitis tends to be more severe).

**Women**: Often asymptomatic. Cervicitis — mucopurulent discharge, friable cervix. PID (ascending infection). Bartholinitis.

**Both**: Pharyngeal gonorrhoea (usually asymptomatic — high rate in MSM due to receptive oral sex); anorectal gonorrhoea (asymptomatic or proctitis).

**Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI)**: Bacteraemic spread → triad of dermatitis (haemorrhagic pustules on acral skin), tenosynovitis (migratory polyarthralgia + tendon sheath inflammation), and septic arthritis. Blood cultures often negative (organism sequesters in joints). Gram stain of urethral swab.

### Diagnosis and Management

NAAT — most sensitive. Culture required for antibiotic sensitivity testing. Gram stain of urethral discharge in symptomatic men — Gram-negative intracellular diplococci (>95% sensitive).

**Treatment** (BASHH 2023): **IM ceftriaxone 1 g single dose** — first-line for uncomplicated genital, rectal, and pharyngeal gonorrhoea. No longer give azithromycin routinely alongside (BHIVA 2022 update). Disseminated disease: IV ceftriaxone 1 g daily for 7 days.

**Critically important**: Test of cure (TOC) — repeat NAAT 2 weeks after treatment. Culture at 72 hours for pharyngeal infection (NAAT may remain positive after cure due to residual DNA).

## Syphilis

### Pathophysiology

*Treponema pallidum* — obligate intracellular spirochaete; cannot be cultured in vitro. Crosses the placenta (congenital syphilis). Elicits a granulomatous immune response.

### Clinical Stages

**Primary syphilis**: Single (or multiple) painless, indurated, clean-based ulcer (chancre) at the site of inoculation — genitals, perianal, oral. Painless regional lymphadenopathy. Appears 9–90 days after exposure. Heals spontaneously in 3–6 weeks.

**Secondary syphilis** (6–12 weeks after primary): Systemic dissemination. Maculopapular rash classically involving **palms and soles** (virtually pathognomonic of secondary syphilis). Condylomata lata (flat, moist, infectious wart-like lesions on genitalia and perianal skin — distinct from condylomata acuminata of HPV). Snail-track oral ulcers, generalised lymphadenopathy, constitutional symptoms. All symptoms resolve spontaneously.

**Latent syphilis**: No clinical features. Early latent (<2 years from infection); late latent (>2 years). Contagious only in early latent.

**Tertiary syphilis** (years–decades after primary infection — now rare): Three forms:
- **Gummatous**: Granulomatous lesions of skin, bone, liver
- **Cardiovascular**: Aortitis → aortic root dilation → aortic regurgitation; coronary ostial stenosis
- **Neurosyphilis**: Tabes dorsalis (posterior column degeneration — lightning pains, loss of proprioception, Romberg positive, Argyll Robertson pupil); general paresis of the insane (dementia, personality change)

**Argyll Robertson pupil**: "Prostitute's pupil" — accommodates but does not react to light. Caused by midbrain lesion from neurosyphilis. Highly specific.

**Congenital syphilis**: Maternal transmission in pregnancy → saddle nose, Hutchinson's teeth (notched incisors), interstitial keratitis, saber shins (periostitis), sensorineural deafness — "Hutchinson's triad."

### Diagnosis

**Serological testing**: Two-step:
1. **Treponemal tests** (EIA, TPPA, FTA-ABS): Detect anti-treponemal antibodies; positive for life after infection; cannot distinguish active from past infection; used for screening.
2. **Non-treponemal tests** (VDRL, RPR): Detect cardiolipin antibodies; titres correlate with disease activity; use for monitoring treatment response (4-fold titre fall = adequate treatment). False positives in pregnancy, autoimmune disease, viral infections.

**CSF analysis** (for suspected neurosyphilis): Pleocytosis, elevated protein, reactive VDRL in CSF.

**Dark-field microscopy**: Direct visualisation of treponemes from primary chancre — not widely available.

### Management

**First-line**: **IM benzathine benzylpenicillin** — all stages. Single dose for early syphilis (primary, secondary, early latent). Three doses weekly apart for late latent. IV benzylpenicillin for neurosyphilis (2 weeks).

**Penicillin allergy**: Doxycycline 100 mg BD for 14 days (not for neurosyphilis or pregnancy).

**Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction**: Fever, rigors, and worsening of rash within 8–12 hours of first antibiotic dose — from rapid spirochaete lysis and cytokine release. Manage with paracetamol and reassurance. Warn patients before treatment.

## Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

### Overview

Ascending infection from the cervix to the endometrium, fallopian tubes, and ovaries. Most commonly caused by *C. trachomatis*, *N. gonorrhoeae*, or polymicrobial (anaerobes, *G. vaginalis*). A major cause of infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and chronic pelvic pain.

### Diagnosis

PID is a **clinical diagnosis** — no single test is diagnostic. BASHH guidance: minimum criteria to diagnose and treat empirically — **lower abdominal pain + cervical motion tenderness or uterine/adnexal tenderness in a sexually active woman with no other identifiable cause.**

Send NAATs for chlamydia and gonorrhoea, vaginal swabs, FBC, CRP. USS is not required for diagnosis but should be performed if tubo-ovarian abscess is suspected. Laparoscopy is gold standard but not routine.

### Management

**Outpatient (most cases):** IM ceftriaxone 1g single dose + oral doxycycline 100 mg BD for 14 days + metronidazole 400 mg BD for 14 days.

**Inpatient** (suspected abscess, systemic illness, surgical emergency excluded, pregnancy, failure of outpatient): IV cefoxitin + doxycycline, or IV clindamycin + gentamicin.

**Partner notification and treatment** — all sexual partners within 6 months. Abstain from sex until patient and partner have completed treatment.

## Herpes Simplex (Genital)

HSV-2 causes most genital herpes; HSV-1 is increasingly common. Primary infection: multiple painful vesicles → ulcers on genitalia, perineum, buttocks; systemic features (fever, malaise) in first episode; urinary retention possible from sacral nerve involvement. Recurrences (triggered by stress, illness, immunosuppression) are typically milder and shorter. Asymptomatic viral shedding enables transmission without recognised lesions.

**Diagnosis**: Swab from ulcer base for PCR (highly sensitive). Serology not reliable for acute diagnosis.

**Treatment**: **Aciclovir 400 mg TDS for 5 days** (or valaciclovir 500 mg BD for 5 days) for primary and recurrent episodes. Suppressive therapy (aciclovir 400 mg BD daily) for ≥6 recurrences/year.

## Human Papillomavirus (HPV)

HPV types 6 and 11 cause genital warts (condylomata acuminata). Types 16 and 18 cause cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) and cervical cancer, as well as anal, vulval, vaginal, penile, and oropharyngeal cancers.

**Genital warts**: Soft, flesh-coloured, cauliflower-like papules on genitalia and perianal skin. Treatment: podophyllotoxin 0.15% cream (patient-applied, home treatment), imiquimod (immune modulator), cryotherapy, or surgical excision. No treatment affects viral clearance; warts may recur.

**HPV vaccination**: Gardasil-9 (9-valent — covers HPV 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58). NHS programme: offered to all children aged 12–13 years and to MSM up to age 45 in sexual health clinics.

## Clinical Insight

Partner notification is not optional — it is a core component of STI management. A treated patient with an untreated partner will be reinfected immediately upon resuming sexual contact. Notification can be patient-facilitated (patient informs partners) or provider-facilitated (clinic contacts partners directly, with patient consent). For gonorrhoea and syphilis, active follow-up of partners is essential given the serious complications and resistance implications.

The first episode of genital herpes in pregnancy near term is a major obstetric emergency — the risk of neonatal herpes (encephalitis, disseminated disease) is high. This warrants immediate specialist referral, consideration of caesarean delivery, and neonatal prophylaxis with IV aciclovir. Recurrent herpes in the mother is far less risky — the baby has transplacentally acquired maternal antibodies.

The maculopapular rash of secondary syphilis involving the palms and soles is one of the most high-yield clinical findings in infectious disease. In any sexually active patient with a rash on the palms and soles — regardless of other symptoms — syphilis serology is mandatory. Secondary syphilis is "the great imitator" and can mimic almost any dermatological and systemic condition.
