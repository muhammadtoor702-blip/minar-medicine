# Headache

## Clinical Scenario

A 29-year-old woman presents to her GP for the third time in two months with recurrent severe headaches. She describes unilateral, throbbing pain lasting 12–18 hours, associated with nausea, vomiting, photophobia, and phonophobia, forcing her to lie in a dark room. The headaches are triggered by menstruation and stress. She has been taking ibuprofen and codeine almost daily to manage them. She is otherwise well, and her neurological examination is normal.

## Overview

Headache is one of the most common presenting complaints in medicine, affecting over 90% of the population at some point. The clinical challenge lies in distinguishing the vast majority of primary headaches (migraine, tension-type, cluster) — benign, episodic, and managed without investigation — from the small but critical minority of secondary headaches caused by serious underlying pathology. Missing a subarachnoid haemorrhage or meningitis is catastrophic; over-investigating benign headache is wasteful and anxiety-inducing.

## Primary Headache Disorders

### Migraine

The most clinically significant and disabling primary headache disorder, affecting approximately 15% of the population, with a 3:1 female predominance.

**Pathophysiology**: Migraine involves cortical spreading depression — a wave of neuronal depolarisation followed by sustained suppression propagating across the cortex — which triggers trigeminovascular activation, releasing neuropeptides (CGRP, substance P) that cause neurogenic inflammation of meningeal vessels, producing the characteristic pulsating pain. The aura reflects the spreading depression passing through eloquent cortex (visual cortex → visual aura; sensory cortex → paraesthesiae).

**Diagnostic criteria (ICHD-3):** ≥5 attacks lasting 4–72 hours with at least 2 of: unilateral, pulsating, moderate–severe intensity, worsened by routine activity; and at least 1 of: nausea/vomiting, photophobia + phonophobia.

**Migraine with aura**: 30% of migraineurs. Focal neurological symptoms developing over 5–20 minutes and lasting <60 minutes before or during headache. The visual aura (positive: scintillating scotoma, fortification spectra; negative: scotoma, blurred vision) is most common and is pathognomonic. Sensory aura (unilateral tingling spreading over minutes — the "march" distinguishes it from stroke onset) and speech aura also occur.

**Triggers**: Menstruation, stress, sleep disruption, alcohol (particularly red wine), certain foods (cheese, chocolate — inconsistent), dehydration, bright lights, strong odours.

**Management:**

Acute treatment:
- **Triptans** (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptan) — selective serotonin 5-HT₁B/D agonists; cause meningeal vasoconstriction and block trigeminovascular nociception. First-line for moderate-severe migraine. Take at headache onset (not aura). Contraindicated in ischaemic stroke, IHD, uncontrolled hypertension, and hemiplegic/basilar migraine.
- **NSAIDs** (ibuprofen, aspirin) + antiemetics (domperidone, metoclopramide) — effective for mild-moderate attacks; antiemetics also improve gastric motility and oral drug absorption
- Avoid opioids — ineffective for migraine, promote medication overuse headache

Preventive treatment (indicated if ≥4 attacks/month, attacks significantly impairing quality of life, or acute treatments are contraindicated/ineffective):
- **Propranolol** or **metoprolol** (beta-blockers) — first-line; avoid in asthma, depression
- **Topiramate** — effective but teratogenic (avoid in women of childbearing age without contraception); causes cognitive slowing ("dopamax") and kidney stones
- **Amitriptyline** — particularly useful if concurrent tension-type headache or insomnia
- **Candesartan** — ARB; good evidence, few side effects
- **Anti-CGRP monoclonal antibodies** (erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab) — highly targeted, highly effective; for chronic migraine or failure of ≥2 preventives; very expensive

### Tension-Type Headache

The most common headache disorder overall. Bilateral, pressing/tightening (non-pulsating), mild-moderate severity, not worsened by routine activity. No nausea, no vomiting, mild photophobia or phonophobia but not both. Lasts 30 minutes to 7 days.

Management: Simple analgesia (paracetamol, ibuprofen), stress management, regular sleep, physiotherapy for cervicogenic component. Avoid regular analgesic use (medication overuse). Amitriptyline for frequent episodic or chronic tension-type headache.

### Cluster Headache

The most severe primary headache disorder. Unilateral, excruciating orbital/periorbital/temporal pain, lasting 15–180 minutes, occurring in clusters (daily attacks for weeks to months, then remission). Associated ipsilateral autonomic features: lacrimation, rhinorrhoea, ptosis, miosis, conjunctival injection, eyelid oedema. Patients are restless and agitated (unlike migraine patients who prefer stillness).

Predominantly affects men (3:1). Smoking and alcohol during cluster periods are strong triggers.

**Acute treatment**: 100% oxygen (high-flow, 12–15 L/min via non-rebreather mask, for 15–20 minutes) — highly effective and unique to cluster headache. Subcutaneous or intranasal sumatriptan.

**Preventive**: Verapamil (first-line; regular ECG monitoring — risk of heart block). Short-course oral prednisolone to break a cluster. Lithium for chronic cluster headache.

### Medication Overuse Headache (MOH)

A critically important diagnosis. Regular use of acute analgesics or triptans on ≥10–15 days per month for more than 3 months transforms episodic headache into daily or near-daily headache — a paradoxical worsening caused by the treatment itself.

The mechanism involves central sensitisation from repeated analgesic use. Opioids, codeine, and combination analgesics (most common culprits), triptans, and NSAIDs can all cause MOH.

Treatment: Withdrawal of the overused medication — abrupt withdrawal (preferred for opioids with medical supervision; gradual for opioids if dependence established). Headache typically worsens for 2–3 weeks before improving. Add preventive treatment. This patient's daily codeine and ibuprofen use is causing MOH.

## Secondary Headaches — Red Flags

Secondary headaches account for <10% of presentations but include life-threatening conditions. Red flags mandate urgent investigation.

**The SNOOP mnemonic for secondary headache red flags:**

| Letter | Feature | Concern |
|--------|---------|---------|
| S | Systemic illness (fever, meningism, rash, weight loss) | Meningitis, SAH, malignancy |
| N | Neurological deficit (focal signs, papilloedema, altered GCS) | SOL, subdural, abscess |
| O | Onset: sudden, thunderclap ("worst headache of my life") | SAH |
| O | Older patient: new headache >50 years, progressive | GCA, SOL, malignancy |
| P | Postural change, Valsalva (cough, straining), Papilloedema | Raised ICP |

**Subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH):**

The thunderclap headache — sudden onset, maximal within seconds ("as if hit on the head"), the worst headache the patient has ever experienced. May be accompanied by brief loss of consciousness, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting. A sentinel headache (warning bleed) may precede the major bleed by hours to days.

Investigation: Non-contrast CT head first — identifies blood in the subarachnoid space in ~95% within 6 hours of onset. If CT is negative but SAH remains suspected: **LP at ≥12 hours after headache onset** (allows time for xanthochromia to develop — yellowish discolouration of CSF from haemoglobin breakdown; spectrophotometry is the gold standard). CT angiography identifies the underlying aneurysm. Management: neurosurgical referral, coiling or clipping of the aneurysm, nimodipine (reduces vasospasm).

**Giant cell arteritis (GCA) / temporal arteritis:**

A granulomatous vasculitis of medium and large vessels; almost exclusively in patients over 50 (mean age 70). New temporal headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication (pathognomonic — pain on chewing from masseter ischaemia), visual loss (risk of irreversible blindness from ischaemic optic neuropathy), thickened/tender/non-pulsatile temporal arteries. ESR and CRP are markedly elevated. Temporal artery biopsy confirms (but treat before biopsy result).

**Management**: High-dose prednisolone (60–80 mg/day) must be started immediately upon clinical suspicion — do not wait for biopsy. Visual loss, once established, is usually irreversible. Biopsy within 1–2 weeks (steroid use does not immediately negate histological findings). Treat for 1–2 years with gradual tapering.

**Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH):**

Raised ICP without underlying structural cause. Classic patient: obese woman of childbearing age. Headache, pulsatile tinnitus, transient visual obscurations, papilloedema, and if severe — permanent visual loss. LP opening pressure >25 cmH₂O with normal CSF composition. MRI may show empty sella, flattening of optic nerve head, or slit-like ventricles.

Treatment: Weight loss (most effective), acetazolamide (reduces CSF production), topiramate, repeated LP, CSF diversion (VP shunt or optic nerve sheath fenestration for visual threat).

## Diagnosis

**Primary headache is a clinical diagnosis** — no investigation is required if the history is classic and there are no red flags. Unnecessary imaging in low-risk headache increases patient anxiety, exposes to radiation, and identifies incidentalomas.

**CT head** for suspected raised ICP before LP, for thunderclap headache (exclude SAH), and for suspected space-occupying lesion or subdural haematoma.

**LP** for suspected meningitis (after CT if focal deficit or raised ICP signs) and for suspected SAH with normal CT (≥12 hours after headache onset).

**MRI brain** for chronic progressive headache, cluster headache (exclude secondary cause), atypical aura, or suspicion of structural pathology.

**ESR, CRP, temporal artery biopsy** for suspected GCA.

## Clinical Insight

The thunderclap headache is a diagnosis until proven otherwise. "The worst headache of my life" in a patient who has never had a headache like it requires an immediate non-contrast CT head, and if negative, a lumbar puncture at 12 hours. A normal CT does not exclude SAH — the sensitivity of CT drops from 95% at 6 hours to 50% at 1 week. Sending a patient home with analgesia after a thunderclap headache without LP is a preventable catastrophe.

Medication overuse headache is the most under-recognised and under-treated cause of chronic daily headache. The first step in managing any patient with near-daily headache is to ask precisely how often they take analgesics. More than 10–15 days per month means MOH until proven otherwise. No preventive therapy will work until the overused medication is stopped.

Giant cell arteritis is a visual emergency. The risk is ischaemic optic neuropathy causing permanent blindness. Any patient over 50 with a new temporal headache and jaw claudication should receive high-dose prednisolone that same day — before the ESR result, before the biopsy, and before the rheumatology appointment. One day's delay can mean lifelong blindness.
