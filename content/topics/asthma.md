## Overview

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory airway disease characterised by variable, reversible airflow obstruction and bronchial hyperresponsiveness. It affects roughly 8% of the population and remains a significant cause of preventable death — the majority of fatal and near-fatal attacks occur in patients with inadequately treated or under-recognised severe disease. The defining feature that distinguishes asthma from COPD is **reversibility**: airflow obstruction that varies spontaneously or with treatment.

---

## Pathophysiology

Asthma is fundamentally a disease of airway inflammation. In allergic asthma — the most common phenotype — allergen exposure in a sensitised individual triggers a type I (IgE-mediated) hypersensitivity response. Allergen-specific IgE on mast cell surfaces cross-links on re-exposure, triggering degranulation and release of histamine, cysteinyl leukotrienes, and prostaglandins. This produces the **early phase response** (minutes) characterised by bronchospasm, mucus hypersecretion, and oedema.

The **late phase response** (4–8 hours later) is driven by eosinophil recruitment and activation, producing further airway inflammation and a prolonged period of bronchial hyperresponsiveness. Repeated cycles of inflammation and repair lead to **airway remodelling** — subepithelial fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy, and goblet cell hyperplasia — producing partially fixed obstruction in chronic poorly controlled disease.

The result: increased airway resistance → air trapping → hyperinflation → ventilation-perfusion mismatch → hypoxaemia. In early acute attacks, patients hyperventilate, producing a **low PaCO₂** (typically 32–36 mmHg). A rising or normal PaCO₂ in an acute attack therefore means the patient is tiring — this is a pre-arrest warning.

**Peak expiratory flow and diurnal variation:** PEF is characteristically lowest between 2–4am — this "morning dipping" is so characteristic that it forms part of the diagnostic criteria for asthma. Diurnal variation >20% (highest minus lowest, divided by highest) is diagnostic of variable airflow obstruction. In clinical practice, asking patients about nocturnal wakening with wheeze or early-morning chest tightness captures this pattern without requiring formal PEF monitoring.

**Triggers:** Viral URTIs (most common trigger for acute attacks in all age groups), allergens (house dust mite, pet dander, grass and tree pollen, mould), exercise, cold air, aspirin and NSAIDs, beta-blockers, occupational sensitisers, emotional stress, and gastro-oesophageal reflux.

**Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD):** Present in 40–60% of asthmatics. GORD triggers asthma via the vagal reflex (oesophageal acid triggers reflex bronchoconstriction) and micro-aspiration of acid into the airways. Always ask about heartburn in difficult-to-control asthma — treating GORD can markedly improve asthma control.

**Occupational asthma:** Approximately 9–15% of adult-onset asthma is occupational in origin. Common sensitisers include isocyanates (spray painters, polyurethane manufacturers), flour and grain dust (bakers, millers), latex (healthcare workers), colophony (electronics soldering), and laboratory animals. Key diagnostic approach: **serial peak flow monitoring** at work and away from work on 4+ occasions — a pattern of improvement at weekends or on holiday strongly suggests occupational cause. Identification is critical: early removal from exposure can prevent disease progression; continued exposure leads to irreversible fixed obstruction.

**Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD / Samter's triad):** Asthma + aspirin/NSAID sensitivity + chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps. Results from shunting of arachidonic acid metabolism toward the leukotriene pathway when COX-1 is inhibited. Avoid all NSAIDs. Paracetamol is generally safe at standard doses.

## Clinical Presentation

**Symptoms:** Episodic wheeze (expiratory), breathlessness, chest tightness, and cough — typically worse at night and early morning, and triggered by identifiable factors. The combination of two or more of these symptoms with no alternative explanation makes asthma likely.

**Signs:** Expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiratory phase, use of accessory muscles in acute attacks. In severe attacks: inability to complete sentences, tachycardia, tachypnoea.

**Acute severity assessment:**

| Feature | Moderate | Severe | Life-threatening |
|---------|---------|--------|-----------------|
| PEFR | 50–75% best | 33–50% best | <33% best |
| Speech | Full sentences | Phrases only | Single words or mute |
| SpO₂ | >95% | 92–95% | <92% |
| Respiratory rate | <25/min | ≥25/min | Any very high rate |
| PaCO₂ | Low (hyperventilating) | Low | Normal or rising — pre-arrest |
| Other | — | — | Cyanosis, bradycardia, exhaustion, confusion, silent chest |

**A silent chest in acute asthma is a pre-arrest sign** — absent wheeze means no airflow, not improvement. The patient is unable to move sufficient air to generate wheeze.

**Differential for wheeze:**
- COPD — older smoker, fixed obstruction, reduced DLCO
- Acute pulmonary oedema ("cardiac asthma") — bibasal crepitations, raised JVP, cardiac history, CXR evidence of pulmonary oedema
- Anaphylaxis — allergen exposure, urticaria, angioedema, hypotension
- Foreign body aspiration — sudden onset, unilateral wheeze, no prodrome, often paediatric
- Vocal cord dysfunction — predominantly inspiratory stridor, normal spirometry, anxiety-related, paradoxical vocal cord motion on laryngoscopy

## Diagnosis

A diagnosis of asthma is based on characteristic symptoms combined with objective evidence of variable airflow obstruction. The NICE probability framework:

**More likely asthma:**
- Variable wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness, or cough
- Symptoms worse at night or early morning
- Symptoms triggered by exercise, allergens, cold air, viral infections
- Personal or family history of atopy (eczema, hayfever, food allergy)

**Less likely asthma (consider alternative):**
- Symptoms since early childhood without clear interval of normal
- Symptoms only with viral URTI; no interval symptoms
- Prominent dizziness, light-headedness, peripheral tingling
- Chronic productive cough without wheeze
- No objective evidence of airflow variability on testing

**Spirometry with reversibility testing:** Obstructive pattern (FEV1/FVC <0.7) with **significant reversibility** — defined as ≥12% AND ≥200 mL improvement in FEV1 after salbutamol 400 micrograms (4 puffs via spacer). This distinguishes asthma from COPD (where obstruction is fixed after bronchodilator).

**PEFR diurnal variability >20%** across multiple readings (ideally for 2 weeks) is diagnostic of variable airflow obstruction. Most useful for occupational asthma assessment.

**FeNO (fractional exhaled nitric oxide):** A biomarker of eosinophilic airway inflammation.
- >40 ppb (adults) — high likelihood of eosinophilic inflammation; supports asthma diagnosis and predicts good response to ICS
- 25–40 ppb — intermediate; interpret in clinical context
- <25 ppb — low; eosinophilic airway inflammation unlikely

FeNO should not be used in isolation — it is one component of the diagnostic workup.

**Bronchial challenge testing (methacholine or mannitol):** Used when spirometry and FeNO are normal but clinical suspicion remains high. Demonstrates bronchial hyperresponsiveness — a positive test is compatible with asthma but not specific to it.

**Allergy testing:** Skin prick tests or specific IgE (RAST) for allergen sensitisation — guides allergen avoidance and identifies candidates for immunotherapy or anti-IgE biologic therapy (omalizumab).

## Management

### Acute Asthma

**Immediate:**
- Oxygen — 40–60%, target SpO₂ **94–98%** (contrast with COPD where target is 88–92% — in asthma, give enough oxygen)
- **Nebulised salbutamol** 2.5–5mg immediately; driven by oxygen; repeat every 20 minutes or continuously in severe/life-threatening attacks
- **Nebulised ipratropium bromide** 0.5mg — add in severe and life-threatening attacks; synergistic bronchodilation; repeat 4–6 hourly
- **Oral prednisolone** 40–50mg (or IV hydrocortisone 100mg if unable to swallow) — start immediately; continue for 5–7 days; steroids work in hours, not minutes

**Escalation for life-threatening or inadequate response at one hour:**
- **IV magnesium sulphate 2g over 20 minutes** — direct smooth muscle relaxation; first-line escalation agent in life-threatening asthma; evidence of benefit in severe attacks
- **IV aminophylline** — used in refractory cases; narrow therapeutic window (tachyarrhythmias, seizures); always load with plasma level if patient is on oral theophylline to avoid toxicity
- **Heliox** (helium-oxygen mixture, e.g., 79:21) — reduces turbulent flow in obstructed airways; used as a temporising measure; oxygen concentration is lower, so not suitable if significant hypoxia
- **Senior anaesthetic review** for potential intubation — intubating a severe asthmatic is high-risk (hyperinflated lungs, high intrathoracic pressures, risk of pneumothorax); use as last resort

**Discharge criteria:** PEFR >75% predicted and sustained at 60 minutes, SpO₂ ≥94%, patient comfortable and able to use inhaler correctly, no nocturnal or early-morning symptoms.

**Mandatory before discharge:** Written personalised asthma action plan (with clear instructions on when to increase inhaler use and when to attend A&E), preventer inhaler prescribed and inhaler technique checked, GP follow-up within 48 hours, education on trigger avoidance. Failure to complete these steps is associated with higher readmission rates.

### Chronic Management — BTS/SIGN Stepwise Approach

| Step | Treatment | Notes |
|------|----------|-------|
| 1 | SABA (salbutamol) PRN | For infrequent intermittent symptoms; salbutamol 100–200 mcg (1–2 puffs) PRN |
| 2 | Add low-dose ICS | Beclometasone 200–400 mcg/day, or equivalent. Start even if only mild symptoms. |
| 3 | Add LABA as fixed ICS/LABA combination | **Never prescribe LABA without ICS in asthma.** Use budesonide/formoterol or salmeterol/fluticasone. |
| 4 | Increase ICS dose; add LTRA or LAMA | Montelukast 10mg OD; tiotropium (LAMA) for poorly controlled asthma at step 4 |
| 5 | Biologic therapy; consider oral steroids | Add on supervised by specialist. Aim for minimum effective dose of any oral steroid. |

**LABA without ICS is dangerous in asthma.** It is associated with increased risk of severe attacks and asthma-related death. Always prescribe as a fixed-dose combination (e.g., salmeterol/fluticasone, formoterol/budesonide, formoterol/beclometasone). A patient on a LABA alone is incorrectly prescribed — correct it.

**Biologic therapies for severe asthma (Step 5):**
- **Omalizumab** (anti-IgE) — for allergic asthma with elevated total IgE and sensitisation to a perennial allergen; SC injection every 2–4 weeks
- **Mepolizumab, benralizumab** (anti-IL-5/IL-5R) — for eosinophilic asthma with blood eosinophils ≥300 cells/µL; significantly reduce exacerbation frequency
- **Dupilumab** (anti-IL-4Rα) — for type 2 inflammatory asthma; also treats coexisting atopic dermatitis and chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps
- **Tezepelumab** (anti-TSLP) — for severe uncontrolled asthma regardless of phenotype; broadest eligibility

**Stepping up and down:** Always ensure adherence and correct inhaler technique before stepping up. Poor technique and non-adherence are the most common reasons for inadequately controlled asthma. Check technique at every consultation.

## Complications

- **Near-fatal and fatal asthma attacks** — most deaths are preventable with adequate treatment and recognition of severity; risk factors include prior near-fatal attack, recent hospital admission, poor adherence, failure to obtain or use rescue inhalers
- **Pneumothorax and pneumomediastinum** — from raised intrathoracic pressure during acute attack; look for subcutaneous emphysema
- **Hypokalaemia** — from high-dose beta-agonists (intracellular K⁺ shift) and systemic steroids; monitor electrolytes and replace
- **Airway remodelling** — irreversible fixed obstruction with chronic poor control; some patients develop an asthma-COPD overlap phenotype
- **Adrenal suppression** — from long-term high-dose ICS or repeated oral steroid courses; counsel patients never to stop steroids abruptly after prolonged courses

## Clinical Insight

A rising PaCO₂ in an acute asthma attack is a medical emergency. In acute asthma, patients hyperventilate and their PaCO₂ should be low — typically 32–36 mmHg. A PaCO₂ that is normal (40–45 mmHg) or elevated means the patient is exhausting their respiratory reserve and ventilatory failure is imminent. Call anaesthetics immediately and do not delay escalation waiting for further response to nebulisers.

The LABA-without-ICS rule is absolute. On examinations, any answer that involves prescribing a LABA as monotherapy in asthma is wrong. In COPD, LABA monotherapy is standard — this distinction is a frequent source of error. The SMART trial established that LABA monotherapy in asthma increases asthma-related mortality.

Many deaths from asthma occur in patients who were not recognised as severe. Patients with brittle asthma, frequent hospitalisations, prior intubation, or poor adherence are at highest risk. These patients need a specialist-led care plan, ready access to rescue medication, and a low threshold for emergency review. A written personalised asthma action plan is the single most evidence-based intervention for preventing severe attacks and should be given to every patient with diagnosed asthma.
