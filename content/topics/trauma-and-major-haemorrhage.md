## Overview

Trauma is the leading cause of death in people aged 1–45 years worldwide. The death distribution in trauma follows a trimodal pattern: immediate deaths (seconds to minutes — unsurvivable structural injury), early deaths (minutes to hours — the preventable deaths from haemorrhage and airway loss that modern trauma systems target), and late deaths (days to weeks — from infection, multi-organ failure, and ARDS). Modern trauma care is built around the premise that most preventable trauma deaths result from haemorrhage, and the entire system — from prehospital TXA to damage control surgery — is designed to stop bleeding quickly. The mantra is: haemorrhage control before everything else.

## Pathophysiology

### Haemorrhagic Shock

Blood volume in an adult = approximately 70 mL/kg (5 L in a 70 kg person). The physiological response to haemorrhage is a compensatory activation of the sympathetic nervous system — tachycardia, peripheral vasoconstriction, and redistribution of blood flow to core organs. The clinical classification of haemorrhagic shock:

| Class | Blood Loss | % Volume | HR | SBP | Pulse Pressure | RR | Urine (mL/h) | Mental Status |
|-------|-----------|---------|----|----|---------------|----|----|------|
| I | <750 mL | <15% | <100 | Normal | Normal/↑ | 14–20 | >30 | Normal |
| II | 750–1500 mL | 15–30% | 100–120 | Normal | ↓ | 20–30 | 20–30 | Anxious |
| III | 1500–2000 mL | 30–40% | 120–140 | ↓ | ↓ | 30–40 | 5–15 | Confused |
| IV | >2000 mL | >40% | >140 | ↓↓ | ↓↓ | >35 | Negligible | Lethargic/coma |

Class III and IV = haemodynamic instability; Class IV = immediately life-threatening — surgical control or interventional radiology within minutes.

**The lethal triad:** The combination of **hypothermia** (impaired clotting factor function), **acidosis** (lactic acidosis from tissue hypoperfusion impairs coagulation enzymes), and **coagulopathy** (dilutional from crystalloid resuscitation, consumptive from haemorrhage, hypothermia-worsened) is synergistic and self-perpetuating. Crystalloid resuscitation worsens all three: it dilutes clotting factors, is room temperature and cools the patient, and does nothing to restore oxygen-carrying capacity.

### Traumatic Brain Injury Physiology

**Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) = MAP − ICP**

Normal ICP: <15 mmHg. Normal CPP: >60 mmHg.

In TBI, primary brain injury (direct mechanical disruption) is irreversible. Secondary brain injury results from processes that occur after the initial impact:
- **Hypotension** (MAP falls → CPP falls → cerebral ischaemia)
- **Hypoxia** (SpO₂ <90% → worsens ischaemia)
- **Raised ICP** (cerebral oedema, haematoma expansion)
- **Hyperthermia, hypoglycaemia, seizures**

Management goal: **CPP ≥60 mmHg** (maintain MAP ≥60–70 mmHg, minimise ICP). This creates a direct conflict with damage control resuscitation's permissive hypotension — in TBI, permissive hypotension is absolutely contraindicated.

## Clinical Presentation — ATLS C-ABCDE

The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) framework provides a systematic approach:

**C — Catastrophic haemorrhage control (pre-ABCDE):** Any life-threatening external haemorrhage is addressed before airway — apply direct pressure, tourniquets for limb haemorrhage, wound packing for junctional wounds.

**A — Airway (with cervical spine protection):**
- Is the airway open? Gurgling = blood/secretions; snoring = tongue obstruction
- Jaw thrust (not head tilt in trauma — cervical spine injury until excluded)
- Suction, oral airway adjunct, nasopharyngeal airway
- Definitive airway (RSI + intubation) for GCS ≤8, inability to protect airway, or anticipated deterioration

**B — Breathing:**
- Look, listen, feel: asymmetric chest expansion, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation
- Immediately life-threatening thoracic injuries — the **ATOM FC** mnemonic:
  - **A**irway obstruction
  - **T**ension pneumothorax: absent breath sounds + tracheal deviation + haemodynamic compromise → immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line), then chest drain
  - **O**pen pneumothorax: sucking chest wound → three-sided dressing (allows egress, prevents air entry) → chest drain
  - **M**assive haemothorax: >1500 mL blood in pleural space; dull percussion + absent breath sounds → large-bore chest drain + urgent operative control
  - **F**lail chest: ≥2 consecutive ribs fractured in ≥2 places → paradoxical movement → non-invasive or invasive ventilation if respiratory failure
  - **C**ardiac tamponade: distended neck veins + muffled heart sounds + hypotension (Beck's triad) → emergency pericardiocentesis or thoracotomy

**C — Circulation:**
- IV access (two large-bore IVs, 16G minimum; intraosseous if IV inaccessible)
- Control haemorrhage (direct pressure, tourniquets, pelvic binder for pelvic fractures)
- FAST exam (Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma):
  - Morrison's pouch (right hepatorenal space)
  - Splenorenal space
  - Pelvis (pouch of Douglas / rectovesical pouch)
  - Pericardial window (subxiphoid)
  - Extended FAST (eFAST) adds bilateral pleural views for pneumothorax
  - Free fluid = haemoperitoneum / haemopericardium → urgent operative control

**D — Disability (Neurological):**
- GCS, pupils (asymmetry = herniation until proven otherwise), limb responses
- Glucose (hypoglycaemia mimics TBI — check always)

**E — Exposure and Environmental control:**
- Complete undress + log roll for posterior examination
- Warm blankets, warm fluids (prevent hypothermia)
- Temperature, rectal examination, inspect back

### Pelvic Fractures

High-energy pelvic fractures (open book, vertical shear) can cause massive retroperitoneal haemorrhage — up to 4–5 L in the closed retroperitoneal space. Clinical signs: perineal bruising, scrotal/labial haematoma, leg length discrepancy, pain on pelvic compression (do NOT compress repeatedly — worsens haemorrhage). **Pelvic binder** (circumferential compression device) applied immediately — closes the pelvic ring, reduces volume of the retroperitoneal space, tamponades venous bleeding. Pelvic X-ray in primary survey. Definitive haemorrhage control: interventional radiology (angio-embolisation of pelvic arterial bleeding) or pre-peritoneal packing.

## Diagnosis

**Primary survey investigations (run simultaneously with resuscitation):**
- Portable CXR and pelvic X-ray (during primary survey)
- eFAST ultrasound
- ECG
- Bloods: FBC, coagulation (PT, APTT, fibrinogen), U&E, LFTs, glucose, blood gas (lactate — marker of shock severity and resuscitation adequacy), cross-match (type and screen/full crossmatch)
- Urinalysis (haematuria = urological injury)

**Whole-body CT (CT trauma — CT head, neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis with IV contrast):** The definitive investigation for stable or stabilised trauma patients. Identifies all significant injuries and defines the operative plan. **Do not send a haemodynamically unstable patient to the CT scanner** — if FAST is positive and the patient is haemodynamically unstable, go to the operating theatre directly.

## Management

### Damage Control Resuscitation (DCR)

The shift from crystalloid-based resuscitation to haemostatic resuscitation is the most important change in trauma management of the past 20 years.

**Principles of DCR:**
1. **Tranexamic acid (TXA) 1 g IV over 10 minutes within 3 hours of injury** (CRASH-2 trial: 15% relative risk reduction in haemorrhagic death; no benefit and possible harm if given after 3 hours). Mechanism: antifibrinolytic (inhibits plasminogen activation) — prevents clot lysis and preserves the clot that has formed. TXA is the most evidence-based pharmacological intervention in trauma haemorrhage.
2. **1:1:1 balanced blood product resuscitation** (packed red blood cells : fresh frozen plasma : platelets): Replaces blood with blood — maintains clotting factors (FFP), platelets, and oxygen-carrying capacity (pRBC) simultaneously. Avoids the dilutional coagulopathy of crystalloid. Target: pRBC:FFP ratio of 1:1 (not 4:1 as was previous practice).
3. **Permissive hypotension** in penetrating trauma and haemorrhagic shock (no TBI): target SBP **80–90 mmHg** until haemorrhage control achieved. Lower pressure reduces clot disruption, reduces further haemorrhage, buys time for damage control surgery. **Contraindicated if TBI** (requires MAP ≥80 to maintain CPP).
4. **Prevent the lethal triad:** Warm all fluids, warm the environment, correct acidosis with adequate resuscitation (not bicarbonate), avoid crystalloid dilution.

**Massive Haemorrhage Protocol (MHP) activation criteria:**
- Actual or anticipated transfusion of ≥4 units pRBC in 1 hour
- Or ≥10 units pRBC in 24 hours

MHP delivers pre-prepared packs of blood products in the 1:1:1 ratio to the bedside, without waiting for cross-match (type O negative blood and group AB FFP are used until full cross-match available).

**Fibrinogen replacement:** Fibrinogen is the first clotting factor to become critically depleted in massive haemorrhage. Cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate (RiaSTAP) when fibrinogen <150 mg/dL. FIBTEM test on viscoelastic testing (TEG/ROTEM) guides targeted fibrinogen replacement.

### Damage Control Surgery

The concept: do the minimum necessary to stop the bleeding and control contamination, then close the abdomen temporarily (open abdomen technique), resuscitate in the ICU, and return for definitive repair 24–48 hours later when the patient is no longer in the lethal triad.

**Damage control laparotomy sequence:**
1. Packing (perihepatic, perisplenic, pelvic) to tamponade haemorrhage
2. Vascular control (clamp aorta, ligate bleeding vessels)
3. Bowel resection without anastomosis (staple ends closed — definitive anastomosis at re-look)
4. Temporary abdominal closure (Bogota bag or vacuum-assisted wound closure)
5. ICU: rewarm, correct coagulopathy, resuscitate
6. Return to OR at 24–48h: definitive haemostasis, anastomoses, abdominal closure

### Burns

**Assessment:**
- **Rule of Nines (Wallace):** Head/neck 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%
- **Lund-Browder chart:** More accurate — accounts for age-related variations in body surface area (children have proportionally larger heads)
- Burn depth: superficial (erythema, no blisters — sunburn), superficial partial thickness (blisters, moist, painful), deep partial thickness (pale, mottled, reduced sensation), full thickness (leathery, insensate, waxy/charred)
- Only partial and full-thickness burns >1% TBSA are included in fluid resuscitation calculations

**Parkland formula for IV fluid resuscitation in burns (first 24h):**
**4 mL × weight (kg) × % TBSA burned**
- Give **half** in the **first 8 hours** (from time of burn, not time of admission)
- Give the remaining **half over the next 16 hours**
- Fluid: Hartmann's/lactated Ringer's (preferred over 0.9% NaCl — less hyperchloraemic acidosis)
- Titrate to urine output: target **0.5 mL/kg/hour** in adults (1 mL/kg/hour in children)

## Complications

- **Early:** Tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, massive haemothorax, TBI herniation, abdominal compartment syndrome
- **Haemorrhage:** Coagulopathic bleeding, transfusion-related lung injury (TRALI), transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO)
- **Infection:** Pneumonia (aspiration, ventilator-associated), wound infection, abdominal contamination, sepsis
- **ARDS:** Inflammatory response to massive transfusion, lung contusion, sepsis — develops 24–72h post-injury
- **Abdominal compartment syndrome:** Open abdomen → bowel oedema → increasing intra-abdominal pressure → impaired venous return, renal failure, bowel ischaemia; measure bladder pressure (>20 mmHg + organ dysfunction = ACS)
- **Multi-organ failure:** The final common pathway of severe trauma — kidneys (ATN), liver (ischaemic hepatitis), coagulation (DIC), lungs (ARDS)
- **Fat embolism syndrome:** Long bone fractures → fat globules enter circulation → emboli to lungs (respiratory failure), brain (petechiae, confusion), skin (petechial rash); typically 24–72h after injury

## Clinical Insight

Permissive hypotension is one of the most evidence-based interventions in trauma, and also one of the most nuanced. The logic is physiological: a clot that has formed in a lacerated vessel will be dislodged by high arterial pressure; keeping the pressure at 80–90 mmHg until surgical control is achieved preserves the clot. The CRASH trial confirmed benefit. But this is only true in the absence of TBI — the brain needs CPP >60 mmHg, so any head-injured patient needs normal blood pressure. In real trauma, patients have polytrauma including TBI — and the answer is to achieve surgical haemorrhage control as fast as possible, so that you can then restore normal MAP.

Tranexamic acid must be given within 3 hours of injury. CRASH-2 showed no benefit and possible harm beyond 3 hours (possible pro-thrombotic effect in the later phase of fibrinolysis that may actually be protective). The message for the ED is: give TXA on arrival if major haemorrhage is suspected, before the 3-hour clock runs out. Pre-hospital TXA is now standard in military contexts and increasingly in civilian pre-hospital care.

The open book pelvic fracture is a hidden haemorrhage that kills. The retroperitoneal space is capacious — 3–4 L of blood can accumulate without visible external haemorrhage. A patient with unexplained haemodynamic instability after blunt trauma + a pelvic fracture on AP X-ray has a pelvic haemorrhage until proven otherwise. Apply the pelvic binder (or wrap a sheet around the greater trochanters) immediately — this reduces the pelvic volume and tamponades venous bleeding. Never remove the binder until the patient is in the operating theatre.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 34-year-old motorcyclist, GCS 13, HR 138, BP 78/52, RR 28, SpO₂ 94%. Active haemorrhage from open right femur fracture. Absent left breath sounds + asymmetric chest expansion.

**Step 1 — C (catastrophic haemorrhage control first):**
- Open femur fracture with active haemorrhage: apply a tourniquet to the right thigh immediately (femur fractures can cause 1–2 L blood loss into the thigh)
- Direct pressure to the wound

**Step 2 — Airway:** GCS 13 — she can currently protect her airway. Jaw thrust, oxygen at 15L/min NRB mask. Prepare for RSI if GCS falls.

**Step 3 — Breathing — this is a tension pneumothorax:**
- Left absent breath sounds + haemodynamic compromise (HR 138, BP 78/52) = tension pneumothorax until proven otherwise
- **Immediate needle decompression:** 2nd ICS, midclavicular line, left side (14G cannula) — listen for the hiss of air
- Follow with left chest drain insertion (5th ICS, anterior axillary line)
- Reassess — if BP improves, this was the primary problem

**Step 4 — Circulation:**
- Two large-bore IV (antecubital fossae)
- **Activate the massive haemorrhage protocol** — HR 138, BP 78/52, active haemorrhage, femur fracture = Class III haemorrhagic shock
- **Tranexamic acid 1 g IV over 10 minutes** — do not delay
- Request O-negative pRBC and group AB FFP while crossmatch pending
- **Do NOT give large crystalloid boluses** — DCR with blood products only; max 250 mL crystalloid as a bridge
- FAST exam: free fluid in Morrison's pouch? Pericardial window?

**Step 5 — Disability:**
- GCS 13 — brief LOC at scene = concussion at minimum, TBI possible
- Pupils: equal and reactive? Any asymmetry = herniation alarm
- Blood glucose
- **If TBI confirmed: do NOT allow BP to fall below 90 mmHg systolic** — abandon permissive hypotension

**Step 6 — Decision: CT or Theatre?**
- If FAST negative, patient responding to blood products → CT trauma for full injury survey
- If FAST positive (haemoperitoneum) + persistent haemodynamic instability → operating theatre directly for damage control laparotomy

**Exam trigger:** *Haemodynamically unstable trauma patient + absent left breath sounds* → tension pneumothorax first (needle decompression before anything else). Then: major haemorrhage protocol + TXA within 3 hours. The distractor is starting large-volume crystalloid for the hypotension — crystalloid worsens the lethal triad and delays definitive haemostasis with blood products.
