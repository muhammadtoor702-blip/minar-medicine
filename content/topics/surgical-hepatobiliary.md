## Overview

Hepatobiliary surgical disease encompasses a spectrum of conditions ranging from uncomplicated biliary colic — a self-limiting pain syndrome from gallstone impaction — to life-threatening acute cholangitis, which demands emergency decompression within hours. The gallstone triad of biliary colic, acute cholecystitis, and choledocholithiasis accounts for the majority of acute surgical admissions worldwide. Pancreatitis, most commonly gallstone-triggered, ranges from mild self-limiting inflammation to necrotising pancreatitis with multi-organ failure and 30% mortality. Understanding the anatomy of the biliary tree and the physiological consequences of obstruction at each level is the key to understanding the entire spectrum.

## Pathophysiology

### Gallstone Formation and Consequences

Gallstones form when bile becomes supersaturated with cholesterol (80% of stones in the developed world) or unconjugated bilirubin (pigment stones — haemolytic disease, cirrhosis). The classic risk factors are the **5 Fs**: Fat, Forty, Female, Fertile, Family history.

**Biliary colic:** A gallstone impacts the cystic duct or Hartmann's pouch. Smooth muscle of the gallbladder contracts against the obstruction, causing severe colicky pain in the epigastrium or RUQ, typically post-prandially (cholecystokinin triggers gallbladder contraction). The stone disimpacts or passes, and the pain resolves completely within 1–6 hours. No fever, no raised WBC, no peritonism. Recurrence is the rule.

**Acute cholecystitis:** Persistent cystic duct obstruction → secondary bacterial infection of stagnant bile → transmural inflammation of the gallbladder wall → fever, leucocytosis, peritonism over the gallbladder fundus. If untreated: gangrenous cholecystitis → perforation → biliary peritonitis or pericholecystic abscess. Empyema (pus-filled gallbladder) is a complication requiring urgent cholecystectomy or percutaneous cholecystostomy in unfit patients.

**Choledocholithiasis:** Stone passes from the gallbladder into the common bile duct (CBD) via the cystic duct → obstructs biliary drainage → conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia (jaundice + dark urine + pale stools), raised ALP and GGT. If the stone obstructs the sphincter of Oddi at the ampulla of Vater, biliary stasis above creates a medium for bacterial infection → acute cholangitis.

**Acute cholangitis:** Bacterial infection of an obstructed biliary system. The ductal pressure rises, bacteria enter the hepatic veins → bacteraemia → sepsis. Organisms: Escherichia coli (most common), Klebsiella, Enterococcus. **Charcot's triad** (present in 50–70%): RUQ pain + fever/rigors + jaundice. **Reynolds' pentad** (adds hypotension + confusion) = septic shock from biliary sepsis — this is a surgical emergency with mortality >50% if not decompressed within hours.

### Pancreatitis — Pathophysiology

The **GET SMASHED** mnemonic for causes: **G**allstones (40%, most common in UK/US), **E**thanol (35%), **T**rauma, **S**teroids, **M**umps/viruses, **A**utoimmune, **S**corpion stings, **H**ypertriglyceridaemia (>1000 mg/dL), **E**RCP (post-procedure), **D**rugs (azathioprine, thiazides, furosemide, valproate).

**Mechanism:** Premature activation of trypsinogen to trypsin within pancreatic acinar cells → autodigestion of the pancreas → local inflammation → release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1, IL-6) → systemic inflammatory response → SIRS → multi-organ dysfunction.

**Mild acute pancreatitis** (80%): oedematous interstitial pancreatitis — resolves within 7–10 days with supportive care.

**Severe acute pancreatitis** (20%): necrotising pancreatitis — areas of pancreatic parenchyma become necrotic (pancreatic necrosis on CT with contrast — non-enhancing areas). Risk of:
- **Infected necrosis** (occurs after week 1, peaks at weeks 3–4): fever + deteriorating clinical course + CT showing gas bubbles in necrotic collection = infection. Confirmed by CT-guided fine-needle aspiration + culture. Management: **step-up approach** — antibiotics (imipenem) → percutaneous catheter drainage → minimally invasive necrosectomy (video-assisted retroperitoneal debridement [VARD] or endoscopic transluminal necrosectomy) → open necrosectomy only if all less invasive approaches fail.
- **Pseudocyst:** Fluid collection arising from ductal disruption; most resolve spontaneously; large/symptomatic → endoscopic cystgastrostomy.
- **Vascular complications:** Splenic artery pseudoaneurysm (sentinel bleed from drain — angio-embolisation).

## Clinical Presentation

### Biliary Colic vs. Acute Cholecystitis

| Feature | Biliary Colic | Acute Cholecystitis |
|---------|--------------|---------------------|
| Pain duration | 1–6 hours | >6 hours, persistent |
| Fever | Absent | Present (>38°C) |
| WBC | Normal | Elevated (leucocytosis) |
| Murphy's sign | Negative | **Positive** |
| Peritonism | Absent | Guarding/rebound over RUQ |
| USS | Gallstones ± CBD | Gallstones + thick wall + pericholecystic fluid |

**Murphy's sign:** The examiner places two fingers over the RUQ (gallbladder fundus at the junction of the right MCL and costal margin) and asks the patient to breathe in deeply. Inspiration descends the diaphragm and gallbladder; if the gallbladder is inflamed, contact with the examiner's fingers causes sudden cessation of inspiration due to pain — a positive sign. Sensitivity ~97% for cholecystitis if the test is performed by ultrasound probe (sonographic Murphy's sign); clinical Murphy's has lower sensitivity.

### Tokyo Guidelines for Cholecystitis Severity

The Tokyo Guidelines (TG18) grade cholecystitis severity:
- **Grade I (mild):** Mild local inflammation, no organ dysfunction
- **Grade II (moderate):** Leucocytosis >18 × 10⁹/L, palpable RUQ mass, duration >72h, marked local inflammation
- **Grade III (severe):** Organ dysfunction (cardiovascular, neurological, respiratory, renal, hepatic, coagulopathy)

### Acute Cholangitis

The clinical hallmark is **Charcot's triad** — RUQ pain, fever with rigors, and jaundice. Only 50–70% of patients have all three. Any two of the three with biliary obstruction on imaging should prompt treatment as cholangitis. Reynolds' pentad (adding septic shock and confusion) indicates fulminant cholangitis with very high mortality; urgent biliary decompression becomes the life-saving priority.

**Biochemistry:** Raised bilirubin (conjugated, >2 mg/dL), raised ALP (>2× upper limit), raised GGT, raised WBC with neutrophilia, positive blood cultures (>50%).

### Acute Pancreatitis

**Classic presentation:** Sudden-onset severe epigastric pain radiating to the back ("boring" quality), vomiting, anorexia. The patient lies still (unlike in renal colic, where the patient is restless). Tachycardia, fever, ileus. Epigastric tenderness without true peritonism (unless perforation of adjacent viscus or bowel infarction).

**Rare but memorable signs (both indicate haemorrhagic pancreatitis):**
- **Cullen's sign**: periumbilical bruising (haemoperitoneum from retroperitoneal blood tracking anteriorly)
- **Grey Turner's sign**: flank bruising (retroperitoneal haematoma)

**Severity scoring — Glasgow (Imrie) Criteria** (mnemonic: **PANCREAS**):
- **P**aO₂ <60 mmHg
- **A**lbumin <3.2 g/dL
- **N**eutrophils (WBC) >15 × 10⁹/L
- **C**alcium <8 mg/dL
- **R**enal (urea/BUN) >45 mg/dL
- **E**nzymes (LDH >600 U/L, AST >200 U/L)
- **A**ge >55
- **S**ugar (glucose) >180 mg/dL

Score ≥3 within 48 hours of admission = severe pancreatitis. **Ranson's criteria** similarly predicts severity at admission and at 48h (11 criteria total). C-reactive protein (CRP) >150 mg/L at 48h is also a validated marker of severity.

## Diagnosis

**Ultrasound:** First-line for all suspected biliary disease. Identifies gallstones (>95% sensitivity), gallbladder wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid, CBD diameter (normal <6–7 mm; dilated ≥8–10 mm suggests choledocholithiasis even without visible stone, as stones in the CBD are seen in only 50% of cases on USS). Biliary obstruction causes intrahepatic duct dilatation.

**CT abdomen with IV contrast:** For pancreatitis — defines extent of necrosis (CT Severity Index/Balthazar grade). Performed at 48–72 hours after onset, not acutely (necrosis may not be apparent within first 24h). For cholecystitis: confirms diagnosis in equivocal cases, detects complications (abscess, perforation).

**MRCP (Magnetic Resonance Cholangiopancreatography):** Non-invasive imaging of the biliary tree; sensitivity ~95% for CBD stones. Preferred pre-operative investigation to plan ERCP or surgical CBD exploration. Does not require contrast or ionising radiation. Cannot be therapeutic.

**ERCP (Endoscopic Retrograde Cholangiopancreatography):** Both diagnostic and therapeutic. Indications: suspected choledocholithiasis, acute cholangitis requiring decompression. Sphincterotomy + stone extraction. Risk of post-ERCP pancreatitis (3–5%) — reduced by rectal indomethacin.

**Serum amylase/lipase:** Elevated >3× upper limit of normal in pancreatitis. Lipase is more sensitive and specific than amylase (amylase can be normal in alcoholic pancreatitis). A very high amylase (>1000 U/L) does not necessarily indicate severe disease, and a mildly elevated amylase does not exclude acute pancreatitis in its later phases.

## Management

### Acute Cholecystitis

**Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within 72 hours of symptom onset** is the standard of care (NICE, Tokyo Guidelines, ACCEA). Multiple RCTs (ACALC, CHOCOLATE, PACIFIC) confirm that early surgery reduces overall complications, length of stay, and risk of complications compared with delayed (interval) cholecystectomy at 6 weeks.

- **Antibiotics:** Co-amoxiclav or cefuroxime + metronidazole perioperatively; continue post-operatively for complicated cholecystitis (perforation, abscess, gangrenous).
- **Grade III (severe) cholecystitis:** Emergency cholecystostomy (percutaneous drain of the gallbladder) if patient is too unwell for surgery, followed by interval cholecystectomy.
- **Mirizzi syndrome:** Gallstone impacted in the Hartmann's pouch compresses the common hepatic duct → obstructive jaundice; requires careful open surgical dissection (risk of CBD injury from inflammatory adherence).

### Acute Cholangitis

**Resuscitate and decompile urgently:**
1. IV fluids, antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem for septic shock), blood cultures
2. **ERCP within 24 hours** (Tokyo Guidelines): biliary decompression via sphincterotomy + stone extraction is the definitive treatment. In centres without 24h ERCP access: percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC) for drainage.
3. Reynolds' pentad (septic shock + confusion) = ICU admission + emergency ERCP within hours, not 24h.
4. Post-decompression: elective cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence (delayed 4–6 weeks).

### Acute Pancreatitis

**Mild pancreatitis (Glasgow <3):**
- Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (Hartmann's/lactated Ringer's preferred over 0.9% NaCl — less acidosis): 250–500 mL/hour in the first 24h
- Analgesia (PCA morphine or epidural)
- Oral feeding as soon as tolerated (early enteral nutrition reduces complications)
- No antibiotics (routine prophylactic antibiotics do NOT reduce infectious complications — multiple RCTs, IMRIE criteria confirm)
- Monitor for complications (renal function, glucose, calcium)

**Severe pancreatitis (Glasgow ≥3 or organ dysfunction):**
- ICU transfer
- **Enteral nutrition via NG/NJ tube within 24–48h** — superior to parenteral nutrition (TPN): reduces gut bacterial translocation, reduces mortality. TPN only if enteral route impossible.
- **Do NOT perform early ERCP** unless there is concurrent cholangitis — urgent ERCP for pancreatitis alone does NOT improve outcomes (PANTER trial).
- Gallstone-triggered pancreatitis: ERCP with sphincterotomy within 72h IF cholangitis or persisting biliary obstruction. Cholecystectomy BEFORE discharge (index admission) for mild-moderate gallstone pancreatitis to prevent recurrence.

**Step-up approach for infected pancreatic necrosis:**
1. Antibiotics (imipenem-cilastatin or meropenem)
2. Radiologically guided percutaneous catheter drainage
3. Minimally invasive necrosectomy (endoscopic transluminal, VARD)
4. Open necrosectomy (last resort — high morbidity)
**Avoid surgery before week 3–4** (necrosis not yet liquefied; early surgery has very high mortality).

## Complications

- **Cholecystitis:** Gangrenous cholecystitis (perforation → biliary peritonitis), pericholecystic abscess, cholecystoenteric fistula, gallstone ileus (stone erodes into duodenum → passes to terminal ileum → small bowel obstruction; CXR: **pneumobilia** + small bowel obstruction = Rigler's triad)
- **Cholangitis:** Septic shock, hepatic abscess, portal pyaemia
- **Pancreatitis:** Necrotising pancreatitis, infected necrosis, pseudocyst, pancreatic fistula, splenic/portal vein thrombosis, haemorrhage (pseudoaneurysm), multi-organ failure, ARDS
- **Post-cholecystectomy syndrome:** Persistent biliary-type pain after cholecystectomy — causes include retained CBD stone (early), bile leak, sphincter of Oddi dysfunction
- **Bile duct injury:** The most feared complication of laparoscopic cholecystectomy (incidence ~0.3%); intraoperative cholangiography reduces risk; immediate repair if identified; delayed stricture if unrecognised

## Clinical Insight

The three-level classification of biliary disease is the key mental model: (1) gallstone in the gallbladder cystic duct = colic/cholecystitis; (2) stone in the CBD = obstructive jaundice; (3) stone plus infection in the CBD = cholangitis. Cholangitis is a different disease from cholecystitis — it is a systemic sepsis from an infected obstructed ductal system, not an inflamed gallbladder. The treatment for cholangitis is biliary decompression (ERCP), not cholecystectomy. Removing the gallbladder in active cholangitis is unnecessary and hazardous.

The "normal amylase" trap in pancreatitis: amylase has a short serum half-life of ~10 hours. A patient presenting 3–4 days after the onset of pain may have a normal or near-normal amylase. Lipase persists longer in serum and is the better test. Clinical history, elevated CRP, and CT features confirm the diagnosis when biochemistry is unhelpful.

Early surgery in acute cholecystitis was resisted for decades based on the concern that inflamed tissues would make dissection dangerous. The RCT evidence is now unambiguous: early surgery (within 72h) is safer than delayed surgery because the anatomy is clearer before dense fibrotic adhesions form. Delayed surgery after failed conservative management (failed after 6 weeks) is technically harder with higher bile duct injury rates.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 52-year-old obese woman, 3 previous biliary colic episodes, now 6h of severe RUQ pain + fever + jaundice. Murphy's sign positive, guarding, rebound. WBC 18.3 × 10⁹/L, bilirubin 4.2 mg/dL, ALP 340 U/L. USS: gallstones + gallbladder wall thickening + pericholecystic fluid + CBD 9 mm.

**Step 1 — Diagnose the disease level:** This is NOT simple cholecystitis. The jaundice (bilirubin 4.2 mg/dL, ALP 340) + dilated CBD (9 mm) = CBD stone causing biliary obstruction. The fever + rigors + RUQ pain = Charcot's triad = **acute cholangitis** superimposed on acute cholecystitis. This is a combined gallbladder and ductal emergency.

**Step 2 — Immediate management:**
- IV access + fluid resuscitation (0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's)
- Blood cultures (before antibiotics)
- IV antibiotics: piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5g TID (gram-negative + enterococcal cover)
- Analgesia (diclofenac PR or morphine PCA)
- Monitor urine output, lactate, renal function

**Step 3 — ERCP within 24 hours:**
- Biliary decompression is the life-saving priority
- Sphincterotomy + CBD stone extraction
- Placement of plastic stent if stone cannot be cleared (staged clearance)
- This converts the obstructed infected bile duct into a drained system — temperature will improve within 24–48h

**Step 4 — Cholecystectomy:**
- NOT during the acute episode
- Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy 4–6 weeks after ERCP (allows inflammation to settle, stent removed at same time or beforehand)
- Tokyo Grade II cholecystitis (WBC >18 × 10⁹/L) — early cholecystectomy within 72h is appropriate if patient stabilises after ERCP

**Step 5 — Post-procedure monitoring:**
- Watch for post-ERCP pancreatitis (amylase at 2h post-procedure)
- Rectal indomethacin 100 mg routinely reduces post-ERCP pancreatitis risk
- Daily LFTs to confirm biliary decompression (bilirubin falling)

**Exam trigger:** *RUQ pain + fever + jaundice (Charcot's triad) + dilated CBD on USS* → acute cholangitis. Immediate IV antibiotics + ERCP within 24 hours = the two interventions that reduce mortality. The distractor is rushing to cholecystectomy — this treats the gallbladder but not the infected bile duct. ERCP first, cholecystectomy later.
