## Overview

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) encompasses two chronic, relapsing conditions — **ulcerative colitis (UC)** and **Crohn's disease (CD)** — characterised by immune-mediated gastrointestinal inflammation. They share epidemiological features, some extraintestinal manifestations, and therapeutic approaches, but differ fundamentally in the distribution, depth, and pattern of inflammation. These differences determine their complications, surgical implications, and long-term prognosis. Distinguishing them matters: UC can be cured by colectomy; Crohn's recurs at the anastomosis.

---

## Pathophysiology

IBD results from a dysregulated immune response to commensal gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals. Over 200 susceptibility loci have been identified. **NOD2/CARD15** mutations on chromosome 16 are the strongest single genetic risk factor for Crohn's disease — NOD2 is an intracellular pattern recognition receptor for bacterial muramyl dipeptide; loss of function impairs mucosal barrier integrity and bacterial sensing, allowing uncontrolled immune activation.

**In UC**: Mucosal and submucosal inflammation driven predominantly by Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13). Inflammation is continuous, confined to the colon, and invariably involves the rectum. The hallmarks are cryptitis, crypt abscesses, and goblet cell depletion with loss of the protective mucus layer. Inflammation is limited to the mucosa — never transmural in pure UC (transmural involvement means Crohn's or toxic megacolon). UC is **3× more common in non-smokers** — smoking has a paradoxical protective effect in UC through nicotinic receptor modulation of mucosal immunity. The opposite is true for Crohn's: smoking worsens disease activity 3–4 fold.

**In Crohn's disease**: Transmural (full-thickness) inflammation driven by Th1/Th17 pathways and TNF-α. Granulomatous inflammation involving all layers of the bowel wall explains the complications unique to CD: fistulae (inflammation burrows through the bowel wall into adjacent structures — bladder, vagina, skin), strictures (fibrotic healing of transmural inflammation), abscesses, and perianal disease. Non-caseating granulomas on histology are pathognomonic when present — they are absent in ~50% of biopsies.

| Feature | Ulcerative Colitis | Crohn's Disease |
|---------|------------------|----------------|
| Distribution | Rectum → colon, continuous | Entire GI tract from mouth to anus, skip lesions |
| Depth | Mucosal only | Transmural |
| Rectal involvement | Always | Variable (~50%) |
| Small bowel | Never (except backwash ileitis) | Terminal ileum in ~70% |
| Gross appearance | Pseudopolyps, contact bleeding | Cobblestone mucosa, deep fissuring ulcers |
| Histology | Crypt abscesses, goblet cell depletion | Non-caseating granulomas |
| Smoking | Protective | Worsens disease (3–4×) |
| Surgery | Curative (colectomy removes all affected bowel) | Not curative — recurs at anastomosis |
| Complications | Toxic megacolon, CRC | Fistulae, strictures, abscesses, perianal disease |

**Prevalence**: Each ~200–1000/100,000 in the UK; incidence 10–20/100,000/year; typical onset 15–40 years. UC and CD share many susceptibility loci — small differences in genetics combined with environmental modifiers (smoking, diet, microbiome) explain the distinct phenotypes.

## Clinical Presentation

**UC**: Bloody diarrhoea is the cardinal symptom — always present, always mixed with stool (not coating it as in haemorrhoids). Mucus, urgency, tenesmus (sensation of incomplete evacuation), and lower abdominal cramping accompany it. Bowel frequency directly reflects disease severity.

**Assessing severity in UC (Truelove and Witts criteria):**

| Variable | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---------|------|---------|--------|
| Motions per day | ≤4 | 4–5 | ≥6 |
| Rectal bleeding | Small | Moderate | Large/continuous |
| Temperature | Apyrexial | 37.1–37.8°C | >37.8°C |
| Resting pulse | <70 bpm | 70–90 bpm | >90 bpm |
| Haemoglobin | >11.0 g/dL | 10.5–11.0 g/dL | <10.5 g/dL |
| ESR / CRP | <30 mm/hr | ESR >30 or CRP >45 mg/L | ESR >30 or CRP >45 mg/L |

Severe UC requires hospital admission — it is a medical emergency with risk of perforation and death.

**Crohn's disease**: More heterogeneous — diarrhoea (often without blood in small bowel disease), right iliac fossa pain (ileocaecal disease most common — ~70%), weight loss, anorexia, and fever. Perianal disease (fissures, fistulae, skin tags, abscesses) occurs in ~50% and may precede or dominate GI symptoms. Mouth: aphthous ulcers. The poor prognostic factors for Crohn's: age <40 at diagnosis, steroid requirement at first presentation, perianal disease, isolated terminal ileitis, smoking.

**Extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs):**

| EIM | Correlates with Bowel Activity? | Notes |
|-----|--------------------------------|-------|
| Erythema nodosum | Yes | Tender red nodules on shins; resolves with bowel treatment |
| Pyoderma gangrenosum | No | Ulcerating lesion with violaceous border; treat with immunosuppression regardless of bowel activity |
| Episcleritis | Yes | Eye redness and pain; resolves with bowel treatment |
| Uveitis | No | Painful eye with photophobia; requires ophthalmology; independent course |
| Peripheral arthropathy (large joint, pauciarticular) | Yes | Resolves with bowel treatment |
| Axial arthropathy / ankylosing spondylitis | No | Independent course; persists after colectomy |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) | No | Strongly UC-associated (~5%); elevated ALP; beaded biliary tree on MRCP; cholangiocarcinoma risk; persists and progresses after colectomy |

PSC and axial arthropathy follow an independent course — bowel remission does not control them, and they require separate specialist management.

## Diagnosis

**Exclude infection first**: Before diagnosing an IBD flare or starting steroids, send stool MC&S and *C. difficile* toxin. Administering IV steroids or immunosuppression to a patient with infective colitis — including *C. difficile* colitis — makes the infection worse and can cause toxic megacolon. The stool sample comes before the steroid prescription.

**Faecal calprotectin**: A simple, non-invasive marker of GI mucosal inflammation — calprotectin is released by neutrophils infiltrating the bowel wall. High sensitivity for inflammatory causes of diarrhoea (IBD, infection); a normal faecal calprotectin essentially excludes active IBD and supports IBS. Useful both for initial diagnosis and for monitoring treatment response.

**Bloods**: FBC (anaemia from blood loss, chronic disease, or folate/B12 deficiency; leucocytosis in active disease), CRP (active inflammation), albumin (hypoalbuminaemia reflects severity and nutritional state), LFTs (PSC?), B12 and folate (particularly in Crohn's with terminal ileal involvement).

**Colonoscopy + biopsies**: Gold standard. In acute severe UC, flexible sigmoidoscopy is preferred over full colonoscopy (lower perforation risk; avoids bowel preparation in a sick patient; rectosigmoid appearances are sufficient to confirm the diagnosis and start treatment). Biopsies from multiple sites — including macroscopically normal mucosa — are essential for histological confirmation.

**Small bowel imaging**: MRI enterography (preferred — no radiation, excellent soft-tissue detail for bowel wall thickness and mesenteric changes) or CT enterography for small bowel Crohn's. MRI pelvis is the gold standard for perianal Crohn's — maps fistula tracts and abscesses before surgery.

## Management

### Ulcerative Colitis

**Inducing remission — Mild to moderate UC:**
- **5-aminosalicylate (mesalazine/5-ASA)** — cornerstone. Topical preparations are more effective than oral for distal disease: suppository (Pentasa 1g) for proctitis; enema for proctosigmoiditis/left-sided disease. Combine topical and oral for more extensive disease (oral Pentasa 2g daily).
- **Oral prednisolone 40 mg/day** for flares not responding to 5-ASA after 1 month; taper by 5 mg/week over 7–8 weeks. Steroids are not effective as maintenance therapy and should not be continued long-term.

**Acute severe UC — inpatient management:**
- IV hydrocortisone 100 mg QDS (or methylprednisolone 40 mg BD IV)
- Rectal steroids: hydrocortisone 100 mg in 100 mL 0.9% saline PR BD
- IV hydration and electrolyte replacement; monitor serum potassium daily
- Thromboembolism prophylaxis (LMWH) — venous thromboembolism risk is significantly elevated in active IBD; prophylaxis must be given even with active rectal bleeding
- Daily stool chart, AXR (exclude toxic megacolon — transverse colon >6 cm with systemic toxicity)
- Daily FBC, ESR, CRP

**The day 3 decision rule**: If by day 3 the patient has >8 stools/day, OR stools 3–8/day with CRP >45 mg/L, the probability of needing a colectomy during that admission is ~85%. Rescue immunosuppression should be considered at this point — do not wait.

**Rescue therapy (day 3–5)**: IV ciclosporin OR IV infliximab can avoid colectomy in ~60–80% of patients who fail IV steroids. Engage the surgeons early in shared decision-making — do not delay colectomy so long that steroid exposure and malnutrition make recovery more difficult.

**Toxic megacolon**: Transverse colon >6 cm on AXR with systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, leucocytosis). Urgent surgical review. Mortality of perforation is high — colectomy is often the life-saving intervention.

**Maintaining remission:**
- Oral (and/or topical) 5-ASA long-term — reduces relapse from ~80% to ~20% at 1 year; also reduces CRC risk. Once-daily dosing is as effective as split dosing and improves adherence. Monitor renal function (nephrotoxicity risk): U&E at baseline, 2–3 months, then annually.
- **Azathioprine 2–2.5 mg/kg/day** for steroid-dependent or frequently relapsing disease. Takes 6–10 weeks to work. **Check TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) activity before starting** — approximately 1% of the population have no TPMT activity and are at risk of potentially life-threatening bone marrow suppression; intermediate activity requires dose reduction. 30% of patients will develop side effects requiring cessation including abdominal pain, nausea, pancreatitis, leucopenia, or abnormal LFTs. Monitor FBC + LFTs + U&E weekly for 4 weeks, then 4-weekly for 3 months, then 3-monthly thereafter.
- **Biologics**: Anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab), anti-integrin (vedolizumab — gut-selective, reduced systemic immunosuppression), anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), and JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, filgotinib, upadacitinib — oral small molecule drugs) for moderate-severe refractory disease.
- **Surgery** in ~20% overall: subtotal colectomy + ileostomy (emergency), followed by completion proctectomy or ileo-anal pouch construction (elective). An ileo-anal pouch allows stoma reversal — ~6 stools/day, risk of recurrent pouchitis (treat with metronidazole + ciprofloxacin).

**CRC surveillance**: Colonoscopy every 1–5 years (interval based on risk factors: PSC, disease extent, previous dysplasia, and inflammatory activity) after 8–10 years of colitis. Dysplasia may occur in flat, normal-looking mucosa — surveillance with dye-spraying and targeted biopsies is more effective than random biopsies.

### Crohn's Disease

**Inducing remission — Mild to moderate:**
- Oral prednisolone 40 mg/day for 1 week, then taper by 5 mg/week over 7 weeks
- Budesonide (enteric-coated corticosteroid with extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism — fewer systemic side effects) for ileocaecal disease without systemic features; less effective than prednisolone for moderate-severe disease
- **Exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN)**: polymeric (whole protein) formula administered as a sole food source, achieving remission rates equivalent to steroids in children; increasingly used in adults, particularly pre-operatively to improve nutritional state and reduce fistula activity. More palatable than elemental (amino acid-based) formulas.

**Severe Crohn's**: Admit for IV steroids and similar care to acute severe UC. Consider abdominal sepsis (abscess or fistula) as a complication before starting or escalating immunosuppression — CT/MRI to exclude abscess; abscesses must be drained before biologics.

**Maintaining remission:**
- **Azathioprine 2–2.5 mg/kg/day** (check TPMT first, same monitoring as in UC) — effective in steroid-refractory or steroid-dependent disease. Not effective at inducing remission; requires 6–10 weeks onset.
- **5-ASA has no role in Crohn's** — unlike UC, 5-ASA is not beneficial in CD
- Anti-TNF biologics (infliximab, adalimumab) — indicated for moderate-severe disease, fistulising disease, or steroid-refractory disease. Combination with azathioprine (SONIC trial: combination superior to either alone) in early disease. Long-term concern: increased risk of lymphoma.
- Vedolizumab (anti-α4β7 integrin — gut-selective) or ustekinumab (anti-IL12/23) for patients failing anti-TNF
- Methotrexate ≥15 mg/week SC — alternative immunomodulator; contraindicated in women of reproductive age (teratogenic)

**Perianal disease**: Antibiotics (metronidazole + ciprofloxacin) for symptom control. Infliximab for fistula closure (ACCENT II trial: 36% fistula closure vs 19% placebo). Surgical drainage of any associated abscess before biologic therapy (biologics given in the presence of undrained abscess risk septic extension). Seton insertion to maintain fistula drainage while medical therapy is optimised.

**Surgery in Crohn's** (50–80% require at least one operation): Not curative — disease recurs at the anastomosis. Indications: drug failure (most common), GI obstruction, perforation, fistulae, abscess. Aims: resect affected segment with minimal bowel sacrifice; beware short bowel syndrome after repeated resections. Laparoscopic resection preferred. Postoperative prophylaxis (azathioprine ± anti-TNF) recommended to reduce recurrence.

### Pre-Biologic and Immunomodulator Screening

Before starting azathioprine, biologics, or any immunosuppressive therapy:
- **TPMT testing** before azathioprine
- **IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay)** to exclude latent TB — biologics, particularly anti-TNF agents, can reactivate TB
- **Hepatitis B serology** (HBsAg, anti-HBc, anti-HBs) — HBV reactivation under immunosuppression can cause fulminant hepatic failure; treat or vaccinate accordingly
- **HIV serology** in high-risk patients
- **Varicella IgG** — if no clear history of chickenpox/shingles, test; VZV vaccination should be given before immunosuppression if not immune
- **Vaccination review**: Pneumococcal and annual influenza vaccines recommended. Live vaccines (MMR, yellow fever, varicella, BCG) contraindicated during biologic therapy.

## Complications

**UC**: Toxic megacolon (perforation risk), colorectal carcinoma (risk proportional to extent and duration of active inflammation; 5–10% risk after 20 years of uncontrolled pancolitis), massive haemorrhage, perforation, PSC.

**Crohn's**: Intestinal obstruction (stricturing disease), enteric fistulae (to skin, bladder [colovesical — pneumaturia, faecaluria], vagina [colovaginal]), intra-abdominal abscess, perianal sepsis, short bowel syndrome (after repeated resections), malnutrition and micronutrient deficiency (B12 and fat-soluble vitamins from terminal ileal disease; iron, folate, zinc).

**Both**: Osteoporosis (steroid exposure); opportunistic infections from immunosuppression (CMV colitis, PCP, fungal); skin cancer risk with long-term thiopurines; lymphoma with thiopurines/anti-TNF; gallstones in Crohn's (impaired ileal bile acid reabsorption → depleted bile acid pool → lithogenic bile).

## Clinical Insight

Always exclude *C. difficile* before escalating immunosuppression. Giving IV steroids or biologics to a patient with infective colitis worsens the infection. The stool sample comes before the steroid prescription — this principle should be inviolable. An undrained abscess in Crohn's is equally dangerous: anti-TNF therapy in the presence of an undrained collection risks extension to life-threatening sepsis.

PSC follows an independent course from UC — the bowel can be in complete remission while PSC progresses silently. Any UC patient with an unexplained elevated ALP requires MRCP. The risk of cholangiocarcinoma in PSC makes this a surveillance and management priority that cannot be deferred.

Toxic megacolon is a radiological diagnosis. Every patient admitted with acute severe colitis requires a daily AXR. A patient who looks clinically stable — even improving — may have a colon hours from perforation. The radiological finding of transverse colon >6 cm mandates urgent surgical review regardless of clinical appearance.
