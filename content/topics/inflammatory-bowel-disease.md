## Overview

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) encompasses two chronic, relapsing conditions — **ulcerative colitis (UC)** and **Crohn's disease (CD)** — characterised by immune-mediated gastrointestinal inflammation. They share epidemiological features, some extraintestinal manifestations, and therapeutic approaches, but differ fundamentally in the distribution, depth, and pattern of inflammation. These differences determine their complications, surgical implications, and long-term prognosis.

## Pathophysiology

IBD results from a dysregulated immune response to commensal gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals. Over 200 susceptibility loci have been identified. NOD2/CARD15 mutations are the strongest genetic risk factor for CD — NOD2 is a pattern recognition receptor whose loss disrupts mucosal barrier integrity and bacterial sensing.

**In UC**: Mucosal and submucosal inflammation driven by Th2 cytokines. Inflammation is continuous, starting at the rectum and extending proximally. Cryptitis and crypt abscesses are histological hallmarks. Goblet cell depletion leads to loss of the protective mucus layer.

**In Crohn's disease**: Transmural (full-thickness) inflammation driven by Th1/Th17 pathways and TNF-α. Transmural involvement explains the complications unique to CD: fistulae (inflammation burrowing through the bowel wall into adjacent structures), strictures (fibrotic healing), abscesses, and perianal disease. Non-caseating granulomas on histology are pathognomonic when present.

| Feature | Ulcerative Colitis | Crohn's Disease |
|---------|------------------|----------------|
| Distribution | Rectum → colon, continuous | Entire GI tract, skip lesions |
| Depth | Mucosal only | Transmural |
| Rectal involvement | Always | Variable (~50%) |
| Gross appearance | Pseudopolyps, contact bleeding | Cobblestone mucosa, deep fissuring ulcers |
| Histology | Crypt abscesses, goblet cell depletion | Non-caseating granulomas |
| Smoking | Protective | Worsens disease |
| Surgery | Curative (colectomy) | Not curative — recurs at anastomosis |
| Complications | Toxic megacolon, CRC | Fistulae, strictures, abscesses, perianal disease |

## Clinical Presentation

**UC**: Bloody diarrhoea is the cardinal symptom — always present. Mucus, urgency, and tenesmus accompany it. Severity ranges from mild (fewer than 4 stools/day, no systemic features) to severe (≥6 bloody stools/day with fever, tachycardia, or anaemia).

**Crohn's disease**: More heterogeneous — diarrhoea (often without blood), right iliac fossa pain (ileocaecal disease is most common), weight loss, and perianal disease (fissures, fistulae, abscesses — in ~30%).

**Extraintestinal manifestations (EIMs):**

| EIM | Correlates with Activity? | Notes |
|-----|--------------------------|-------|
| Erythema nodosum | Yes | Painful red nodules on shins |
| Pyoderma gangrenosum | No | Painful ulcerating lesion; treat with immunosuppression |
| Episcleritis | Yes | Eye redness; resolves with bowel treatment |
| Uveitis | No | Requires ophthalmology; independent course |
| Peripheral arthropathy (large joint) | Yes | Resolves with bowel treatment |
| Axial arthropathy / ankylosing spondylitis | No | Independent course; persists after colectomy |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) | No | Strongly UC-associated; elevated ALP; cholangiocarcinoma risk |

PSC and axial arthropathy follow an independent course — they progress even when the bowel is quiescent and persist after colectomy.

## Diagnosis

**Exclude infection first**: Before treating a presumed IBD flare with steroids, always send stool for MC&S and *C. difficile* toxin. Stool faecal calprotectin is a sensitive marker of mucosal inflammation — useful to distinguish IBD from IBS.

**Bloods**: FBC, CRP, albumin, LFTs (PSC?), iron studies, B12 and folate.

**Colonoscopy + biopsies**: Gold standard. In acute severe UC, use flexible sigmoidoscopy (lower perforation risk). Biopsies from multiple sites — including macroscopically normal mucosa — confirm the histological diagnosis.

**Small bowel imaging**: MRI enterography (preferred — no radiation) or CT enterography for small bowel Crohn's. CT is faster in an acute setting. MRI pelvis is gold standard for perianal Crohn's — maps fistula tracts and abscesses.
## Management

### Ulcerative Colitis

**Mild-moderate active UC:**
- **5-aminosalicylate (mesalazine)** — cornerstone of induction and maintenance. Topical (suppository for proctitis, enema for left-sided) is more effective than oral for distal disease. Combine topical and oral for better outcomes.
- **Oral prednisolone** 40 mg/day for acute flares — effective induction, not maintenance. Taper over 8 weeks.

**Acute severe UC (inpatient):** Truelove and Witts criteria: ≥6 bloody stools/day + at least one of: HR >90, temperature >37.8°C, Hb <10.5 g/dL, CRP >30 mg/L.

- IV hydrocortisone 100 mg QDS; daily stool chart and AXR (exclude toxic megacolon)
- If no response by day 3: **IV ciclosporin** or **IV infliximab** as rescue therapy
- If rescue therapy fails: **colectomy** — do not delay surgery in deteriorating patients

**Toxic megacolon**: Transverse colon >6 cm on AXR with systemic toxicity. Urgent surgical review. Risk of perforation is high.

**Maintenance**: 5-ASA long-term (reduces relapse and CRC risk). Azathioprine/6-MP for steroid-dependent disease. Biologics (infliximab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) for moderate-severe refractory disease.

**CRC surveillance**: Colonoscopy every 1–5 years after 8–10 years of extensive colitis (interval based on risk factors including PSC, disease extent, and activity).

### Crohn's Disease

**Induction**: Oral prednisolone for active flares. **Exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN)** — polymeric formula diet — is first-line in children (avoids steroids) and increasingly used in adults, particularly pre-operatively.

**Maintenance**: Azathioprine/6-MP (check TPMT activity before starting). Anti-TNF biologics (infliximab, adalimumab) for moderate-severe disease, fistulising disease, or steroid-refractory disease. Vedolizumab or ustekinumab for anti-TNF failure.

**Perianal fistula**: Antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) for symptom control. Infliximab for fistula closure. Surgical drainage of any associated abscess before or alongside biologic therapy.

**Surgery in Crohn's**: Not curative — disease recurs at the anastomosis. Reserved for complications (obstruction, abscess, fistulae, failure of medical therapy). Bowel-conserving resections preferred.

### Common Principles

Before starting biologics: screen for latent TB (IGRA) and hepatitis B. Live vaccines are contraindicated during biologic therapy. Monitor thiopurines with 3-monthly FBC (myelosuppression) and LFTs (hepatotoxicity).

## Complications

**UC**: Toxic megacolon, colorectal carcinoma (risk rises with extent and duration), massive haemorrhage, perforation.

**Crohn's**: Intestinal obstruction (strictures), enteric fistulae (to skin, bladder, vagina), abdominal abscess, perianal sepsis, short bowel syndrome, malnutrition and micronutrient deficiency (B12, iron, zinc, fat-soluble vitamins).

**Both**: Osteoporosis (steroid use), opportunistic infections from immunosuppression.

## Clinical Insight

Always exclude *C. difficile* before escalating immunosuppression. Giving IV steroids or biologics to a patient with infective colitis makes the infection worse. The stool sample comes before the steroid prescription.

PSC follows an independent course from UC — the bowel can be in complete remission while PSC progresses silently. Any UC patient with an unexplained elevated ALP requires MRCP. The risk of cholangiocarcinoma in PSC makes this a surveillance and management priority.

Toxic megacolon is a radiological diagnosis. Order an AXR in every patient with acute severe colitis on admission and daily during the inpatient episode. A patient who looks clinically stable may have a colon hours from perforation.
