## Overview

Cardiac arrest is the cessation of effective cardiac mechanical activity, resulting in loss of consciousness, absent pulse, and absent effective breathing. Without intervention, irreversible brain injury begins within 4–6 minutes and death follows within 10 minutes. The survival chain — early recognition, early bystander CPR, early defibrillation, advanced life support, and post-resuscitation care — is the framework through which modern resuscitation systems attempt to convert otherwise fatal events into survivable ones. In witnessed in-hospital cardiac arrest with a shockable rhythm, survival rates exceed 35% in well-organised systems. The single most time-sensitive intervention is defibrillation for ventricular fibrillation: for every minute without defibrillation, survival decreases by 7–10%.

## Pathophysiology

### Mechanisms of Cardiac Arrest

**Ventricular fibrillation (VF):** Chaotic, disorganised electrical activity in the ventricles — no coordinated mechanical contraction. The most common initial rhythm in witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA). Caused by myocardial ischaemia (most common — acute MI with ventricular irritability), electrolyte disturbances (hypokalaemia, hyperkalaemia, hypomagnesaemia), drug toxicity (TCAs, antiarrhythmics), hypothermia, Brugada syndrome, long QT syndrome. Defibrillation (unsynchronised DC shock) depolarises the entire myocardium simultaneously, allowing the sinoatrial node to re-establish organised rhythm.

**Pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT):** Organised ventricular electrical activity at >100 bpm (usually >150 bpm) but no effective cardiac output. Treated identically to VF (defibrillation).

**Pulseless electrical activity (PEA):** Organised (or semi-organised) electrical activity on ECG but no cardiac output. The most common initial rhythm in in-hospital cardiac arrest and non-cardiac causes. Always has a reversible cause — the 4Hs and 4Ts (see below). PEA cannot be defibrillated — the treatment is CPR + reversal of the underlying cause.

**Asystole:** Complete absence of electrical and mechanical activity. The worst prognostic rhythm. Ensure it is truly asystole (not fine VF — check leads, increase gain); a potentially shockable rhythm should never be treated as asystole. Not defibrillatable — treat with CPR + adrenaline + reversible cause.

### Reversible Causes — The 4Hs and 4Ts

| 4Hs | 4Ts |
|-----|-----|
| **H**ypoxia → ensure airway + ventilation | **T**ension pneumothorax → needle decompression |
| **H**ypovolaemia → IV fluid bolus | **T**amponade (cardiac) → pericardiocentesis |
| **H**ypo/hyperkalaemia, metabolic → VBG | **T**oxins/drugs → antidotes (naloxone, bicarbonate) |
| **H**ypothermia → core temperature | **T**hrombosis (PE or coronary) → thrombolysis/PCI |

These must be actively considered and treated during every resuscitation attempt — ignoring reversible causes converts a potentially survivable arrest into a futile one.

### Why CPR Buys Time — But Not Much

External chest compressions generate approximately 25–30% of normal cardiac output by direct compression of the heart between the sternum and spine, and by the thoracic pump mechanism (changes in intrathoracic pressure during compression/relaxation cycle). This is sufficient to maintain some cerebral and coronary perfusion — but not enough to restore consciousness or spontaneous circulation. CPR extends the window for definitive treatment (defibrillation, reversal of cause) but does not itself cause ROSC (return of spontaneous circulation) except in rare circumstances.

**The importance of hands-off time:** Interruptions in chest compressions (for intubation, rhythm checks, pulse checks) cause coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure to drop immediately. Even 10 seconds of no compressions can reduce the chance of successful defibrillation. Every interruption is potentially fatal.

## Clinical Presentation

Cardiac arrest is a clinical diagnosis made within seconds:
1. **Unresponsive** (shout, shake the shoulders)
2. **No normal breathing** (look for chest rise for no more than 10 seconds; agonal gasps are NOT normal breathing and should trigger CPR)
3. **No pulse** (feel carotid for no more than 10 seconds; trained rescuers only — untrained rescuers should not check pulse)

Do not waste time looking for subtle signs of life — if in doubt, start CPR. The harm from performing CPR on a patient who is not in arrest is minimal (rib fractures, soft tissue bruising). The harm from failing to start CPR on a patient in arrest is death.

## Diagnosis

**Cardiac monitor/defibrillator as soon as available:** Identify the rhythm — shockable (VF/pVT) vs non-shockable (PEA/asystole). This determines the algorithm pathway.

**Rhythm differentiation:**
- **Coarse VF:** Irregular chaotic waveforms without organised complexes → shock immediately
- **Fine VF:** Low-amplitude chaotic waveform — can be mistaken for asystole; increase gain, check another lead
- **pVT:** Rapid, wide-complex, organised rhythm without pulse
- **PEA:** Recognisable QRS complexes (may be wide or narrow), no pulse
- **Asystole:** Flat line ± P-wave activity; confirm in two leads

**Reversible cause investigation (during CPR — do not pause for investigations):**
- Venous blood gas: K+ (hyperkalaemia: peaked T waves, wide QRS — treat with calcium chloride + insulin + dextrose), glucose, pH, lactate
- eFAST: cardiac tamponade (pericardial effusion on echo), tension pneumothorax, PE (right heart strain, D-sign)
- **Pulse check with echo (ultrasound in CPR):** 10-second echo window during rhythm/pulse check — pericardial effusion, wall motion abnormality (suggests MI), empty ventricle (hypovolaemia), PE

## Management

### Adult Advanced Life Support (ALS) Algorithm — 2021 Resuscitation Council UK

**All cardiac arrests:**
1. **Start CPR 30:2** (30 compressions : 2 ventilations) immediately
2. Attach defibrillator/monitor as soon as available
3. **Minimise interruptions** — if advanced airway (ETT/supraglottic), switch to continuous compressions at 100–120/min (no pauses for ventilation) with ventilation at 10 breaths/min asynchronously

**Shockable (VF/pVT):**
1. Defibrillate: **360 J monophasic** or **150–200 J biphasic** (device-dependent)
2. Resume CPR immediately for **2 minutes** — do NOT check pulse immediately after shock (the heart needs a 2-minute CPR period to recover perfusion pressure before it can sustain ROSC)
3. Recheck rhythm after 2 minutes
4. If still VF/pVT: second shock + 2 min CPR
5. If still VF/pVT: third shock → immediately give **adrenaline 1 mg IV** + **amiodarone 300 mg IV** (both given just after the third shock, then CPR continues)
6. Adrenaline: repeat 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes (alternating loops)
7. Amiodarone: second dose 150 mg IV after the fifth shock if still in VF/pVT

**Non-shockable (PEA/asystole):**
1. CPR 30:2 (or continuous if advanced airway)
2. **Adrenaline 1 mg IV as soon as IV access achieved** — then every 3–5 minutes (every other 2-minute loop)
3. Actively look for and treat reversible causes (4Hs + 4Ts) throughout
4. No defibrillation (unshockable rhythm)

### High-Quality CPR — Technique

| Parameter | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| Rate | 100–120 compressions/min |
| Depth | 5–6 cm (adults) |
| Recoil | Allow full chest recoil between compressions — do not lean on the chest |
| Interruptions | Minimise to <10 seconds (maximum pause for rhythm check/defibrillation) |
| Rescuer fatigue | Switch compressors every 2 minutes |
| Position | Heel of hand on lower half of sternum; arms straight |

**Mechanical CPR devices** (AutoPulse, LUCAS): comparable outcome to manual CPR in RCTs (PARAMEDIC, CIRC trials); useful for prolonged resuscitation (e.g., during catheter lab PCI) and transport — do not pause for device application.

### Airway Management During CPR

**Initial:** Airway opening manoeuvres (head tilt-chin lift or jaw thrust), bag-mask ventilation with 100% O₂, two-person technique for bag-mask (better seal). Do NOT interrupt compressions for airway management.

**Advanced airway:**
- Endotracheal intubation (ETT): gold standard for airway protection, allows continuous compressions; should only be performed by experienced operators with minimal interruption (<10 seconds)
- Supraglottic airway (LMA, i-gel): suitable alternative if intubation not immediately feasible; less aspiration protection; appropriate for non-hospital or operator-inexperienced settings

**Do NOT hyperventilate:** Over-ventilation increases intrathoracic pressure → impedes venous return → reduces cardiac output. Target: 10 breaths/min with advanced airway.

**Waveform capnography:** Attach as soon as intubated — confirms ETT position (CO₂ confirms tracheal placement), and ETCO₂ correlates with cardiac output during CPR. **ETCO₂ <10 mmHg** after 20 minutes of optimal CPR = strong predictor of failure to achieve ROSC (informative for stopping resuscitation). **Sudden rise in ETCO₂ during CPR** may indicate ROSC.

### Drugs in Cardiac Arrest

**Adrenaline (epinephrine):**
- Mechanism: alpha-1 vasoconstriction → increases aortic diastolic pressure → improves coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure during CPR
- Dose: 1 mg (1 mL of 1:1000 or 10 mL of 1:10,000) IV every 3–5 min
- **PARAMEDIC-2 trial (2018):** Adrenaline vs. placebo in OHCA — adrenaline significantly increased 30-day survival (3.2% vs 2.4%, NNT 112), but **no improvement in 3-year survival** and significantly increased severe neurological impairment in survivors. The benefit is modest; the mechanism (increased short-term ROSC but potential cerebral vasoconstriction and post-ROSC myocardial injury) may offset longer-term outcomes. Current consensus: give adrenaline; the evidence supports short-term benefit.
- Timing: For shockable rhythms, **delay adrenaline** until after the third shock (to avoid vasoconstriction impairing defibrillation threshold and coronary filling); for non-shockable rhythms, give adrenaline as soon as IV access available.

**Amiodarone:**
- Blocks sodium, potassium, calcium channels and beta receptors; prolongs action potential duration; reduces defibrillation threshold
- **300 mg IV** after the third shock in refractory VF/pVT; second dose 150 mg after fifth shock
- Dose diluted in 20 mL 5% dextrose (poorly soluble in saline)
- Lidocaine (lignocaine) 100 mg IV: alternative if amiodarone unavailable; inferior evidence base

**Sodium bicarbonate:** NOT routinely recommended — CO₂ generation from bicarbonate worsens intracellular acidosis. Use only for specific indications: tricyclic antidepressant overdose, hyperkalaemia, prolonged cardiac arrest (>20 min) with severe acidosis documented on VBG.

**Calcium chloride:** 10 mL of 10% IV for hyperkalaemia (peaked T waves, wide QRS), hypocalcaemia, calcium channel blocker overdose.

**Thrombolysis during CPR:** If suspected massive PE as the cause of cardiac arrest — tenecteplase 500 mcg/kg IV bolus or alteplase 50 mg IV; continue CPR for ≥60–90 minutes post-thrombolysis before considering stopping (thrombolysis takes time to work). Evidence: case series; no RCT in this specific setting.

### Post-Resuscitation Care (ROSC)

**Targeted Temperature Management (TTM):**
- **TTMII trial (2021):** TTM at 36°C was not inferior to 33°C for death or neurological function at 6 months in OHCA with shockable rhythm. Current guidance: **avoid fever (temperature >37.7°C) for 72 hours post-ROSC** using active normothermia. The era of mandatory therapeutic hypothermia at 33°C has been replaced by fever prevention. Sedation and paralysis may be needed to prevent shivering.
- **No benefit from temperature management was shown in non-shockable OHCA** (TTM2 subgroup analysis).

**Coronary angiography:**
- For ROSC after cardiac arrest with ST elevation on 12-lead ECG: **emergency coronary angiography ± PCI** (equivalent to STEMI management)
- For ROSC after cardiac arrest without ST elevation: COACT and TOMAHAWK trials showed **no benefit from immediate coronary angiography** vs. delayed (3–5 days later) in terms of survival — routine immediate angio is not warranted in all post-arrest patients without ST elevation

**Neurological prognostication:**
- Not before **72 hours** after cardiac arrest (sedation, hypothermia, and post-arrest encephalopathy can mimic permanent brain injury)
- Multimodal approach: clinical examination (absent pupillary responses, absent corneal reflexes at 72h = poor prognosis), EEG (absent cortical activity, burst suppression), SSEP (absent N20 peaks bilaterally = very poor prognosis), CT/MRI brain (diffuse anoxic injury), neuron-specific enolase (NSE >33 mcg/L at 48–72h = poor prognosis with high specificity)
- Self-fulfilling prophecy bias: avoid premature withdrawal based on single poor prognostic indicator

### When to Stop Resuscitation

- No reversible cause identified
- Asystole throughout (no VF/pVT at any point) for >20 minutes with no reversible cause
- ETCO₂ <10 mmHg after 20 minutes of optimal CPR
- **Exceptions:** Hypothermia ("not dead until warm and dead" — continue CPR until core temperature ≥32°C or ECMO available), drowning in cold water, toxicological cause

### Perimortem Caesarean Section

In cardiac arrest at ≥23 weeks gestation: **perimortem Caesarean section within 4 minutes of arrest** if no ROSC — this relieves aortocaval compression and may be the cause of ROSC in the mother (not primarily a foetal rescue procedure, though neonatal survival is also possible). Delivery should not delay CPR; ideally performed in situ while compressions continue.

## Complications

- **Post-ROSC:** Myocardial stunning (reduced EF for 24–72h post-arrest — dobutamine if severe), re-arrest (5–20%), ARDS, AKI, post-anoxic encephalopathy
- **CPR complications:** Rib fractures (~50% even with correct technique), sternal fracture, pneumothorax, haemopericardium, liver laceration, regurgitation and aspiration
- **Therapeutic hypothermia complications (33°C — now less commonly used):** Bradycardia, coagulopathy, increased infection risk, hypokalaemia, diuresis (cold diuresis), insulin resistance
- **Neurological:** Post-anoxic myoclonus, status epilepticus (EEG monitoring warranted for 24–48h post-ROSC), hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy, locked-in syndrome, vegetative state
- **Psychological:** Post-traumatic stress in survivors, depression; ICU survivors may have persisting cognitive impairment

## Clinical Insight

The PARAMEDIC-2 trial changed the intellectual framework for adrenaline in cardiac arrest. The drug clearly increases short-term ROSC and 30-day survival, but the 3-year data showed no meaningful survival benefit and possibly increased severe neurological impairment. The mechanistic explanation: adrenaline causes cerebral vasoconstriction (via alpha-1 receptors) while increasing myocardial perfusion — it may restart the heart but at a cost to the brain. This doesn't mean "don't give adrenaline" — the current evidence still supports giving it — but it does mean that the drug that "restarts the heart" is not as straightforwardly beneficial as decades of assumption suggested.

The 2-minute CPR cycle before rhythm check is one of the most important changes in the modern ALS algorithm and is routinely misunderstood. After a defibrillation shock, even if successful, the heart requires 2 minutes of CPR to rebuild coronary perfusion pressure before it can sustain a spontaneous rhythm. Checking the pulse immediately after a shock — which reveals no output even though VF has terminated — and interpreting this as "the shock failed" leads to unnecessary repeated shocks and dangerous hands-off time. Trust the algorithm: shock → CPR → 2 minutes → check rhythm.

The waveform capnography trend during CPR is arguably the most useful real-time CPR quality monitor available. A persistent ETCO₂ below 10 mmHg despite 20 minutes of well-performed CPR tells you that cardiac output is inadequate to generate CO₂ delivery to the lungs — a strong predictor of non-survival. A sudden spike in ETCO₂ to >30 mmHg without any change in CPR technique almost invariably means ROSC has occurred — check the pulse and be ready to stop compressions.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 58-year-old, witnessed VF arrest in the ED, preceded by chest pain. Coarse VF on monitor.

**Step 1 — Immediate actions (0–30 seconds):**
- Shout for help + activate crash team
- Start CPR: 30 compressions (100–120/min, 5–6 cm depth) : 2 ventilations with bag-mask + 100% O₂
- Apply defibrillator pads (do not stop compressions)

**Step 2 — First shock (as soon as defibrillator charged):**
- Charge to 200 J biphasic (or 360 J monophasic)
- "Stand clear" — confirm nobody is touching the patient
- Shock → immediately resume CPR (no pulse check) for 2 minutes
- This is a witnessed VF in the ED — the faster the first shock, the better the outcome

**Step 3 — Rhythm checks and drug delivery:**
- After 2 min CPR: rhythm check
- If still VF → second shock → 2 min CPR
- If still VF → third shock → **immediately give adrenaline 1 mg IV + amiodarone 300 mg IV** → 2 min CPR
- Continue adrenaline 1 mg IV every 3–5 minutes (every other cycle)

**Step 4 — Airway:**
- Video laryngoscopy for RSI by most experienced operator available (brief pause <10 seconds for intubation)
- Confirm ETT with waveform capnography — if ETCO₂ detected, tube is in trachea
- Switch to continuous compressions + 10 breaths/min asynchronous

**Step 5 — Reversible causes:**
- This patient had chest pain before arrest → **coronary thrombosis (acute MI) is the most likely cause**
- VBG: K+ 4.1, pH 7.18 (lactic acidosis from arrest), glucose 8.1 — no metabolic cause
- 12-lead ECG when rhythm restores to assess for STEMI

**Step 6 — ROSC achieved after third shock (common scenario in witnessed VF):**
- ETCO₂ spikes to 38 mmHg → confirm pulse (carotid) → present → ROSC
- Post-ROSC care:
  - 12-lead ECG: ST elevation in V1–V4 → **emergency coronary angiography + primary PCI** (STEMI pathway)
  - Avoid fever: active normothermia (target 36°C for 72h)
  - Mechanical ventilation (SpO₂ 94–98%, PaCO₂ 35–45 mmHg — avoid hyperoxia and hypocapnia)
  - ICU transfer for haemodynamic monitoring + neurological prognostication at 72h

**Exam trigger:** *Witnessed VF arrest + coarse VF on monitor* → immediate defibrillation + resume CPR. The distractor is giving adrenaline before the first shock (incorrect — adrenaline delays in shockable rhythms until after third shock). Another distractor is checking the pulse immediately after the shock — resume CPR immediately without pulse check.
