## Overview

The red eye is one of the most common presentations in both primary care and emergency medicine, and it spans a spectrum from the entirely benign (conjunctivitis) to the immediately sight-threatening (acute angle-closure glaucoma, corneal ulcer with impending perforation). The critical skill is not knowing all causes of red eye — it is reliably identifying the small proportion that require emergency ophthalmological treatment within hours. The major determinants of urgency are: **visual acuity, degree of pain, and the character of any discharge**. A red eye with reduced visual acuity should be assumed to be an ophthalmological emergency until proven otherwise.

## Pathophysiology

### Anatomical Framework

The visible "redness" of a red eye reflects vasodilation of the conjunctival, episcleral, or ciliary vessels:

- **Conjunctival injection (superficial):** Vessels mobile with conjunctiva; blanches with topical phenylephrine; associated with conjunctivitis, episcleritis
- **Ciliary/deep injection (perilimbal flush):** Vessels do not move with conjunctiva; does NOT blanch with topical phenylephrine; radiates from limbus; indicates anterior segment disease (uveitis, keratitis, acute angle-closure glaucoma)

### Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG)

The anterior chamber angle is the drainage pathway for aqueous humour (trabecular meshwork → Schlemm's canal → episcleral veins). In anatomically predisposed eyes — **hypermetropic (far-sighted), small eyes, shallow anterior chamber, large lens** — the peripheral iris lies in close proximity to the trabecular meshwork.

**Trigger mechanism:** Pupil mid-dilation (low lighting, anticholinergic drugs, sympathomimetics, stress) → iris bows forward (pupil block — aqueous cannot flow from posterior to anterior chamber through pupil) → peripheral iris occludes trabecular meshwork → aqueous outflow obstructed → **acute IOP rise** (from normal <21 mmHg to 40–70 mmHg) → optic nerve ischaemia, corneal oedema, severe pain.

Precipitants: darkness, emotional stress, reading (miosis then dilation), dilating eye drops (fundoscopy), anticholinergic drugs (antihistamines, antidepressants, antispasmodics, antipsychotics), alpha-agonists (decongestants).

### Anterior Uveitis (Iritis)

Inflammation of the uveal tract (iris + ciliary body + choroid). Anterior uveitis (iritis/iridocyclitis) is the most common form. Mechanisms:
- **Autoimmune:** HLA-B27-associated (ankylosing spondylitis, Reiter's syndrome, psoriatic arthritis, IBD) — accounts for ~50% of anterior uveitis; recurrent unilateral
- **Infectious:** Herpes simplex, herpes zoster, toxoplasmosis, syphilis, TB — important to consider when uveitis is granulomatous or posterior

**Pathophysiology:** T-cell mediated inflammation of iris stroma → protein and cells leak into anterior chamber (flare and cells on slit-lamp) → adhesions between iris and lens (posterior synechiae) → irregular, sluggish pupil → ciliary spasm → photophobia + aching pain.

### Bacterial Conjunctivitis

The most common cause of red eye in primary care. Direct bacterial invasion of the conjunctival epithelium (usually Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae) → purulent discharge → matting of lids on waking.

**Gonococcal conjunctivitis:** *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* → hyperacute purulent conjunctivitis (hours to days) → copious purulent discharge → can penetrate intact cornea → corneal perforation within 24–48h. Medical emergency. Treat immediately with IV ceftriaxone + intensive topical.

**Neonatal conjunctivitis (ophthalmia neonatorum):**
- Chemical (first 24h): silver nitrate prophylaxis
- Gonococcal (2–5 days): IV ceftriaxone — emergency, risk of corneal perforation
- Chlamydial (5–14 days): oral/systemic azithromycin; can also cause pneumonitis

### Episcleritis vs Scleritis

- **Episcleritis:** Inflammation of the superficial episcleral tissue; sectoral or diffuse; mild discomfort; blanches with phenylephrine; idiopathic in most; benign, self-limiting; treat with NSAID drops or oral NSAIDs
- **Scleritis:** Inflammation of the sclera proper; severe boring pain (worse at night, radiating to jaw/temple); does NOT blanch; violaceous deep red; associated with systemic autoimmune disease (RA, SLE, vasculitis, GPA) in ~50%; can cause scleral thinning (scleromalacia perforans in severe RA) → perforation; treat with oral NSAIDs; immunosuppression for severe/necrotising

## Clinical Presentation

### Differential Diagnosis — Key Distinguishing Features

| Feature | Conjunctivitis | Anterior Uveitis | Acute Angle-Closure | Corneal Ulcer/Keratitis | Episcleritis |
|---------|---------------|-----------------|--------------------|-----------------------|-------------|
| **Pain** | Gritty/itchy | Aching, photophobia | **Severe + headache + nausea** | **Severe** | Mild |
| **Vision** | Normal | Mildly reduced | **Severely reduced** | Reduced | Normal |
| **Discharge** | Watery/purulent | Watery/none | None | Watery/mucopurulent | None |
| **Cornea** | Clear | Usually clear | **Hazy (oedematous)** | **Ulcer/opacity** | Clear |
| **Pupil** | Normal | **Irregular, small** (posterior synechiae) or sluggish | **Mid-dilated, fixed** | Normal | Normal |
| **IOP** | Normal | Normal/low | **Very high (40–70 mmHg)** | Normal/low | Normal |
| **Distribution** | Diffuse, bilateral often | Perilimbal flush | Perilimbal flush | Perilimbal (localised) | Sectoral, red |

### Immediate Red Flags — MUST Refer Ophthalmology Same Day

- **Reduced visual acuity** — any unexplained visual loss with red eye
- **Severe eye pain** (deep, boring, ciliary)
- **Hazy or opaque cornea**
- **Fixed dilated pupil** or abnormal pupil
- **High IOP**
- **Hyphaema** (blood in anterior chamber)
- **Hypopyon** (pus in anterior chamber — white layer at base of AC)
- Contact lens wearer with red eye (Pseudomonas corneal ulcer risk)
- **Vesicular skin rash around eye** (herpes zoster ophthalmicus)

### Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus (HZO)

VZV reactivation in the ophthalmic division of CN V → vesicular rash on forehead/tip of nose (Hutchinson's sign — nasociliary nerve involvement predicts ocular involvement) → corneal involvement (dendritic or disciform keratitis), anterior uveitis, episcleritis. Treatment: aciclovir 800 mg 5× daily × 7–10 days (or valaciclovir); topical steroid ± antiviral for keratitis; urgent ophthalmology review.

### Subconjunctival Haemorrhage

Spontaneous bleeding between conjunctiva and sclera — typically bright red, sharply demarcated, unilateral, asymptomatic. Alarming to patient but benign. Causes: Valsalva (coughing, straining), hypertension, anticoagulation, trauma. Treatment: reassurance — resolves in 2–3 weeks. Investigate if recurrent: check BP, coagulation screen.

## Diagnosis

### Clinical Assessment

**Visual acuity (Snellen chart or pinhole):** The single most important measurement in any red eye assessment. Reduced VA = serious cause until proven otherwise.

**Slit-lamp examination (in ED or ophthalmology clinic):**
- Fluorescein staining: identifies corneal epithelial defects (ulcer = geographic staining; dendritic ulcer = branching HSV pattern; punctate = dry eye/UV keratopathy)
- Cells and flare in anterior chamber: iritis/uveitis
- Hypopyon (pus settling by gravity in AC): severe keratitis, endophthalmitis
- Corneal clarity, depth of anterior chamber
- Posterior synechiae (iris adhesions to lens): chronic uveitis

**IOP measurement (tonometry):**
- Goldmann applanation: gold standard (in slit-lamp)
- Icare rebound tonometer: non-contact, portable, used in A&E
- Normal: 10–21 mmHg; >21 = elevated; >30 = urgent ophthalmic review; >40 = emergency

**Gonioscopy:** Angle assessment (by ophthalmologist) — confirms angle closure; differentiates open-angle from closed-angle glaucoma.

## Management

### Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma — Emergency Treatment

**Target: lower IOP as rapidly as possible to prevent irreversible optic nerve damage**

**Step 1 — Immediate medical treatment (initiated in A&E):**
- **Timolol 0.5% eye drops** (beta-blocker — reduces aqueous production) 1 drop immediately
- **Brimonidine 0.2% eye drops** (alpha-2 agonist — reduces production + increases uveoscleral outflow) 1 drop immediately
- **Dorzolamide 2% eye drops** (topical carbonic anhydrase inhibitor — reduces aqueous production) 1 drop
- **Acetazolamide 500 mg IV or oral** (systemic carbonic anhydrase inhibitor — strongly reduces aqueous production) — if oral tolerated; IV if vomiting (patient in scenario is vomiting → IV)
- **Analgesia and antiemetics** — pain and nausea management
- Supine or sitting — avoid prone positioning

**Step 2 — Pilocarpine eye drops:**
- **Pilocarpine 2–4% (cholinergic miotic)** — pulls peripheral iris away from trabecular meshwork by miosis
- ONLY effective once IOP has been lowered to < ~35–40 mmHg (at very high IOP, iris sphincter is ischaemic and does not respond to pilocarpine)
- Once IOP falls: pilocarpine 1 drop to affected eye; also give 1 drop to fellow eye (prophylactic — bilateral risk in predisposed anatomy)

**Step 3 — Definitive treatment:**
- **Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI):** Creates a hole in the peripheral iris → aqueous bypasses the pupil block → angle reopens → IOP normalises; definitive treatment; performed on both eyes (bilateral anatomical risk)
- Cataract extraction: Removes large lens → deepens anterior chamber → prevents angle closure; increasingly favoured in elderly or when cataract is present

**Step 4 — Systemic analgesia:**
- IV morphine titrated for severe pain
- Ondansetron/metoclopramide for nausea/vomiting

### Anterior Uveitis

- **Cyclopentolate 1% or atropine 1%** eye drops: Mydriatic + cycloplegic → breaks posterior synechiae, relieves ciliary spasm → reduces pain and photophobia
- **Prednisolone acetate 1% eye drops**: Topical steroid — reduces anterior chamber inflammation; use intensively (hourly initially) tapering over weeks
- **Investigate for systemic cause:** HLA-B27 (ankylosing spondylitis screen), rheumatology referral if recurrent
- **Systemic immunosuppression** (methotrexate, mycophenolate, adalimumab) for chronic or refractory uveitis

### Bacterial Conjunctivitis

- Most cases are self-limiting (7–10 days without treatment)
- **Chloramphenicol 0.5% drops** (or fusidic acid gel): Shorten duration by 2–4 days; recommended for contact lens wearers or severe cases
- **Gonococcal conjunctivitis:** IV ceftriaxone 1g IM/IV single dose; admit; intensive topical lavage; contact tracing

### Corneal Ulcer/Bacterial Keratitis

- **Urgent same-day ophthalmology review**
- Corneal scrape for MC&S before starting antibiotics
- **Intensive topical fluoroquinolone** (ciprofloxacin 0.3% or ofloxacin 0.3%) hourly for first 24–48h
- Avoid topical steroids until infection controlled
- Remove contact lens; never wear contact lens until healed

## Complications

- **Acute angle-closure:** Permanent optic nerve damage (irreversible visual field loss) if IOP not lowered within hours; corneal endothelial decompensation; cataract (lens opacity from ischaemia — glaukomflecken)
- **Bacterial keratitis:** Corneal perforation; endophthalmitis; permanent corneal scarring → irregular astigmatism → corneal transplant needed
- **Anterior uveitis:** Posterior synechiae → irregular pupil → angle closure glaucoma (seclusio pupillae if circumferential adhesion); cataract; band keratopathy (calcium deposition in cornea from chronic inflammation)
- **Untreated scleritis:** Scleral thinning → uveal tissue prolapses (staphyloma) → globe perforation
- **HZO:** Post-herpetic neuralgia (severe chronic pain in trigeminal distribution); corneal anaesthesia → neurotrophic keratopathy → recurrent corneal breakdown

## Clinical Insight

The mid-dilated fixed pupil is the single most important sign in the red eye assessment, because it is pathognomonic of acute angle-closure glaucoma and differentiates it from all other causes of a painful red eye. In uveitis, the pupil is small and irregular (ciliary spasm + posterior synechiae). In conjunctivitis or episcleritis, the pupil is normal. Only in acute angle-closure is the pupil fixed in mid-dilation — because at this pupil position, the pupil block effect is maximal (the iris is at the furthest point from both the open/small and closed/large pupil positions, maximising the force bowing the peripheral iris forward against the trabecular meshwork).

The clinical error of sending a patient with acute angle-closure home with analgesia and antiemetics (because the nausea and vomiting suggest a gastrointestinal cause) is a recognised and preventable catastrophe. Any patient with severe unilateral headache + nausea + vomiting + red eye + visual blurring must have IOP measured in A&E. The presentation mimics migraine, raised ICP, and GI illness. The three-minute test of visual acuity + pupil examination + IOP is sufficient to make the diagnosis.

Hyphaema (blood in the anterior chamber) and hypopyon (pus in the anterior chamber) are both ophthalmological emergencies requiring same-day specialist review. Hyphaema — visible blood layering at the inferior AC — indicates disruption of anterior segment vasculature (trauma, iris rubeosis from neovascular glaucoma, acute IOP spikes). Hypopyon — white cellular material layering inferiorly — indicates severe anterior chamber inflammation, most commonly from severe bacterial or fungal keratitis, or endophthalmitis. Seeing either on slit-lamp examination in A&E → immediate ophthalmology phone call.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 62F, 3-hour severe right eye pain, headache, nausea/vomiting, visual blurring, halos, hazy cornea, mid-dilated fixed right pupil, IOP 52 mmHg.

**Diagnosis: Acute angle-closure glaucoma — ophthalmic emergency**

**Step 1 — Immediate treatment (initiate in A&E, call ophthalmology simultaneously):**
- **Timolol 0.5% 1 drop right eye** (topical beta-blocker → reduces aqueous production)
- **Brimonidine 0.2% 1 drop right eye** (alpha-2 agonist)
- **Dorzolamide 2% 1 drop right eye** (topical CAI)
- **Acetazolamide 500 mg IV** (systemic CAI — vomiting prevents oral; consider IV infusion)
- IV antiemetic (ondansetron 4 mg) and analgesia (IV morphine titrated)

**Step 2 — Once IOP begins to fall (<35–40 mmHg):**
- **Pilocarpine 2% 1 drop right eye** — iris ischaemia prevents response at very high IOP; check repeat IOP before adding
- **Pilocarpine 2% 1 drop LEFT eye** — prophylactic, bilateral anatomical risk

**Step 3 — Ophthalmology review (urgent):**
- Slit-lamp: corneal oedema, shallow AC, closed angle
- Gonioscopy once IOP falls
- Target IOP <21 mmHg with medical treatment

**Step 4 — Definitive:**
- **Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI)** to both eyes — curative; prevents recurrence
- If IOP cannot be controlled medically → surgical iridectomy

**Step 5 — Contributory factors:**
- Was she started on any new anticholinergic/sympathomimetic medication? Check drug history.
- Hypermetropic eyes? Small eye? All family members at risk — screen for narrow angles

**Exam trigger:** *Painful red eye + fixed mid-dilated pupil + hazy cornea + reduced VA + nausea/vomiting* → acute angle-closure glaucoma — immediately lower IOP with topical (timolol + brimonidine + dorzolamide) + systemic (IV acetazolamide). The distractor is treating nausea/vomiting as a GI presentation and discharging without checking IOP or pupils.
