## Overview

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is infection of the lung parenchyma acquired outside hospital (or within 48 hours of admission). It is one of the most common causes of emergency hospital admission and a leading infectious cause of death globally. Accurate severity assessment at presentation drives triage, antibiotic choice, and disposition decisions.

## Pathophysiology

The lower respiratory tract is normally sterile, protected by mucociliary clearance, the cough reflex, alveolar macrophages, and secretory IgA. Pneumonia occurs when these defences are overwhelmed by an inoculum of sufficient size or virulence, or when host defences are impaired — by alcohol, aspiration, viral infection, immunosuppression, or structural lung disease.

**Pathological stages of lobar pneumonia (classically Streptococcal):**

1. **Congestion** — alveolar oedema and vascular congestion
2. **Red hepatisation** — alveoli packed with RBCs, neutrophils, and fibrin; lung is solid and airless
3. **Grey hepatisation** — RBC degradation; continued neutrophil and fibrin infiltration
4. **Resolution** — enzymatic digestion of exudate; macrophage clearance; structural recovery

Consolidation produces **V/Q mismatch** — alveoli are perfused but not ventilated, causing intrapulmonary shunting and hypoxaemia that does not fully correct with oxygen supplementation.

**Organisms by clinical context:**

| Organism | Clinical Clue |
|---------|--------------|
| *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | Most common CAP. Abrupt onset, rusty sputum, lobar consolidation, herpes labialis. Any age. |
| *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* | Young adults, insidious onset, dry cough, bilateral patchy infiltrates, cold agglutinins (haemolytic anaemia), erythema multiforme. Atypical. |
| *Legionella pneumophila* | Severe CAP. Hyponatraemia, diarrhoea, confusion. Hotel/travel/air conditioning exposure. Urinary antigen test. |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Post-influenzal pneumonia. Cavitation, rapid progression, high mortality. |
| *Klebsiella pneumoniae* | Alcoholics, diabetics. Upper lobe, "currant jelly" (bloody mucoid) sputum. Cavitation. |
| *Haemophilus influenzae* | COPD, elderly, immunocompromised. Exacerbation setting. |
| *Chlamydophila psittaci* | Bird exposure (psittacosis). Atypical presentation. |

## Clinical Presentation

**Typical CAP** (*S. pneumoniae*): Abrupt onset, high fever, rigors, productive cough with purulent or rusty sputum, pleuritic chest pain, and focal consolidation.

**Atypical CAP** (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, *Chlamydophila*): Gradual onset over days, dry cough, bilateral patchy infiltrates, systemic features (myalgia, headache, diarrhoea), extrapulmonary manifestations.

**Examination findings of consolidation:** Dullness to percussion, bronchial breathing (sound of turbulent flow in the large airways transmitted through consolidated lung), increased tactile and vocal fremitus, crepitations.

**Differential for fever and CXR opacity:**
- Tuberculosis — upper lobe, cavitation, chronicity, night sweats, weight loss, risk factors
- Pulmonary oedema — bilateral perihilar shadowing, raised JVP, cardiac history
- Pulmonary infarction from PE — pleuritic pain, pleural effusion, risk factors for DVT
- Lung cancer with post-obstructive pneumonia — lobar collapse, hilar mass, symptoms preceding infection
- Organising pneumonia — subacute, bilateral, often peribronchovascular, no organism identified

## Diagnosis

**Severity assessment — CURB-65 score:**

| Criterion | Points |
|----------|--------|
| Confusion (new, AMT ≤8) | 1 |
| Urea >7 mmol/L | 1 |
| Respiratory rate ≥30/min | 1 |
| Blood pressure <90 systolic or ≤60 diastolic | 1 |
| Age ≥65 years | 1 |

| Score | 30-day Mortality | Disposition |
|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| 0–1 | <3% | Consider home treatment |
| 2 | ~9% | Hospital admission |
| 3–5 | 15–40% | Consider HDU/ICU |

**Investigations:**
- CXR — confirm consolidation, identify complications (parapneumonic effusion, empyema, cavitation)
- FBC, U&E, CRP, LFTs, blood glucose
- Blood cultures × 2 — before antibiotics in moderate-severe CAP
- Sputum culture — before antibiotics if possible
- **Urinary Legionella antigen** — all severe CAP or epidemiological risk factors
- **Urinary pneumococcal antigen** — moderate-severe CAP; sensitive and specific
- HIV test — should be offered to all patients with CAP
- **CXR at 6 weeks** — mandatory to confirm resolution; failure to clear raises concern for underlying malignancy

## Management

### Antibiotics — NICE/BTS Guidance

| Severity | Setting | Regimen |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Low (CURB-65 0–1) | Home | Amoxicillin 500mg TDS for 5 days. Doxycycline if atypical suspected. |
| Moderate (CURB-65 2) | Hospital | Amoxicillin 500mg TDS + clarithromycin 500mg BD (covers atypicals) |
| Severe (CURB-65 3–5) | Hospital/ICU | Co-amoxiclav 1.2g IV TDS + clarithromycin 500mg IV BD. Add levofloxacin if Legionella suspected. |

Duration: 5 days for low-moderate; 7–10 days for severe. Antibiotics should be de-escalated when culture results are available.

**Atypical organisms** require a macrolide (clarithromycin) or tetracycline (doxycycline). Beta-lactams (amoxicillin) have no activity against *Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, or *Chlamydophila*.

**Supportive care:**
- Oxygen to maintain SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% if known COPD)
- IV fluids for dehydration or hypotension
- Analgesia for pleuritic pain (paracetamol; NSAIDs with caution)
- VTE prophylaxis (LMWH) in hospitalised patients
- Early mobilisation and physiotherapy

**Pleural effusion / empyema:** Any significant parapneumonic effusion should be sampled. If pH <7.2, glucose <2.2 mmol/L, LDH >1000 U/L, or frank pus — the effusion is complicated and requires chest drain insertion.

## Complications

- Parapneumonic effusion — develops in ~40%; most resolve with antibiotics
- Empyema — infected pleural space; requires chest drain and prolonged antibiotics
- Lung abscess — cavitation with air-fluid level; prolonged antibiotics, postural drainage
- Sepsis and multi-organ failure — particularly in elderly and immunocompromised
- Respiratory failure — type 1 from intrapulmonary shunting; may require NIV or intubation

## Clinical Insight

Legionella is reliably missed unless it is actively tested for. The clinical clues — hyponatraemia, diarrhoea, confusion, and a travel or institutional exposure history — should prompt immediate urinary Legionella antigen testing, which gives a result within minutes. Standard CAP antibiotics (amoxicillin) will not treat it.

A 6-week follow-up CXR is not optional in patients over 50 or in smokers. Resolution of consolidation typically takes 4–8 weeks. An opacity that persists beyond this raises the real possibility of an obstructing endobronchial lesion — malignancy or foreign body — and requires urgent CT and bronchoscopy.

*Streptococcus bovis* (now *S. gallolyticus*) bacteraemia is associated with colorectal carcinoma. While not a pulmonary pathogen, this association is worth knowing because it applies whenever the organism is found in any bacteraemia — including pneumococcal bacteraemia from contiguous spread in severe CAP. In any patient with *S. bovis* bacteraemia, arrange colonoscopy.
