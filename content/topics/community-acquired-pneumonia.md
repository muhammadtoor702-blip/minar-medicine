## Overview

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is infection of the lung parenchyma acquired outside hospital (or within 48 hours of admission). It is one of the most common causes of emergency hospital admission and a leading infectious cause of death globally. Accurate severity assessment at presentation drives triage, antibiotic choice, and disposition decisions — a decision that must be made within minutes of the patient's arrival.

---

## Pathophysiology

The lower respiratory tract is normally sterile, protected by mucociliary clearance, the cough reflex, alveolar macrophages, and secretory IgA. Pneumonia occurs when these defences are overwhelmed by an inoculum of sufficient size or virulence, or when host defences are impaired — by alcohol, aspiration, viral infection, immunosuppression, or structural lung disease.

**Pathological stages of lobar pneumonia (classically Streptococcal):**

1. **Congestion** — alveolar oedema and vascular congestion; lungs heavy, red, waterlogged
2. **Red hepatisation** — alveoli packed with RBCs, neutrophils, and fibrin; lung is solid and airless (hence "hepatisation" — resembles liver texture)
3. **Grey hepatisation** — RBC degradation continues; neutrophil and fibrin infiltration persists; lung grey-pink in colour
4. **Resolution** — enzymatic digestion of exudate; macrophage clearance; structural recovery over 4–8 weeks

Consolidation produces **V/Q mismatch** — alveoli are perfused but not ventilated, causing intrapulmonary shunting and hypoxaemia that does not fully correct with supplemental oxygen.

**Organisms by clinical context:**

| Organism | Frequency | Clinical Clue |
|---------|----------|--------------|
| *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | Most common (30–40%) | Abrupt onset, **rusty sputum** (oxidised RBCs in alveoli — pathognomonic), herpes labialis, lobar consolidation. Any age. |
| *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* | 5–15%; cycles every 3–4 years | Young adults, insidious onset over days, dry cough, bilateral patchy infiltrates, cold agglutinins (haemolytic anaemia, acrocyanosis), erythema multiforme. Atypical presentation. |
| *Legionella pneumophila* | 2–5%; severe CAP | **Hyponatraemia** (SIADH), diarrhoea, confusion, relative bradycardia. Hotel/travel/air conditioning exposure. Urinary antigen test — result in minutes. |
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | Uncommon; severe | **Post-influenzal pneumonia** — rapid progression, cavitation, abscess formation, high mortality. IV drug use. Bilateral infiltrates. |
| *Klebsiella pneumoniae* | Uncommon | Alcoholics, diabetics. **Upper lobe** cavitating pneumonia. "Currant jelly" sputum (bloody, mucoid). Bulging fissure sign on CXR. |
| *Haemophilus influenzae* | Common in COPD/elderly | Exacerbation setting. Often polymicrobial. Responds to amoxicillin/co-amoxiclav. |
| *Chlamydophila psittaci* | Rare | **Bird exposure** (psittacosis — parrots, parakeets, pigeons). Atypical; requires specific serology. |
| *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* | Common; underdiagnosed | Similar to Mycoplasma. Serology (IgM/IgG 4-fold rise). |
| *Pneumocystis jirovecii* (PCP) | Immunocompromised | HIV (CD4 <200/µL), immunosuppressive drugs. Bilateral perihilar infiltrates, bilateral spontaneous pneumothoraces, LDH elevated. |

## Clinical Presentation

**Typical CAP** (*S. pneumoniae*): Abrupt onset, high fever, rigors, productive cough with purulent or rusty sputum, pleuritic chest pain, and focal consolidation. Rusty sputum is the clinical signature of pneumococcal pneumonia.

**Atypical CAP** (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, *Chlamydophila*): Gradual onset over days, dry or minimally productive cough, bilateral patchy infiltrates, systemic features (myalgia, headache, diarrhoea), extrapulmonary manifestations disproportionate to respiratory findings.

**Examination findings of consolidation:**
- Dullness to percussion (dull, not stony — stony dullness = pleural fluid)
- **Bronchial breathing** — harsh, high-pitched inspiratory and expiratory sounds of equal length with a pause between; sound of turbulent flow in large airways transmitted through solid (consolidated) lung
- Increased tactile and vocal fremitus (vibration conducted better through solid than air)
- Crepitations (fine, inspiratory)

**Differential for fever and CXR opacity:**
- Tuberculosis — upper lobe, cavitation, chronicity, night sweats, weight loss, risk factors; always in the differential
- Pulmonary oedema — bilateral perihilar "bat wing" shadowing, raised JVP, cardiac history
- Pulmonary infarction from PE — pleuritic pain, pleural effusion, risk factors for DVT
- Lung cancer with post-obstructive pneumonia — lobar collapse, hilar mass, symptoms preceding infection; arrange follow-up CXR to confirm resolution
- Organising pneumonia — subacute, bilateral peribronchovascular opacities, no organism identified, responds to steroids

## Diagnosis

**Severity assessment — CURB-65 score:**

| Criterion | Points |
|----------|--------|
| **C**onfusion (new onset, AMT ≤8 or disorientation) | 1 |
| **U**rea (**BUN >19 mg/dL**) | 1 |
| **R**espiratory rate ≥30/min | 1 |
| **B**lood pressure <90 mmHg systolic or ≤60 mmHg diastolic | 1 |
| **A**ge ≥**65** years | 1 |

| Score | 30-day Mortality | Disposition |
|-------|-----------------|-------------|
| 0–1 | <3% | Consider home treatment with close follow-up |
| 2 | ~9% | Hospital admission |
| 3–5 | 15–40% | Consider HDU/ICU review |

CURB-65 guides severity classification, but clinical judgement always takes precedence — a young patient with a score of 1 but bilateral consolidation and a SpO₂ of 88% may need admission.

**Investigations:**
- CXR — confirm consolidation, identify complications (parapneumonic effusion, empyema, cavitation, multilobar involvement)
- FBC, U&E, CRP, LFTs, blood glucose
- **Blood cultures × 2** — before antibiotics in all moderate-severe CAP (CURB-65 ≥2); bacteraemia in ~25% of severe pneumococcal pneumonia
- Sputum culture — before antibiotics if possible (though low sensitivity ~50% even in pneumococcal disease)
- **Urinary Legionella antigen** — all severe CAP or with epidemiological risk factors; sensitivity >70%, specificity >99%
- **Urinary pneumococcal antigen** — moderate-severe CAP; sensitivity ~80% for bacteraemic disease; remains positive even after antibiotics started
- HIV test — should be offered to all patients admitted with CAP (occult HIV frequently presents with pneumonia)
- Atypical serology (Mycoplasma IgM, Chlamydophila IgG/IgM) — particularly for bilateral or atypical presentations; paired acute and convalescent samples at 4 weeks
- **CXR at 6 weeks** — mandatory to confirm resolution; failure to clear raises concern for underlying malignancy (especially in patients over 50 or smokers)

## Management

### Antibiotics — NICE/BTS Guidance

| Severity | Setting | Regimen | Duration |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Low (CURB-65 0–1) | Home | **Amoxicillin 500mg TDS** PO. Add doxycycline 100mg OD or clarithromycin 500mg BD if atypical features | 5 days |
| Moderate (CURB-65 2) | Hospital | **Amoxicillin 500mg TDS** PO + **clarithromycin 500mg BD** (covers atypicals) | 7 days |
| Severe (CURB-65 3–5) | Hospital/ICU | **Co-amoxiclav 1.2g IV TDS** + **clarithromycin 500mg IV BD**. Add **levofloxacin 500mg OD** if Legionella suspected or confirmed | 7–10 days |

**Why dual therapy in moderate-severe CAP?** Atypical organisms (*Mycoplasma*, *Legionella*, *Chlamydophila*) are intrinsically resistant to beta-lactams (amoxicillin). They have no cell wall and therefore no target for penicillin. A macrolide (clarithromycin) or fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin) covers these organisms.

**Specific organisms and exceptions:**
- *S. aureus* pneumonia (post-influenzal, cavitating): flucloxacillin 1–2g IV QDS if MSSA; vancomycin if MRSA
- *Klebsiella* (upper lobe, alcoholic): ceftriaxone 2g IV OD or cefotaxime; may need prolonged treatment for abscess
- *Legionella*: levofloxacin 500mg IV BD or erythromycin + rifampicin in severe disease; azithromycin is alternative
- Aspiration pneumonia: anaerobic organisms (Bacteroides, Peptostreptococcus); co-amoxiclav or clindamycin; right lower lobe or right middle lobe (due to angle of right main bronchus)

**De-escalation:** Antibiotics should be de-escalated when culture results are available. IV-to-oral switch when the patient is afebrile, improving, and able to swallow.

**Supportive care:**
- Oxygen to maintain SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% if known COPD)
- IV fluids for dehydration or hypotension
- Analgesia for pleuritic pain (paracetamol; NSAIDs with caution in renal impairment)
- VTE prophylaxis (LMWH) in all hospitalised patients
- Early mobilisation and physiotherapy

**Pleural effusion / empyema:** Any significant parapneumonic effusion should be sampled (ultrasound-guided). Complicated effusion criteria requiring chest drain: **pH <7.2**, glucose <40 mg/dL, LDH >1000 U/L, positive MC&S, or frank pus. Delay allows loculation and makes drainage progressively more difficult.

**Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP):** Pneumonia developing >48 hours after admission. Different organism spectrum: *Staphylococcus aureus* (including MRSA), Gram-negative rods (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, *Klebsiella*, *Enterobacter*). Requires broader spectrum antibiotics — co-amoxiclav or piperacillin-tazobactam ± MRSA cover (vancomycin or linezolid) depending on risk.

## Complications

- **Parapneumonic effusion** — develops in ~40% of hospitalised CAP; most resolve with antibiotics alone
- **Empyema** — infected pleural space; requires chest drain insertion and prolonged antibiotics (4–6 weeks); intrapleural fibrinolytics (alteplase + DNase) for loculated empyema (MIST2 trial)
- **Lung abscess** — cavitation with air-fluid level; prolonged antibiotics (minimum 4–6 weeks), postural drainage; surgery/bronchoscopic drainage if failing to resolve
- **Sepsis and multi-organ failure** — particularly in elderly and immunocompromised; early source control (appropriate antibiotics within 1 hour of sepsis recognition)
- **Respiratory failure** — type 1 from intrapulmonary shunting; may require CPAP, HFNO, NIV, or intubation

## Clinical Insight

Legionella is reliably missed unless actively tested for. The clinical clues — hyponatraemia, diarrhoea, confusion, and a travel or institutional exposure history — should prompt immediate urinary Legionella antigen testing, which gives a result within minutes. Standard CAP antibiotics (amoxicillin) will not treat it — atypical organisms have no cell wall and are intrinsically beta-lactam resistant.

A 6-week follow-up CXR is not optional in patients over 50 or smokers. Resolution of consolidation typically takes 4–8 weeks. An opacity that persists beyond this raises the real possibility of an obstructing endobronchial lesion — malignancy or foreign body — and requires urgent CT and bronchoscopy. Treating "recurrent pneumonia" in the same lobe without looking for an obstructing cause is a common diagnostic miss.

*Streptococcus bovis* (*S. gallolyticus*) bacteraemia has a well-established association with colorectal carcinoma, even without bowel symptoms. Any patient with *S. bovis* bacteraemia or endocarditis requires colonoscopy — arrange before discharge or within 3–6 months of completing treatment.
