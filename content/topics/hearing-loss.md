## Overview

Hearing loss is the most common sensory impairment worldwide, affecting over 430 million people. It is classified as conductive (impaired sound transmission through the outer and middle ear), sensorineural (impaired cochlear hair cell or auditory nerve function), or mixed. Accurate clinical distinction between these types directs investigation and management. Age-related hearing loss (presbycusis) is the most common cause in adults; middle ear effusion (glue ear) is the most common cause in children. Sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL) is an ENT emergency requiring same-day specialist assessment and treatment with corticosteroids. Acoustic neuroma (vestibular schwannoma) — a benign tumour of the eighth cranial nerve — classically presents with unilateral SNHL and tinnitus and must be excluded with MRI.

## Pathophysiology

### Conductive Hearing Loss — Mechanisms

Sound waves reach the cochlea via: external canal → tympanic membrane → ossicular chain (malleus, incus, stapes) → oval window → cochlear fluid.

Conductive hearing loss occurs when this pathway is disrupted:
- **External canal:** Wax (cerumen impaction), otitis externa, foreign body, exostoses (surfer's ear from cold water), atresia
- **Tympanic membrane:** Perforation, tympanosclerosis (calcification)
- **Middle ear:** Otitis media with effusion (most common in children), AOM, chronic suppurative OM, cholesteatoma (ossicular erosion)
- **Ossicular fixation:** Otosclerosis (abnormal bone remodelling → stapes fixation — most common cause of progressive conductive HL in young adults; autosomal dominant; associated with blue sclerae and pregnancy exacerbation)
- **Ossicular discontinuity:** Trauma, cholesteatoma erosion

**Maximum conductive hearing loss:** ~60 dB (the "air-bone gap" — difference between air and bone conduction thresholds)

### Sensorineural Hearing Loss — Mechanisms

Cochlear (most common) or retrocochlear (auditory nerve/central).

**Cochlear SNHL:**
- **Presbycusis (age-related):** Progressive loss of outer hair cells in basal cochlea (high-frequency sounds first) → symmetrical bilateral high-frequency SNHL; cochlear amplifier (OHC electromotility) gradually lost
- **Noise-induced:** Intense noise → mechanical trauma + oxidative stress to outer hair cells (particularly 4 kHz notch on audiogram — reflects resonance frequency of ear canal at occupational noise frequencies); cumulative, irreversible
- **Ototoxicity:** Aminoglycosides (gentamicin, amikacin) — irreversible; cisplatin — dose-dependent; loop diuretics (furosemide) — usually reversible; aspirin (reversible tinnitus/SNHL); quinine
- **Viral labyrinthitis:** Mumps (unilateral profound SNHL), CMV (congenital)
- **Ménière's disease:** Endolymphatic hydrops (excess endolymph in cochlea) → low-frequency SNHL initially, progresses to flat audiogram; associated with tinnitus and episodic vertigo

**Retrocochlear/neural:**
- **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma):** Benign Schwann cell tumour of CN VIII (usually vestibular division); unilateral progressive SNHL + unilateral tinnitus; MRI with gadolinium is gold standard; associated with neurofibromatosis type 2 (bilateral acoustic neuromas pathognomonic of NF2)
- Meningitis, CN VIII neuritis, brainstem lesions

### Sudden Sensorineural Hearing Loss (SSNHL)

Defined as ≥30 dB SNHL over 3 or more contiguous audiometric frequencies developing within 72 hours. Aetiology: viral (most common), vascular (cochlear artery occlusion), autoimmune, acoustic neuroma. Spontaneous recovery in ~50% without treatment. Corticosteroids improve outcome.

## Clinical Presentation

### Tuning Fork Tests — Rinne and Weber

**Rinne's test (512 Hz tuning fork — unilateral test):**
Place vibrating fork on mastoid (bone conduction = BC) until sound disappears → immediately place beside ear (air conduction = AC):
- **Rinne positive (AC > BC):** Normal OR sensorineural loss (SNHL)
- **Rinne negative (BC > AC):** Conductive hearing loss ≥25 dB — the middle ear is bypassed
- False negative Rinne: Profound unilateral SNHL — dead ear hears via bone conduction conducted to contralateral cochlea (Weber helps identify this)

**Weber's test (512 Hz tuning fork — lateralisation test):**
Place vibrating fork on vertex/forehead:
- **Lateralises to affected ear:** Conductive hearing loss on that side (masking of ambient noise → bone-conducted sound perceived louder)
- **Lateralises to normal ear:** Sensorineural loss on affected side (functioning cochlea hears better)
- **Midline (bilateral equal hearing):** Normal OR bilateral symmetrical hearing loss

**Interpretation table:**

| Finding | Conductive Loss (Affected Ear) | SNHL (Affected Ear) |
|---------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Rinne (affected ear) | Negative (BC > AC) | Positive (AC > BC) |
| Weber | Lateralises to affected ear | Lateralises to normal ear |

**This patient:** Right ear Rinne negative (BC > AC), Weber lateralises to right → **right conductive hearing loss**. But wait — this conflicts with noise-induced history which would give SNHL. Let me reconcile: if Weber lateralises to the WORSE ear (right) and Rinne is negative on the right — this is indeed right conductive loss. But the scenario says factory worker with progressive HL + tinnitus — cholesteatoma, otosclerosis, or chronic OM could cause this pattern. The left Rinne positive + Weber to right = right-sided conductive loss.

### History Features

**Conductive HL:** Difficulty in noise (relatively less affected — bone conduction intact), speaking softly, often improves if other ear is blocked

**SNHL:** Difficulty understanding speech especially in background noise; voices sound loud enough but distorted; tinnitus common; phonophobia/recruitment (sounds suddenly too loud)

**Bilateral vs unilateral:** Bilateral = presbycusis, noise-induced, ototoxicity, systemic disease; Unilateral = acoustic neuroma, Ménière's, SSNHL, cholesteatoma

**Tinnitus:** High-pitched bilateral = noise-induced SNHL; low-pitched unilateral = Ménière's; pulsatile = vascular (glomus tumour, AV malformation, carotid stenosis)

**Balance symptoms:** Vertigo with hearing loss = Ménière's, labyrinthitis, cholesteatoma into labyrinth

## Diagnosis

### Audiological Investigations

**Pure Tone Audiogram (PTA):** Measures hearing thresholds at each frequency (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 kHz) for air and bone conduction. Audiogram displays:
- Air conduction (AC): O (right), X (left) — tests whole pathway
- Bone conduction (BC): < (right), > (left) — bypasses middle ear; tests cochlear function
- Air-bone gap >15 dB = conductive component
- Normal hearing: 0–25 dB; mild loss: 26–40 dB; moderate: 41–55 dB; moderately severe: 56–70 dB; severe: 71–90 dB; profound: >90 dB

**Tympanometry:** Measures TM compliance vs ear canal pressure:
- Type A (normal compliance): Normal TM, normal middle ear
- Type B (flat, no compliance peak): Middle ear effusion or TM perforation
- Type C (pressure peak at negative pressure): Eustachian tube dysfunction

**Stapedial reflexes:** Absent ipsilateral reflex = conductive loss or CN VII lesion; absent contralateral = CN VIII lesion or brainstem pathology

**Speech audiometry:** Assesses word discrimination — poor discrimination disproportionate to pure tone loss suggests retrocochlear pathology

**Auditory Brainstem Response (ABR):** Objective hearing test (no patient response required); useful in infants (neonatal hearing screening) and suspected retrocochlear pathology

**MRI internal auditory meati (IAM) with gadolinium:** Gold standard for excluding vestibular schwannoma — mandatory for unilateral SNHL, unilateral tinnitus, asymmetrical SNHL (>10 dB difference at two frequencies between ears)

**CT temporal bones:** For conductive HL, cholesteatoma assessment, otosclerosis (halo sign around stapes)

## Management

### Cerumen (Wax) Removal

- Olive oil/sodium bicarbonate ear drops for 2–4 weeks to soften wax
- **Microsuction:** Safest removal method; safe for perforated TM; preferred
- **Syringing/irrigation:** Water jet; contraindicated with TM perforation, acute OM, previous ear surgery
- **Manual removal with hook/forceps:** By ENT

### Conductive Hearing Loss

- **Otosclerosis:** Hearing aid OR **stapedectomy** (stapes removed, replaced with prosthesis — excellent outcomes, >90% hearing improvement)
- **OME:** Grommets (see Otitis Media article)
- **TM perforation:** Myringoplasty (tympanoplasty) — fat plug, paper patch, or formal graft under GA
- **Cholesteatoma:** Mastoidectomy + ossiculoplasty (see Otitis Media article)

### Sensorineural Hearing Loss

**Presbycusis (age-related SNHL):**
- **Hearing aids:** Digital aids — most effective intervention; significantly improves communication, quality of life, reduces social isolation and depression; underprescribed (only 1 in 3 eligible adults uses hearing aids)
- **Aural rehabilitation:** Communication strategies, lip-reading training, assistive listening devices

**Sudden SNHL:**
- **Emergency same-day ENT referral** — do not delay for GP investigation
- **Oral prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day (max 60 mg) × 10–14 days with taper** — improves recovery rate; best evidence when started within 72h of onset
- **Intratympanic steroids:** Salvage therapy or primary (avoids systemic side effects); dexamethasone injected through TM under otoscopic guidance
- **MRI IAM** — all SSNHL cases require exclusion of acoustic neuroma

**Noise-induced SNHL:**
- Prevention is the only effective strategy — irreversible
- Hearing protection (earplugs, earmuffs) in high-noise environments
- Occupational health noise monitoring
- Hearing aids for established loss

**Ototoxicity:**
- Monitor audiogram before and during aminoglycoside/cisplatin therapy
- Adjust dose/duration of aminoglycosides; serum drug levels (trough monitoring)
- Once-daily aminoglycoside dosing associated with less nephrotoxicity (less clear for ototoxicity)

### Cochlear Implant

Surgically implanted electronic device that converts sound to electrical signals delivered directly to cochlear nerve fibres, bypassing damaged hair cells.

**Indications:**
- Severe-profound bilateral SNHL (pure tone average >70 dB at 2–4 kHz)
- Inadequate benefit from hearing aids
- Children: congenital profound SNHL → early implantation (before age 2–3) critical for speech/language development
- Post-lingual deafness in adults: excellent outcomes

**Contraindications:** Absent cochlear nerve, cochlear ossification (some cases), active middle ear infection

### Acoustic Neuroma Management

- **Watch and wait (serial MRI):** Small tumours, elderly, minimal symptoms — 50% do not grow in 3 years
- **Stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife):** For growing tumours <3 cm; halts growth in >90%; preserves residual hearing better than surgery
- **Surgical resection:** Large tumours (>3 cm), brainstem compression, failed radiosurgery; approaches: translabyrinthine (sacrifices hearing), retrosigmoid (hearing preservation possible), middle fossa

## Complications

- **Untreated presbycusis:** Social isolation, depression, cognitive decline (growing evidence that hearing loss is modifiable risk factor for dementia); hearing aids may partially mitigate cognitive decline risk
- **Cochlear implant complications:** Facial nerve injury (<1%), electrode mislacement, device failure, meningitis risk (vaccinate with pneumococcal vaccine pre-implant)
- **Acoustic neuroma growth:** Facial nerve palsy (VII runs adjacent to VIII in IAC), obstructive hydrocephalus from large tumours, brainstem compression
- **Noise-induced SNHL:** Tinnitus is the most disabling consequence; no cure; management with sound therapy, CBT, hearing aids

## Clinical Insight

The Rinne and Weber tests are among the most frequently misinterpreted bedside assessments in clinical medicine. The essential principle is that Weber lateralises to the WORSE ear in conductive loss (because in conductive loss, ambient noise is not reaching the middle ear on the affected side — so the bone-conducted fork signal appears relatively louder there), and to the BETTER ear in sensorineural loss (because the intact cochlea processes the signal more efficiently). The combination of Rinne negative in one ear + Weber lateralising to that ear = conductive loss in that ear — unambiguous.

The 4 kHz notch on the audiogram is pathognomonic of noise-induced hearing loss and reflects a fascinating aspect of ear anatomy. The resonant frequency of the adult external ear canal is approximately 3,500–4,000 Hz — this means high-intensity sounds are naturally amplified at this frequency before they reach the cochlea. Combined with the cochlea's tonotopic organisation (basal turn processes high frequencies), the basal outer hair cells at the 4 kHz region receive disproportionately intense stimulation — explaining the characteristic audiometric notch at 4 kHz even when the damaging noise was predominantly at other frequencies.

Sudden SNHL is frequently attributed by patients (and sometimes clinicians) to "wax" or a "blocked ear" — and this misattribution delays treatment at a critical window. The defining characteristic is the sudden onset (minutes to hours) of hearing loss, often noticed on waking, frequently unilateral, and accompanied by a sensation of aural fullness and tinnitus. The same-day steroid treatment window closes at 72 hours — after which the evidence for benefit is much weaker. This is an ENT emergency in the same category as retinal artery occlusion.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 58M, 6-month progressive right HL + right-sided tinnitus, 25-year factory work history. Normal otoscopy. Rinne negative right (BC > AC), Rinne positive left (AC > BC), Weber lateralises right.

**Tuning fork interpretation:**
- Right Rinne negative + Weber lateralises right = **right-sided conductive hearing loss**
- This is unexpected for noise-induced HL (which causes bilateral SNHL)
- With normal otoscopy → no obvious conductive cause visible
- **Differential for conductive HL with normal otoscopy: otosclerosis (most likely — stapes fixation, not visible on otoscopy), ossicular chain abnormality, early cholesteatoma, superior semicircular canal dehiscence**

**Step 1 — Investigations:**
- **Formal PTA with tympanometry** — confirm air-bone gap; type A tympanogram (normal compliance) in otosclerosis (unlike OME which gives type B)
- **Stapedial reflex testing** — absent in otosclerosis
- **CT temporal bones** — "halo sign" (lucency around stapes footplate/fissula ante fenestram) in otosclerosis
- **MRI IAM** — exclude acoustic neuroma (unilateral HL + tinnitus always needs acoustic neuroma exclusion, even if conductive pattern)

**Step 2 — If otosclerosis confirmed:**
- Options: **hearing aid** (effective, no surgical risk) OR **stapedectomy** (laser or conventional — removes fixed stapes, inserts prosthesis; >90% hearing improvement; small risk of SNHL from labyrinthine penetration)
- Age 58, active — discuss preference; stapedectomy offers definitive improvement

**Step 3 — Tinnitus management:**
- Tinnitus usually improves with restoration of hearing (aids or surgery)
- If persisting: sound therapy (white noise/tinnitus masker), CBT-based tinnitus retraining therapy (TRT)

**Exam trigger:** *Rinne negative + Weber to affected ear* → conductive loss. *Rinne negative + Weber to opposite ear* → profound SNHL ("dead ear"). The distractor is misinterpreting the Weber — remembering which direction it goes in which condition is the commonest tuning fork exam error.
