## Overview

Sepsis is defined as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. It is not simply severe infection — it is the point at which the host's immune response becomes more damaging than the infection itself. Globally, sepsis causes approximately 11 million deaths annually, and in high-income countries it remains the most common cause of in-hospital death and the leading cause of ICU admission. The history of sepsis management is a history of recognising that early, aggressive, protocolised care — the "Surviving Sepsis Campaign" approach — saves lives. The key insight from the ProCESS, ARISE, and ProMISe trials is that the pathophysiology of early septic shock is tissue hypoperfusion, and every hour of delay in restoring perfusion is associated with worsening outcomes.

---

## Pathophysiology

### The Dysregulated Host Response

Sepsis begins when a pathogen (bacteria, virus, fungi, parasites) triggers pattern-recognition receptors (Toll-like receptors, NOD-like receptors) on immune cells → activation of pro-inflammatory cascades (NF-κB → TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) → massive cytokine release. In normal infection, this is controlled and localised. In sepsis, the response becomes systemic and self-propagating:

1. **Vascular effects:** Endothelial activation → nitric oxide synthase overactivation → massive vasodilation → distributive shock. Endothelial damage → glycocalyx degradation → increased capillary permeability → fluid shifts into the interstitium → oedema + relative intravascular hypovolaemia ("third spacing"). Microthrombosis (from endothelial tissue factor expression) → organ microcirculatory failure.

2. **Myocardial dysfunction:** Septic cardiomyopathy — TNF-α, IL-1 directly depress myocardial contractility → reduced cardiac output + worsening hypotension (biventricular dysfunction in 40–50% of septic shock).

3. **Mitochondrial dysfunction:** Cytokines impair mitochondrial electron transport → reduced ATP production → cells cannot utilise oxygen even when delivered → cytopathic hypoxia. This explains why tissue hypoxia persists even after cardiac output and DO₂ are restored — and why ScvO₂ may normalise while lactate remains elevated.

4. **Coagulopathy:** Endothelial damage + tissue factor expression → activation of coagulation cascade → disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in severe sepsis → consumption of clotting factors and platelets → thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT/APTT.

### Sepsis-3 Definitions (Singer et al., JAMA 2016)

**Infection:** Documented or suspected microbial aetiology of a pathological process.

**Sepsis:** Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Organ dysfunction identified by **SOFA score increase ≥2 points** from baseline.

**Septic shock:** Sepsis + circulatory and cellular/metabolic abnormalities profound enough to substantially increase mortality: vasopressor requirement to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg AND serum lactate >2 mmol/L (despite adequate fluid resuscitation). Mortality: ~40%.

**qSOFA score (quick SOFA):** Bedside screening tool — NOT diagnostic; identifies patients at risk of poor outcome who need further assessment:
- Altered mental status (GCS <15) = 1 point
- RR ≥22/min = 1 point
- Systolic BP ≤100 mmHg = 1 point
- Score ≥2 = high risk for in-hospital mortality; triggers full SOFA assessment and consideration of ICU review

**SOFA score** (0–24): Assesses 6 organ systems: respiratory (PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio), coagulation (platelets), liver (bilirubin), cardiovascular (MAP + vasopressor dose), neurological (GCS), renal (creatinine/urine output). Score ≥2 above baseline = organ dysfunction (sepsis).

### Lactate in Sepsis

Serum lactate elevation in sepsis results from:
1. Tissue hypoperfusion → anaerobic glycolysis → lactate production (supply-dependent — addressable with resuscitation)
2. Cytopathic hypoxia — impaired mitochondrial oxygen utilisation despite adequate delivery (demand-independent — may not respond to resuscitation)
3. Reduced hepatic lactate clearance (in liver dysfunction)

**Lactate is both a diagnostic and prognostic marker:**
- Lactate >2 mmol/L with vasopressor requirement = septic shock definition
- Lactate >4 mmol/L = very high mortality risk (~40–60%); immediate resuscitation required regardless of blood pressure
- Lactate clearance (reduction of ≥10% after 2 hours of resuscitation) = marker of response and improved prognosis (LACTATE trial)

## Clinical Presentation

### Organ Dysfunction in Sepsis — SOFA Components

**Respiratory:** Tachypnoea, hypoxaemia (PaO₂/FiO₂ <400 = ARDS risk); SpO₂ <92% on room air.

**Cardiovascular:** Hypotension (MAP <65 mmHg), tachycardia; warm, flushed, bounding pulses in early distributive shock (vasodilation); cold, clammy, mottled in late/cardiogenic component.

**Neurological:** Confusion, agitation, drowsiness, coma — cerebral hypoperfusion + direct cytokine effects on the brain. New confusion in an elderly patient = sepsis until proven otherwise.

**Renal:** Oliguria (<0.5 mL/kg/h) or rising creatinine (sepsis is the most common cause of AKI in ICU).

**Haematological:** Thrombocytopenia (platelets <150 × 10⁹/L), DIC (prolonged PT/APTT, falling fibrinogen, raised d-dimers).

**Hepatic:** Raised bilirubin, elevated AST/ALT (ischaemic hepatitis or direct toxin effect).

### Common Sources of Sepsis

| Source | Key Organisms | Clinical Clues |
|--------|--------------|---------------|
| Urine (urological) | E. coli, Klebsiella, Proteus | Dysuria, loin pain, pyuria |
| Lung (pneumonia) | S. pneumoniae, S. aureus, Klebsiella | Productive cough, consolidation |
| Abdomen/biliary | E. coli, Klebsiella, Enterococcus, anaerobes | Abdominal pain, surgical history |
| Skin/soft tissue | S. aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes | Cellulitis, wound, IV site |
| CNS (meningitis) | N. meningitidis, S. pneumoniae | Photophobia, neck stiffness |
| Line/catheter (CLABSI) | CoNS, S. aureus, Candida | Indwelling line, no other source |
| Unknown source | Broad-spectrum empirical coverage | Systematic search required |

**Immunocompromised patients** (HIV, steroids, chemotherapy, transplant): unusual organisms — fungal (Candida, Aspergillus), Gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa), PCP (Pneumocystis). Empirical antibiotics must cover broader spectrum.

## Diagnosis

**Blood cultures (×2 sets from different sites):** Take BEFORE antibiotics (ideally — do not delay >45 minutes for cultures). Each additional set increases culture yield by ~10%. Positive in only 30–40% of sepsis cases — most are culture-negative.

**Lactate:** Point-of-care blood gas immediately. Venous lactate correlates well with arterial (acceptable for triage). If >2 mmol/L → sepsis protocol. If >4 mmol/L → septic shock; immediate management.

**Full blood count:** Leucocytosis (WBC >12 × 10⁹/L) or leucopenia (WBC <4 × 10⁹/L — worse prognosis), thrombocytopenia.

**Coagulation:** PT, APTT, fibrinogen — DIC screen.

**Biochemistry:** U&E (AKI, hyperkalaemia from AKI), creatinine, bilirubin (hepatic dysfunction), glucose (hyperglycaemia — insulin resistance; hypoglycaemia in liver failure), LFTs.

**Urinalysis + MSU:** Leucocytes, nitrites = UTI source; culture to guide deescalation.

**CXR:** Pneumonia (consolidation); ARDS (bilateral infiltrates); effusions.

**CT imaging:** If abdominal source suspected (CT abdomen/pelvis); if CNS involvement (CT head before LP).

**Procalcitonin (PCT):** Rises rapidly in bacterial sepsis (>0.5 ng/mL = sepsis, >2 ng/mL = likely bacterial sepsis); less elevated in viral infections. Useful for: confirming bacterial infection at presentation, guiding antibiotic duration (de-escalate/stop when PCT <0.5 ng/mL or falls >80% from peak — PRORATA trial).

## Management

### Surviving Sepsis Campaign — Hour-1 Bundle

(Updated 2018 — immediate measures within 1 hour of recognition):

1. **Measure lactate** (repeat at 2h if initial >2 mmol/L)
2. **Blood cultures** (before antibiotics — 2 sets)
3. **Broad-spectrum antibiotics** — within 1 hour; within 3 hours maximum (every hour of antibiotic delay increases mortality by ~7% — Kumar et al.)
4. **IV crystalloid** (30 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl or balanced crystalloid — Hartmann's) for hypotension or lactate ≥4 mmol/L (give as bolus + reassess; not all patients need the full 30 mL/kg)
5. **Vasopressors** if MAP <65 mmHg despite initial fluid resuscitation

**Antibiotic selection (empirical — source-guided):**

| Suspected Source | Empirical Regimen |
|-----------------|------------------|
| Urine (community) | Co-amoxiclav IV or cefuroxime IV (local resistance patterns) |
| Pneumonia (community) | Co-amoxiclav + clarithromycin IV (atypicals) |
| Abdomen/biliary | Piperacillin-tazobactam IV (or meropenem if severe) |
| Unknown source / severe | Piperacillin-tazobactam + gentamicin (or meropenem) |
| Neutropenic sepsis | Piperacillin-tazobactam IV + ciprofloxacin |
| Suspected meningococcaemia | Ceftriaxone 2 g IV + dexamethasone |
| Necrotising fasciitis | Meropenem + clindamycin (toxin suppression) + surgery |

**De-escalation:** Broad → narrow as cultures and sensitivities return. Tailor antibiotics within 48–72 hours based on culture results. PCT-guided duration protocols reduce antibiotic exposure without worsening outcomes.

### Fluid Resuscitation

**30 mL/kg crystalloid bolus:** Standard initial recommendation — given rapidly (15–30 minutes) for haemodynamic instability or lactate ≥4 mmol/L. After each bolus: reassess MAP, HR, lactate, clinical response.

**Balanced crystalloids (Hartmann's/lactated Ringer's) vs. 0.9% NaCl:**
- 0.9% NaCl → hyperchloraemic metabolic acidosis with large volumes (worsens lactic acidosis, impairs renal tubular function)
- SMART trial (NEJM 2018): balanced crystalloids reduced major adverse kidney events compared to 0.9% NaCl in critically ill patients
- Current SSC guideline: balanced crystalloids preferred

**Albumin 4%:** Not superior to crystalloid for initial resuscitation (SAFE trial). May be used for maintenance fluid in patients requiring large volumes.

**Avoid:** Hydroxyethyl starch (HES) and dextrans — associated with increased AKI and mortality in sepsis (CHEST, 6S trials).

**Dynamic assessment of fluid responsiveness:** Passive leg raise test (elevate legs 45°, observe MAP response — increase >10% = fluid responsive), pulse pressure variation (in ventilated patients — PPV >12% = fluid responsive). Static measures (CVP) are unreliable.

### Vasopressors and Inotropes

**Noradrenaline (norepinephrine) — first-line vasopressor:**
- Alpha-1 agonist → peripheral vasoconstriction → increased SVR → increased MAP
- Start at 0.01–0.02 mcg/kg/min; titrate to MAP ≥65 mmHg
- Target MAP: 65 mmHg for most patients; 75–80 mmHg for chronic hypertensives (SEPSISPAM trial — higher MAP target did not improve overall outcome, but reduced acute renal failure requiring renal replacement in chronic hypertensives)

**Vasopressin 0.03–0.04 units/min** (as adjunct to noradrenaline): When noradrenaline dose is high (>0.25 mcg/kg/min); allows noradrenaline dose reduction; reduces vasopressor requirements (VASST trial).

**Dobutamine** (inotrope — beta-1 agonist): Add if: persisting evidence of hypoperfusion (high lactate, low ScvO₂) despite adequate MAP and volume — indicates low cardiac output component; dose 2.5–20 mcg/kg/min; use with caution if tachycardia.

**Adrenaline:** Reserved for refractory shock; combine alpha + beta effects.

**Corticosteroids in septic shock:**
- Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day (50 mg 6-hourly or 200 mg/day infusion) for patients with refractory septic shock (noradrenaline >0.25 mcg/kg/min despite adequate fluid resuscitation)
- ADRENAL trial (low-dose hydrocortisone): did NOT reduce 90-day mortality vs. placebo, but significantly reduced duration of vasopressor use and time in ICU
- APROCCHSS trial (hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone): reduced 90-day mortality (43% vs. 49%) — ongoing controversy; current SSC: suggest steroids for refractory septic shock; do NOT use in non-shocked patients

### Source Control

**The most important but often overlooked component of sepsis management.** The infection must be removed or drained — antibiotics alone cannot control a collection or obstructed system:
- Urological sepsis (obstructed ureter/pelvic ureteric junction): emergency percutaneous nephrostomy or ureteric stent
- Biliary sepsis: ERCP (biliary decompression) within 24h
- Intra-abdominal abscess: CT-guided percutaneous drainage
- Necrotising fasciitis: emergency debridement within hours (mortality rises rapidly without surgery)
- Infected central line: remove the line

## Complications

- **Acute Kidney Injury:** Most common organ failure in sepsis; affects 40–60% of ICU patients; 25–30% require renal replacement therapy (continuous venovenous haemofiltration [CVVHF]); most AKI resolves but progression to CKD occurs in a significant proportion
- **ARDS:** Acute respiratory distress syndrome — bilateral pulmonary infiltrates + PaO₂/FiO₂ <300 mmHg; lung-protective ventilation (ARMA trial: 6 mL/kg tidal volume, plateau pressure <30 cmH₂O, PEEP titration); prone positioning 16h/day for PaO₂/FiO₂ <150 mmHg (PROSEVA trial — 60% mortality reduction in severe ARDS)
- **DIC:** Thrombocytopenia + prolonged PT/INR + falling fibrinogen + high d-dimers; manage with blood products (FFP, cryoprecipitate, platelets); avoid anticoagulation in bleeding DIC
- **Septic cardiomyopathy:** Reversible myocardial depression; echocardiography-guided management; usually resolves within 7–10 days
- **Post-ICU Syndrome (PICS):** Long-term cognitive impairment, psychological disorders (PTSD, depression, anxiety), and physical debility following ICU admission for sepsis; affects up to 70% of ICU survivors at 1 year

## Clinical Insight

The Hour-1 bundle is one of the best examples in medicine of a simple protocol directly saving lives. But it is also one of the most contested. The PROCESS, ARISE, and PROMISE trials (collectively ~4000 patients) found that "early goal-directed therapy" (specific ScvO₂ targets, strict CVP targets) did not outperform usual care. The key lesson: it was the early antibiotics and early vasopressors that saved lives — not the specific haemodynamic targets. Give antibiotics within 1 hour. Start vasopressors early. Do not over-resuscitate (CLASSIC trial: restrictive fluid strategy not inferior to standard resuscitation after initial 1L).

The lactate is not just a number — it is a trajectory. A lactate of 4.2 mmol/L that falls to 2.1 mmol/L at 2 hours (>50% clearance) predicts a very different outcome from a lactate of 4.2 mmol/L that is unchanged or rising after resuscitation. Serial lactate measurements are therefore more informative than a single value, and lactate-guided resuscitation (target ≥10% clearance per 2 hours) improves outcomes over CVP-guided resuscitation alone.

Choosing an antibiotic in sepsis of unknown source is a decision with real consequences — both under-treatment (missed coverage) and over-treatment (resistance selection, C. difficile risk) have harms. The "correct" empirical choice depends on the likely source, the local microbiology resistance patterns, and the patient's risk factors (prior antibiotics, healthcare exposure, immunosuppression). In genuinely unknown source septic shock, piperacillin-tazobactam covers the widest range of common pathogens without inducing resistance as rapidly as meropenem — reserve carbapenems for the immunocompromised, those with prior resistant organism colonisation, or documented ESBL producers.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** 74-year-old diabetic with CKD. Dysuria + rigors + right loin pain = urosepsis. New confusion, RR 26, HR 118, BP 88/52, SpO₂ 93%, Temp 39.2°C, lactate 4.2 mmol/L.

**Step 1 — Recognise septic shock:**
- Sepsis = SOFA ≥2 (confused + RR 26 + BP 88 = ≥3 organ dysfunction criteria)
- Septic shock = sepsis + vasopressor requirement (likely, given BP 88/52) + lactate 4.2 mmol/L (>2 mmol/L)
- qSOFA = 3/3 (confusion + RR ≥22 + SBP ≤100) = maximum risk score

**Step 2 — Hour-1 Bundle — simultaneously:**
1. IV access ×2 large-bore + blood cultures (×2 sets before antibiotics)
2. Urinalysis + MSU for culture + sensitivities
3. Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E (CKD baseline Cr 1.4 mg/dL — watch for worsening), LFTs, coagulation, glucose
4. **IV antibiotics within 1 hour:** Urosepsis in community → co-amoxiclav 1.2 g TID IV or cefuroxime 1.5 g TID IV (check local guidelines). Given CKD: adjust doses; avoid gentamicin (nephrotoxic) unless no alternative and levels monitored
5. **IV crystalloid:** 30 mL/kg = approximately 1.8 L Hartmann's rapidly; reassess after each 500 mL bolus (HR, BP, lung bases, urine output)
6. **Repeat lactate at 2 hours** — target ≥10% clearance

**Step 3 — Vasopressors (if MAP <65 mmHg after fluids):**
- Noradrenaline via central or peripheral IV access (peripheral can be used initially; central access when time allows)
- Target MAP ≥65 mmHg (consider ≥75 mmHg given chronic hypertension — she is likely autoregulating at a higher baseline)

**Step 4 — Oxygen:**
- SpO₂ 93% → supplemental O₂ via nasal cannula or simple face mask; target SpO₂ 94–98%
- ABG if not responding (assess PaO₂/FiO₂ — ARDS risk)

**Step 5 — Source control:**
- Renal ultrasound/CT if no response to antibiotics at 24h (obstructed ureter in diabetic patient = pyonephrosis requiring nephrostomy — the obstruction blocks antibiotic delivery)
- Urinary catheter (urine output monitoring, confirms response)

**Step 6 — ICU review:**
- Lactate 4.2 mmol/L + septic shock + elderly with comorbidities = direct ICU referral; level 3 care for vasopressor requirement

**Exam trigger:** *Elderly diabetic + loin pain + new confusion + HR 118, BP 88/52, lactate 4.2 mmol/L* → urosepsis with septic shock. Hour-1 bundle: cultures → antibiotics → fluids → vasopressors → lactate clearance. The distractor is waiting for urine culture results before starting antibiotics (fatal delay). The blood cultures go before the antibiotics; the antibiotics do NOT wait for cultures.
