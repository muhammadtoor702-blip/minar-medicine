## Pathophysiology Overview

Valvular heart disease results from stenosis (obstruction to flow) or regurgitation (incompetence/backflow). The haemodynamic consequences depend on which valve is affected and the acuity of onset.

**Key principle:** Acute valvular pathology (e.g., acute MR from papillary muscle rupture) is far more haemodynamically devastating than the same degree of chronic pathology — the heart has no time to compensate.

---

## Aortic Stenosis

**The most common valvular disease in developed countries.**

**Causes (White Coat Companion):**
- **Calcific/degenerative** — most common. Age >65. Progressive calcification of a normal tricuspid valve
- **Bicuspid aortic valve** — congenital (1–2% of population). Presents 20–30 years earlier (40s–50s)
- **Rheumatic heart disease** — leaflet fusion; now uncommon in developed world

**Pathophysiology:** Narrowing valve → LV must generate higher pressure to maintain forward flow → **pressure overload** → concentric LVH → initially compensated. Over time: reduced LV compliance → diastolic dysfunction → eventually systolic failure.

**The three cardinal symptoms (White Coat Companion) — each marks a turning point:**
1. **Angina** — mean survival ~5 years (increased O₂ demand + compressed coronary vessels)
2. **Syncope** — mean survival ~3 years (fixed cardiac output can't increase with exercise)
3. **Heart failure** — mean survival ~1–2 years (LV decompensation)

**Classic signs:**
- **Ejection systolic murmur** — harsh, crescendo-decrescendo, right upper sternal edge (2nd ICS), **radiates to carotids** (only murmur that does)
- **Slow-rising, low-volume pulse** (pulsus parvus et tardus)
- **Narrow pulse pressure**
- **Soft/absent A2**
- **Heaving, non-displaced apex** (pressure overload → concentric hypertrophy, not dilation)
- **S4** (stiff ventricle)

**Severity (echo):** Severe = mean gradient >40 mmHg, AVA <1.0 cm² (normal 3–4 cm²)

**Management:**
- No medical therapy slows progression — valve replacement is definitive
- **SAVR** (surgical AVR) — younger, lower surgical risk patients
- **TAVI/TAVR** — transcatheter approach, now expanding to lower-risk patients
- Symptomatic severe AS → valve replacement urgently (once symptoms appear, prognosis drops sharply)

## Aortic Regurgitation

**Causes:**
- **Acute**: Endocarditis, aortic dissection → surgical emergency
- **Chronic valve**: Bicuspid valve, rheumatic disease, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos)
- **Chronic root dilation**: Syphilitic aortitis, Marfan syndrome, ankylosing spondylitis → murmur heard at RUSB (root dilation AR)

**Pathophysiology:** Incompetent valve → blood regurgitates back into LV during diastole → **volume overload** → LV dilation and eccentric hypertrophy → initially compensated (large stroke volume). Eventually: LV dilates excessively → systolic dysfunction → HF.

**Wide pulse pressure** (systolic high due to large SV, diastolic low due to backflow).

**Classic signs:**
- **Decrescendo diastolic murmur** — left sternal border, sitting forward, expiration
- **Wide pulse pressure** (>60 mmHg)
- **Water-hammer pulse** (Corrigan's pulse) — bounding, collapsing
- **Head/uvula bobbing** (de Musset's sign)
- **Austin Flint murmur** — low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at apex (regurgitant jet partially closing mitral valve)
- **Quincke's sign** — capillary pulsations in nail beds

**Management:**
- Acute AR → emergent surgery (haemodynamically devastating)
- Chronic AR → medical management (vasodilators, ACEi) → surgery when symptomatic or LV dilating/EF falling

## Mitral Regurgitation

**Causes:**
- **Primary (valve leaflet):** Mitral valve prolapse (most common in developed countries), endocarditis, rheumatic disease
- **Secondary (functional):** Ischaemic (papillary muscle dysfunction or rupture), functional (LV dilation in DCM, HF)

**Pathophysiology:** Regurgitant flow back into LA → volume overload of LA and LV → LV dilation → eventually HF. LA dilation predisposes to AF.

**Acute MR** (papillary muscle rupture post-MI, acute endocarditis) → sudden massive volume into unprepared LA → acute pulmonary oedema → cardiogenic shock. Surgical emergency.

**Classic signs:**
- **Holosystolic murmur at apex** radiating to the **axilla**
- Soft S1 (valve not closing properly)
- Displaced, hyperdynamic apex

**Management:**
- Acute: emergent valve repair/replacement
- Chronic: medical HF treatment, diuretics. Surgery when symptomatic or EF falling (EF <60% in MR = already impaired)

## Mitral Valve Prolapse

Myxomatous degeneration of mitral valve leaflets → billowing into LA during systole.

**Signs:** **Mid-systolic click** followed by late systolic murmur. Click moves **earlier** with standing/Valsalva (decreased preload → smaller LV → earlier prolapse); moves **later** with squatting/leg raise (increased preload).

**Management:** Reassurance in most. Beta-blocker for symptomatic palpitations. Surgery if severe MR develops.

---

## Mitral Stenosis

**Cause:** Almost entirely **rheumatic heart disease** (molecular mimicry from GAS → valve leaflet fusion).

**Pathophysiology:** Obstruction to LA outflow → elevated LA pressure → pulmonary hypertension → RV failure. AF very common (enlarged LA).

**Classic presentation:** Young patient from developing country (Pakistan!), progressive dyspnoea, haemoptysis, palpitations (AF), systemic emboli.

**Classic signs:**
- **Opening snap** (abrupt halt of anterior leaflet movement) — follows S2. **Earlier OS = more severe stenosis** (higher LA pressure = valve opens sooner)
- **Mid-diastolic rumble** at apex (low-pitched, heard with bell)
- Loud S1, loud P2 (pulmonary hypertension)
- Tapping apex (palpable S1 due to thickened leaflet snapping open)

**Management:**
- Rate control for AF (digoxin, beta-blocker)
- Anticoagulation — warfarin (valvular AF). NOT DOACs.
- Percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty — definitive in suitable anatomy
- Surgical commissurotomy or MVR if balloon not suitable

## High Yield Pearls

- AS is the only murmur that radiates to the carotids — always auscultate the neck
- AS triad: angina → syncope → HF. Survival drops significantly once any symptom appears — intervene
- MVP click moves earlier with standing (decreased preload), later with squatting (increased preload)
- Mitral stenosis + young patient from Pakistan/developing country = rheumatic heart disease
- Earlier opening snap in MS = more severe stenosis (higher LA pressure forces valve open sooner)
- Acute MR post-MI (papillary muscle rupture) = cardiogenic shock + new holosystolic murmur → emergent surgery
- Valvular AF → warfarin, NOT DOACs (not validated in mechanical valves or rheumatic MS)
