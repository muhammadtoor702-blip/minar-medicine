## Overview

Valvular heart disease results from stenosis (obstruction to forward flow) or regurgitation (backflow due to an incompetent valve). The haemodynamic consequences depend on which valve is affected, the severity and duration of the lesion, and crucially — the acuity of onset. Acute valvular pathology (e.g., papillary muscle rupture in MI, endocarditis destroying a leaflet) is far more haemodynamically devastating than equivalent chronic disease, because the heart has no time for compensatory remodelling.

Four valves dominate clinical practice: the aortic and mitral valves on the left side (exposed to systemic pressures) and the pulmonary and tricuspid on the right. Left-sided lesions dominate in adults.

## Aortic Stenosis

### Pathophysiology

Progressive narrowing of the aortic valve orifice creates fixed obstruction to LV outflow. The LV must generate increasingly high pressures to maintain forward flow → **pressure overload** → **concentric LVH**. This initially preserves cardiac output, but diastolic dysfunction, reduced coronary perfusion reserve (the hypertrophied LV outstrips its blood supply), and eventually systolic failure ensue.

**The three cardinal symptoms mark a prognostic turning point:**

| Symptom | Mechanism | Mean Survival Without Intervention |
|---------|-----------|-----------------------------------|
| Angina | Fixed CO cannot increase coronary perfusion to meet hypertrophied LV demand | ~5 years |
| Syncope | Fixed CO cannot increase to meet exertional demand → cerebral hypoperfusion | ~3 years |
| Heart failure | LV decompensation | 1–2 years |

Sudden cardiac death can occur at any stage, including in asymptomatic patients.

**Causes:**
- **Senile calcification** — the most common cause in adults over 65; progressive calcium deposition on a tricuspid valve
- **Bicuspid aortic valve** — congenital; presents 20–30 years earlier (i.e., in the 40s–50s); requires CT aortogram to exclude associated coarctation or aortic root dilation (bicuspid aortopathy)
- **Rheumatic heart disease** — increasingly rare in the developed world; causes leaflet fusion; usually coexists with mitral disease

### Examination

- **Ejection systolic murmur** — harsh, crescendo-decrescendo; best heard at the right upper sternal edge (aortic area); **radiates to the carotids** — the only murmur that reliably does this
- **Slow-rising, low-volume pulse** (pulsus parvus et tardus — small and delayed carotid upstroke)
- **Narrow pulse pressure**
- **Soft or absent A2** — as the valve calcifies and becomes immobile, aortic valve closure becomes inaudible; in severe AS, A2 may be completely absent; eventually reversed splitting of S2 may occur (but is rare)
- **Heaving non-displaced apex** — concentric hypertrophy without dilation
- **S4** — stiff, non-compliant LV requiring forceful atrial contraction; heard best at the apex with the bell
- **Ejection click** — if the valve is still pliable (less calcified); clicks in early systole, disappears as calcification progresses

**Aortic sclerosis** — important differential: senile degenerative thickening without significant haemodynamic obstruction. There is an ejection systolic murmur, but no carotid radiation, normal pulse character and volume, and a normal S2. The murmur of aortic sclerosis does not radiate to the carotids and the pulse is normal — these two features alone distinguish it from AS.

### Investigations

- **ECG:** LVH with strain pattern (inverted T waves in lateral leads); P-mitrale (enlarged LA); poor R-wave progression; LBBB or complete AV block (calcification of the conduction system)
- **CXR:** LVH (increased cardiothoracic ratio); calcified aortic valve visible on lateral film; post-stenotic dilation of the ascending aorta
- **Echocardiography** is diagnostic. Doppler quantifies severity:
  - **Severe AS:** peak gradient >40 mmHg AND valve area <1.0 cm² (normal 3–4 cm²) AND peak jet velocity >4 m/s
  - **"Low flow, low gradient" severe AS** — if the LV is severely dysfunctional, it cannot generate sufficient pressure gradient even across a severely stenosed valve. The gradient appears modest despite critical stenosis. Dobutamine stress echo can unmask true severity by increasing contractility.
  - Aortic jet velocity increasing by **>0.3 m/s per year** indicates rapid progression — higher risk of complications and earlier intervention
- **CT aortogram** — mandatory for bicuspid aortic valve to assess coarctation and root dilation

### Management

No medical therapy slows disease progression. Once symptomatic, valve replacement is urgent — prognosis without surgery drops sharply.

- **SAVR** (surgical aortic valve replacement) — standard for younger, low-surgical-risk patients. Bioprosthetic valves (no lifelong anticoagulation) vs mechanical valves (lifelong warfarin, target INR 2.5–3.5)
- **TAVI/TAVR** (transcatheter aortic valve implantation) — delivery via femoral artery (transcatheter); now first-line in high surgical risk, age >75, frailty, prior cardiac surgery, or significant comorbidities; increasingly used in intermediate-risk patients with favourable anatomy

**TAVI complications:**
- **Peri-procedural:** stroke (2–5%), paravalvular regurgitation, vascular access complications (haematoma, dissection), AKI, aortic annular rupture, coronary occlusion
- **Long-term:** pacemaker requirement (10–15% due to LBBB from conducting system trauma), valve thrombosis (<1%), prosthetic valve endocarditis, prosthesis-patient mismatch

## Aortic Regurgitation

### Pathophysiology

Incompetent aortic valve closure allows blood to regurgitate from the aorta into the LV during diastole → **volume overload** → LV dilates and undergoes **eccentric hypertrophy** (wall thickness increases proportionally to chamber size). The large end-diastolic volume allows a large stroke volume to be ejected, maintaining forward output → **high systolic BP**. Simultaneously, regurgitant flow reduces diastolic aortic pressure → **wide pulse pressure** and the characteristic peripheral signs.

**Acute AR** (endocarditis, aortic dissection) is a surgical emergency — the LV has no time to dilate and accommodate the regurgitant volume, causing a rapid rise in LVEDP and acute pulmonary oedema with cardiogenic shock.

**Causes:**
- **Valve leaflet pathology:** bicuspid valve, infective endocarditis, rheumatic fever
- **Aortic root dilation:** Marfan syndrome, ankylosing spondylitis (seronegative arthritis — also Reiter's and psoriatic arthropathy), Takayasu arteritis, syphilitic aortitis, osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos, connective tissue disorders, hypertension, pseudoxanthoma elasticum
- **Other:** SLE, appetite suppressants (fenfluramine, phentermine)

### Examination

- **Early diastolic murmur** — blowing, decrescendo, heard best at the **left sternal border** with the patient **sitting forward in expiration** (brings the aortic root closer to the chest wall)
- **Wide pulse pressure** (>60 mmHg) — high systolic, low diastolic BP
- **Collapsing (waterhammer) pulse** — felt by raising the patient's arm and wrapping your hand around their forearm; rapid rise and fall
- **Austin Flint murmur** — low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at the apex, caused by the aortic regurgitant jet partially closing the mitral valve and creating turbulence; denotes severe AR
- **Displaced, hyperdynamic apex** — eccentric LV dilation

**Peripheral eponyms of AR** (all caused by the wide pulse pressure and large stroke volume):
- **Corrigan's sign** — visible carotid pulsation
- **de Musset's sign** — head nodding with each heartbeat
- **Quincke's sign** — capillary pulsations visible in the nail beds (compress gently)
- **Duroziez's sign** — compress the femoral artery 2cm proximal to the stethoscope → systolic murmur; 2cm distal → diastolic murmur (blood flowing backwards)
- **Traube's sign** — "pistol-shot" sound over the femoral arteries

### Management

- **Medical:** Vasodilators (ACE inhibitors, nifedipine) to reduce systolic hypertension and afterload. Echo every 6–12 months to monitor LV dimensions and function.
- **Surgical:** Replace before LV function deteriorates irreversibly.
  - Indications: Severe AR with **increasing symptoms**, **enlarging LV** (end-systolic diameter >50mm), **deteriorating LV function** (EF falling), or **enlarged ascending aorta** (>55mm, or >45–50mm in Marfan)
  - **Predictors of poor post-operative outcome:** EF <50%, NYHA class III or IV, duration of CCF >12 months — operate before reaching these thresholds

## Mitral Regurgitation

### Pathophysiology

Regurgitant flow back into the LA during systole → LA and LV volume overload → progressive LA dilation (predisposing to AF) and LV dilation. The LV can initially maintain forward output because it offloads easily into the low-pressure LA — this makes the EF appear deceptively preserved until myocardial function is significantly impaired.

**An EF of 60% in significant MR may represent the equivalent of an EF of 40–45% in a normal heart** — because ejecting into the LA is much easier than ejecting into the aorta.

**Causes:**
- **Acute:** Papillary muscle rupture (post-MI, 3–5 days; surgical emergency); ruptured chordae tendineae (infective endocarditis, mitral valve prolapse); trauma
- **Chronic:** Mitral valve prolapse (most common cause in the developed world); functional MR (LV dilation in DCM or ischaemic cardiomyopathy causing papillary muscle displacement); annular calcification (elderly); rheumatic; connective tissue disorders (Marfan's, Ehlers-Danlos); HCM; congenital; appetite suppressants

### Examination

- **Pansystolic murmur** at the apex, **radiating to the axilla** — this radiation distinguishes it from most other systolic murmurs; louder on expiration and in the left lateral decubitus position
- **Soft S1** — the mitral valve is not closing properly (less force at mitral closure)
- **Displaced, hyperdynamic apex** — from LV dilation and volume overload
- **Third heart sound (S3)** — ventricular volume overload
- **Loud P2** — if significant pulmonary hypertension has developed
- **Severity correlates with LV size** — the more severe the MR, the larger the left ventricle

### Management

**Acute severe MR** (papillary muscle rupture) — surgical emergency. Initial stabilisation with vasodilators (reduce afterload and regurgitant fraction), IABP, urgent surgical repair.

**Chronic MR:**
- Rate control if AF; anticoagulate (AF + mitral disease)
- Diuretics for congestion
- Surgery when: **symptomatic with severe MR**, or **asymptomatic but EF falling** (EF <60% in MR is already functionally impaired — act before EF drops to the usual threshold of 50%)
- **Aim to repair rather than replace the valve** where possible — lower operative mortality, better LV function preservation, no need for anticoagulation with bioprosthetic repair
- **MitraClip** (transcatheter mitral valve repair) — for patients with significant MR and prohibitive surgical risk and favourable anatomy

## Mitral Stenosis

### Pathophysiology

Almost exclusively caused by **rheumatic heart disease** — Group A Streptococcal pharyngitis triggers a cross-reactive immune response that damages the mitral valve leaflets and causes progressive leaflet thickening, fusion of the commissures, and subvalvular apparatus involvement. LA pressure rises to overcome obstruction → **pulmonary venous hypertension** → pulmonary arterial hypertension → right heart failure. The enlarged LA predisposes to AF and thrombus formation.

**Normal mitral valve area is 4–6 cm²**. Symptoms typically begin when the orifice falls below **2 cm²**. Severe stenosis: <1.0 cm²/m² body surface area.

**Causes:** Rheumatic fever (>99% in developing world); congenital; mucopolysaccharidoses; carcinoid (right-sided predominantly)

### Examination

- **Tapping, non-displaced apex beat** — this is a palpable S1 (forceful valve closure)
- **RV heave** — from pulmonary hypertension
- **Loud S1** — if the valve leaflets are still pliable, they snap shut with a bang
- **Opening snap** — high-pitched early diastolic sound immediately following S2; produced by the abrupt opening of the thickened but still pliable mitral valve under elevated LA pressure. **Key principle: the closer the opening snap to S2, the more severe the stenosis** — because higher LA pressure forces the valve open sooner after it has closed
- **Mid-diastolic rumble** — low-pitched, best heard at the apex with the bell of the stethoscope, patient in the left lateral decubitus position, in expiration. **The longer the diastolic murmur, the more severe the stenosis** — because it takes longer for the LA-LV gradient to equalise across a more obstructed valve. "Pre-systolic accentuation" occurs if the patient is in sinus rhythm — the atrial kick increases flow across the valve just before systole, accentuating the murmur.
- **Malar flush** — plum-coloured cheeks from low cardiac output and peripheral vasodilation
- **AF** is common (enlarged LA)
- **Graham Steell murmur** — pulmonary regurgitation secondary to pulmonary hypertension; early diastolic murmur at left sternal edge

### Management

- **Rate control in AF** — digoxin, beta-blocker, or rate-limiting CCB; digoxin is particularly useful for rate control at rest in sedentary patients
- **Anticoagulation with warfarin** — mandatory if AF or history of embolism. **DOACs are NOT validated in rheumatic mitral stenosis** and are contraindicated
- Diuretics to reduce pulmonary venous congestion
- **Definitive intervention when symptomatic with severe stenosis:**
  - **Percutaneous mitral balloon valvuloplasty** (PMBV) — preferred if valve is pliable and non-calcified, no significant MR, no LA thrombus on TOE. A balloon catheter is advanced across the interatrial septum and inflated within the mitral orifice. Excellent results in appropriate anatomy.
  - **Open mitral valvotomy** — surgical commissurotomy
  - **Valve replacement** — for calcified valves unsuitable for valvuloplasty, or if significant MR coexists

## Mitral Valve Prolapse

Myxomatous degeneration causes one or both mitral leaflets to billow back into the LA during systole. Prevalence ~5% — the most common valvular abnormality. Occurs alone or with connective tissue disorders (Marfan's, Ehlers-Danlos, Turner syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta) and cardiac conditions (ASD, WPW).

**Examination:** Mid-systolic click followed by a late systolic murmur. **Dynamic behaviour — important examination distinction:**
- **Standing or Valsalva** (decreased preload → smaller LV → leaflets prolapse sooner) → click and murmur move **earlier** in systole
- **Squatting or leg raise** (increased preload → larger LV → prolapse delayed) → click and murmur move **later** in systole

Most cases are benign. Complications include: MR (if leaflets fail completely), cerebral emboli, arrhythmias (especially if associated with T-wave inversion in inferior leads), and rarely sudden death.

**Management:** Beta-blockers for palpitations and chest pain; surgery only if severe MR develops.

## Complications

- **Aortic stenosis:** Sudden cardiac death (even in "asymptomatic" severe AS); LV failure; AF; infective endocarditis; post-TAVI — pacemaker (~10–15%), paravalvular AR, stroke
- **Aortic regurgitation:** LV failure; infective endocarditis; aortic root dilation progressing to dissection (especially Marfan syndrome)
- **Mitral regurgitation:** AF (from LA dilation); pulmonary hypertension; LV failure; infective endocarditis
- **Mitral stenosis:** AF (40–50% of patients); systemic embolism and stroke from LA thrombus; pulmonary hypertension; right heart failure; infective endocarditis (less common than in MR)
- **Prosthetic valves:** Structural deterioration (bioprosthetic — typically after 10–20 years); thrombosis (mechanical — lifelong warfarin, INR 2.5–3.5); prosthetic valve endocarditis (carries high mortality); haemolysis (paravalvular leak)

## Clinical Insight

Aortic stenosis is notoriously underappreciated until symptoms develop. A patient with a longstanding systolic murmur who reports a first episode of exertional syncope has crossed a critical prognostic threshold — mean survival without intervention is now ~3 years. Do not wait for a second episode. Refer urgently for echo and surgical evaluation.

In mitral regurgitation, the EF is misleading. Because the LV offloads easily into the low-pressure LA, it appears to function well even when myocardial contractility is impaired. An EF of 55% in severe MR may represent significantly reduced contractile reserve — the threshold for surgical intervention is EF <60%, not the usual <50%.

The opening snap and diastolic murmur in mitral stenosis behave inversely to what might be intuitive. A closer opening snap means more severe disease (higher LA pressure opens the valve sooner); a longer murmur means more severe disease (takes longer for pressures to equalise across a tight orifice). These relationships are tested frequently in examinations and matter clinically for serial monitoring without echo.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 75-year-old woman with 18 months of progressive exertional breathlessness and one episode of exertional near-syncope. Harsh ejection systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border radiating to the carotids. Slow-rising pulse. Soft second heart sound.

**Step 1 — Diagnose severe aortic stenosis:** The clinical findings form the classic triad:
- **Ejection systolic murmur (crescendo-decrescendo) at the right upper sternal border → carotids** = aortic stenosis (not MR, which radiates to the axilla)
- **Slow-rising, low-volume pulse** (pulsus parvus et tardus) = reduced stroke volume across stenotic valve
- **Soft S2** = calcified valve leaflets no longer close with a snap

**Step 2 — Recognise where she is on the prognostic timeline:** The **SAD triad** of severe AS:
- **S**yncope (or near-syncope) → mean survival ~3 years without intervention
- **A**ngina → mean survival ~5 years
- **D**yspnoea/heart failure → mean survival ~2 years
She has dyspnoea + near-syncope. **Mean survival without intervention is now 2–3 years**. Referral must happen today — not at a follow-up in 3 months.

**Step 3 — Urgent echocardiography:** Confirms severe AS (peak transvalvular gradient >40 mmHg OR mean gradient >20–25 mmHg, aortic valve area <1.0 cm²). Also assesses LV function (EF may be preserved initially, falls late).

**Step 4 — Heart team decision — TAVI vs SAVR:**
- Age 75, female, exertional symptoms = **TAVI preferred** in most centres (lower procedural risk, equivalent long-term outcomes in intermediate/high-risk surgical candidates — PARTNER trials)
- SAVR if anatomy unsuitable for TAVI or younger patient with long life expectancy

**Step 5 — What NOT to do while awaiting intervention:**
- **No vasodilators** (ACEi, nitrates, amlodipine) — fixed outflow obstruction means vasodilation causes life-threatening hypotension
- **Avoid vigorous diuresis** — preload-dependent ventricle
- **No strenuous exercise** — exertional syncope risk

**Exam trigger:** *Progressive exertional breathlessness + syncope + ejection systolic murmur at RUSB + slow-rising pulse + soft S2* → severe AS. The SAD triad (Syncope, Angina, Dyspnoea) marks the urgency. Once symptoms develop, immediate cardiac surgery/TAVI referral is required — watchful waiting has a 2–3-year survival.
