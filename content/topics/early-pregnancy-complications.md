## Overview

Early pregnancy complications span a spectrum from threatened miscarriage to ruptured ectopic pregnancy, a surgical emergency with a mortality risk if missed. The golden rule in any woman of reproductive age with abdominal pain: **do a pregnancy test**. The most dangerous diagnosis is the ectopic pregnancy — and it is most dangerous because it is most often delayed.

Hyperemesis gravidarum and gestational trophoblastic disease round out this topic, both with unique pathophysiology and management priorities that regularly appear in examinations.

## Pathophysiology

**Ectopic pregnancy**: After fertilisation, the zygote normally travels along the fallopian tube to implant in the uterine cavity. If implantation occurs outside the cavity — most commonly in the **ampulla of the fallopian tube (70%)**, but also the isthmus (12%), fimbrial end (11%), ovary, cervix, cornua, or caesarean scar — the trophoblast invades local tissue with no myometrial support. The fallopian tube has no capacity to accommodate a growing pregnancy. As the ectopic grows, it erodes into blood vessels → tubal rupture → haemoperitoneum. Free blood in the peritoneum irritates the diaphragm → **referred shoulder tip pain** — a critically important symptom of rupture.

**Miscarriage**: The most common cause is chromosomal abnormality (50–70% of first trimester losses), particularly trisomies. Other causes include uterine structural anomalies, antiphospholipid syndrome, cervical incompetence (recurrent second trimester loss), and thrombophilias. The majority are random chromosomal errors — not preventable and not due to maternal behaviour.

**Molar pregnancy (GTD)**: Abnormal fertilisation produces excessive trophoblastic proliferation without a viable fetus. The trophoblast produces enormous quantities of hCG, which stimulates ovarian theca lutein cysts bilaterally. The hyperproliferating trophoblast can become malignant (choriocarcinoma).

## Clinical Presentation

**Ectopic pregnancy — the classic triad**:
1. Lower abdominal pain (unilateral, colicky or constant)
2. Amenorrhoea (missed period — often only 4–8 weeks)
3. Vaginal bleeding (light — not the heavy bleeding of miscarriage)

The key is that **any or all of these may be absent or atypical**. Women may not know they are pregnant. The bleeding may be mistaken for a light period. Pain may be dismissed as musculoskeletal.

**Signs of rupture** (surgical emergency):
- Haemodynamic instability: hypotension, tachycardia
- Peritonism: guarding, rebound tenderness
- Cervical excitation (cervical motion tenderness) — moving the cervix causes referred adnexal pain
- Shoulder tip pain — blood under the diaphragm
- Adnexal mass (tender)

**Miscarriage types:**

| Type | Cervical Os | Viability on USS | Bleeding |
|------|------------|-----------------|---------|
| Threatened | Closed | Viable fetus visible | Present |
| Inevitable | Open | Pregnancy in cervix/vagina | Heavy |
| Incomplete | Open | Some products retained | Variable |
| Complete | Closed | Empty uterus | Settling |
| Missed/Silent | Closed | Non-viable (empty sac/fetal pole no heartbeat) | None/minimal |
| Septic | Open or closed | Infected products — uterine tenderness, fever, purulent discharge | Variable |

**Hyperemesis gravidarum**: Distinguishable from normal pregnancy nausea by severity — persistent vomiting causing weight loss >5%, ketonuria, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance. Onset 4–9 weeks, peaks 10–13 weeks. Associated with elevated hCG (higher in multiple pregnancy, molar pregnancy, and Down syndrome — all have higher hCG levels).

**GTD presentation**: Uterine size larger than expected for dates, hyperemesis (from very high hCG), vaginal bleeding with grape-like vesicles (pathognomonic), bilateral ovarian enlargement (theca lutein cysts), features of hyperthyroidism (hCG has structural homology with TSH → thyroid stimulation in very high hCG states). The **snowstorm appearance** on USS is the classic imaging finding.

## Diagnosis

**Transvaginal ultrasound (TVS) is the gold standard** for early pregnancy assessment. It can detect an intrauterine sac from around 5 weeks (when hCG is ~1000–2000 mIU/mL).

**hCG discriminatory zone**: When serum hCG ≥**1500–2000 mIU/mL**, a gestational sac should be visible on TVS in a normally-sited intrauterine pregnancy. An empty uterus at this hCG level should be considered **ectopic or recent complete miscarriage** until proven otherwise.

**Serial hCG** (every 48 hours) is invaluable:
- Normal viable intrauterine pregnancy: hCG **doubles every 48 hours** (increases ≥66% in 48h)
- Complete miscarriage: falling hCG
- Ectopic or failing pregnancy: hCG rises but **sub-optimally** (plateau or slower-than-expected rise — does not double)
- Gestational trophoblastic disease: **markedly elevated** hCG, often >100,000 mIU/mL

**Investigations for suspected ectopic**:
- Urine hCG (qualitative) → if positive: serum hCG quantitative
- TVS: look for intrauterine pregnancy, adnexal masses, free fluid in pouch of Douglas
- FBC, group and save (crossmatch if haemodynamically unstable)
- Progesterone level: <5 ng/mL suggests non-viable pregnancy; >20 ng/mL suggests viable IUP (not used in isolation but helpful adjunct)

## Management

**Ectopic pregnancy** — management depends on haemodynamic stability, hCG level, and the woman's future fertility wishes:

*Surgical management*:
- **Salpingectomy** (removal of tube) is preferred for: haemodynamically unstable, hCG >5000 mIU/mL, heterotopic pregnancy, failed medical management, or woman's preference
- **Salpingotomy** (incision and removal of ectopic, preserving tube): considered if the contralateral tube is damaged or absent (to preserve fertility); however, higher rate of **persistent trophoblast** (20% vs. 5%) — requires post-operative hCG monitoring until undetectable
- Route: laparoscopic (preferred) unless haemodynamically unstable → laparotomy

*Medical management* — **methotrexate** (MTX):
- Mechanism: folic acid antagonist → inhibits dihydrofolate reductase → impairs rapidly dividing trophoblast cells
- Eligibility criteria: haemodynamically **stable**, hCG **<5000 mIU/mL**, sac **<3.5 cm**, **no fetal cardiac activity**, no contraindications
- Contraindications: renal impairment, hepatic disease, breastfeeding, immunodeficiency, blood dyscrasias, pulmonary disease
- Regimen: single IM dose (50 mg/m² body surface area); monitor hCG on days 4 and 7 — expect ≥15% fall by day 7; if inadequate fall, second dose or surgery
- Advise: avoid pregnancy for **3 months** after MTX (teratogenic), avoid NSAIDs and folate supplements during treatment, no alcohol (hepatotoxic)

*Expectant management*: appropriate only for declining hCG, very low level (<1000 mIU/mL), clinically stable woman with TVS findings consistent with resolving ectopic; requires very close follow-up

**Anti-D immunoglobulin**: Give to all Rh-negative women with ectopic pregnancy, surgical/medical management of miscarriage, or haemodynamically significant bleeding.

**Miscarriage management**:

| Approach | Method | Details |
|----------|--------|---------|
| Expectant | Wait | 80% success within 2 weeks for incomplete miscarriage; requires follow-up USS |
| Medical | Misoprostol 800 mcg vaginally | Prostaglandin E1 analogue — stimulates uterine contractions; side effects: cramping, bleeding, GI upset |
| Surgical | ERPC/SMM (suction curettage) | Evacuation under GA; risk of Asherman syndrome (intrauterine adhesions) especially with repeated procedures |

For **missed miscarriage** specifically: medical or surgical are the main options (expectant takes longer and is psychologically more difficult for many women).

**Gestational trophoblastic disease**:
1. **Suction curettage** (not sharp curettage — risk of uterine perforation through thin, vascular molar tissue)
2. **Register** with regional GTD centre (Charing Cross, Sheffield, or Dundee in the UK)
3. **Serial hCG monitoring** until normalisation — at registration centre
4. **Avoid pregnancy** until hCG normalises (for complete mole: usually within 3 months; avoid OCP until hCG normal as oestrogen may stimulate residual trophoblast)
5. **Chemotherapy for persistent GTD**: methotrexate (for low-risk) or actinomycin D; EMA/CO regimen for high-risk/metastatic disease
6. Risk of malignant transformation: complete mole ~2% → choriocarcinoma; invasive mole ~15%; partial mole <0.5%

**Hyperemesis gravidarum management**:
1. IV fluid resuscitation: **0.9% NaCl** with **potassium supplementation** (hypokalaemia is common from vomiting)
2. **IV thiamine (Pabrinex) BEFORE any glucose** — the most critical point: glucose administration in a thiamine-depleted patient precipitates **Wernicke's encephalopathy** (ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, confusion). Always give Pabrinex first.
3. Antiemetics: cyclizine, promethazine, ondansetron (use cautiously in first trimester — some concern about cleft palate, but widely used), metoclopramide, prochlorperazine
4. If refractory: consider corticosteroids (prednisolone), nasogastric or nasojejunal feeding, rarely TPN
5. Thromboprophylaxis: vomiting + dehydration + admission = high VTE risk; LMWH + compression stockings

## Complications

**Ectopic pregnancy**: Rupture with catastrophic haemorrhage (can lose 1–2 litres rapidly into peritoneum), maternal death, future ectopic risk (one ectopic → 10% recurrence risk), reduced fertility (especially after salpingectomy if contralateral tube is also diseased).

**Miscarriage**: Haemorrhage, sepsis (especially retained products of conception), Asherman syndrome after surgical evacuation (intrauterine adhesions → secondary amenorrhoea + infertility), psychological impact (grief, anxiety, depression) — often underestimated clinically.

**GTD**: Malignant transformation (choriocarcinoma) — highly chemosensitive even when metastatic; excellent cure rates with chemotherapy. Early registration with GTD centre is life-saving.

**Hyperemesis gravidarum**: Wernicke's encephalopathy (if thiamine not given before glucose), Mallory-Weiss tears, maternal weight loss and muscle wasting, fetal growth restriction in severe prolonged cases, VTE, electrolyte disturbances (hypokalaemia, hyponatraemia — can cause central pontine myelinolysis if corrected too rapidly).

## Clinical Insight

The woman in this scenario has a **ruptured or leaking ectopic pregnancy**. The combination of:
- Positive urine hCG
- Empty uterus on TVS (LMP 6 weeks ago — an IUP would be visible by now)
- Right adnexal mass (2.5 cm)
- Free fluid in the pouch of Douglas (blood in the peritoneum)
- Haemodynamic compromise (BP 102/68, HR 112)

...is a **surgical emergency**. She needs IV access, bloods, group and crossmatch, and immediate senior obstetric/gynaecology review for **emergency laparoscopy** (or laparotomy if she deteriorates further). This is not a candidate for methotrexate — she is haemodynamically unstable.

The free fluid in the pouch of Douglas is haemoperitoneum. The HR 112 reflects early haemorrhagic shock. Resuscitate while preparing theatre — do not delay surgery for adequate resuscitation if she continues to deteriorate.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**Working through the case**: A 24-year-old woman, LMP 6 weeks ago (= 6 weeks pregnant by dates), presents with right-sided abdominal pain, vaginal bleeding, and a positive pregnancy test. TVS shows empty uterus + 2.5 cm right adnexal mass + free fluid. BP 102/68, HR 112.

This is a **ruptured ectopic pregnancy with haemoperitoneum causing haemodynamic instability**. Diagnosis is effectively confirmed by: positive hCG + empty uterus (by 6 weeks a gestational sac should be visible on TVS) + adnexal mass + free fluid + haemodynamic instability.

Management:
- Large-bore IV access (two lines), FBC, U&E, G&S, crossmatch 2 units
- IV crystalloid resuscitation (keep MAP >65 mmHg while awaiting surgery — avoid excessive fluid in haemorrhage)
- Call senior obstetrician and theatre team
- Emergency laparoscopy (salpingectomy preferred — she is haemodynamically unstable; no role for medical management)
- Anti-D immunoglobulin (check blood group — Rh-negative women need it)
- Post-op: serial hCG until undetectable, counselling about future fertility, recurrence risk (~10%)

**Exam trigger:** The classic presentation is right lower abdominal pain + missed period + positive pregnancy test + empty uterus on USS + haemodynamic instability — the answer is ruptured ectopic pregnancy requiring emergency surgery. The most common distractor is considering methotrexate when the patient is haemodynamically unstable; methotrexate is only for stable, unruptured ectopics with hCG <5000 mIU/mL — never when there is haemodynamic compromise or signs of rupture.
