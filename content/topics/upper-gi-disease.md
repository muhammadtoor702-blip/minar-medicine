# Upper GI Disease

## Clinical Scenario

A 54-year-old man presents with a three-month history of epigastric pain occurring 2–3 hours after meals and waking him at night, relieved by eating and antacids. He takes ibuprofen regularly for chronic back pain, smokes 15 cigarettes daily, and drinks 20 units of alcohol weekly. He has lost 4kg unintentionally. Examination reveals epigastric tenderness. Urea breath test is positive for H. pylori. OGD shows a 1.5cm duodenal ulcer with no active bleeding.

## Overview

Upper gastrointestinal disease encompasses conditions of the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum — from the common (peptic ulcer disease, GORD) to the surgical emergency (acute upper GI bleed). These conditions share overlapping symptoms and risk factors, making systematic clinical assessment essential. The critical skill is recognising when upper GI symptoms require urgent investigation versus empirical management.

## Peptic Ulcer Disease

### Pathophysiology

Peptic ulcers result from an imbalance between aggressive factors (gastric acid, pepsin, *H. pylori*, NSAIDs) and defensive factors (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, mucosal blood flow).

***H. pylori*** is a Gram-negative, urease-producing bacterium responsible for ~90% of duodenal ulcers and ~70% of gastric ulcers. It causes injury through direct cytotoxic effects (VacA toxin), inflammatory mucosal damage, increased gastrin-driven acid secretion, and reduced duodenal bicarbonate production.

**NSAIDs** inhibit COX enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. COX-1 is constitutively expressed and maintains mucosal integrity via mucus, bicarbonate, and blood flow. Even topical NSAIDs cause systemic mucosal damage through prostaglandin inhibition.

**Duodenal ulcers**: First part of the duodenum. Pain classically 2–3 hours post-meal and at night, relieved by eating (food buffers acid). Associated with acid hypersecretion and H. pylori.

**Gastric ulcers**: Antrum most commonly. Pain may be worsened by eating. Malignancy must always be excluded — biopsy is mandatory for every gastric ulcer, and repeat endoscopy to confirm healing is required.

### Alarm Features Requiring Urgent Endoscopy

- Unintentional weight loss
- Dysphagia or odynophagia
- Persistent vomiting
- Haematemesis or melaena
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Epigastric mass
- Age >55 with new onset unexplained dyspepsia

### Diagnosis

**OGD (upper GI endoscopy)**: Gold standard — direct visualisation, biopsy (histology, CLO test for H. pylori), and therapeutic capability.

**H. pylori testing:**

| Test | Setting | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| Urea breath test (UBT) | Community / primary care | Most accurate non-invasive test; stop PPI 2 weeks before |
| Stool antigen test | Community | Equivalent accuracy to UBT |
| CLO test (biopsy urease) | At endoscopy | Rapid, highly specific |
| Serology | Rarely used | Cannot distinguish active from past infection |

Stop PPIs 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before non-invasive testing to avoid false negatives.

### Management

**H. pylori eradication (triple therapy):** PPI + clarithromycin + amoxicillin for 7 days. Eradication rates ~80–85%. Confirm eradication with UBT or stool antigen 4 weeks after completing therapy (stop PPI 2 weeks before testing).

If failed or resistant: Quadruple therapy (PPI + bismuth + tetracycline + metronidazole) for 10–14 days.

**NSAID-induced ulcers**: Stop NSAID if possible. If NSAID cannot be stopped: switch to selective COX-2 inhibitor AND add PPI. H. pylori eradication does not fully protect against NSAID ulcers — the mechanisms are independent and additive.

## Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)

### Pathophysiology

GORD results from excessive reflux of gastric contents into the oesophagus via transient lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) relaxations. Hiatus hernia, obesity, and large meals are key risk factors. Los Angeles (LA) grading classifies mucosal injury at endoscopy (A–D).

**Barrett's oesophagus**: Metaplastic replacement of squamous oesophageal epithelium by intestinal columnar epithelium — a consequence of chronic acid exposure. Premalignant: ~0.3% per year risk of oesophageal adenocarcinoma. Endoscopic surveillance every 2–5 years.

### Management

Lifestyle: weight loss, elevate head of bed, avoid late meals, reduce alcohol and caffeine.

**PPIs** — most effective pharmacological treatment. Use for 4–8 weeks for oesophagitis; step down to lowest effective dose for maintenance. Antireflux surgery (Nissen fundoplication) for young patients with refractory disease.

**Barrett's with high-grade dysplasia or early adenocarcinoma**: Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or radiofrequency ablation (RFA).

## Acute Upper GI Bleeding

### Causes

| Cause | Frequency | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| Peptic ulcer (gastric or duodenal) | ~50% | Most common overall |
| Oesophageal/gastric varices | ~15% | High mortality — 30% per episode |
| Mallory-Weiss tear | ~10% | Mucosal tear at GOJ after vomiting |
| Erosive oesophagitis/gastritis | ~10% | |
| Malignancy | ~5% | |
| Dieulafoy lesion | Rare | Large submucosal artery — catastrophic |

### Clinical Presentation

**Haematemesis** — fresh red blood (rapid bleeding) or "coffee grounds" (slower, oxidised blood).

**Melaena** — black, tarry, foul-smelling stool; digested blood; requires ~200 mL in the upper GI tract.

**Haematochezia** (fresh rectal bleeding) can occur from a very rapid UGIB — these patients are usually haemodynamically unstable.

### Risk Stratification — Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS)

Used before endoscopy to predict need for intervention. Score 0 = low risk (outpatient management safe). Score ≥1 = inpatient management and early endoscopy required.

Components: urea, Hb, systolic BP, pulse, presence of melaena, syncope, hepatic disease, cardiac failure.

### Management

**Resuscitation first:**
- IV access × 2 large-bore cannulae, crossmatch 4–6 units
- IV crystalloid for haemodynamic instability
- **Transfuse to Hb ≥70 g/L** (restrictive strategy — reduces rebleeding by not over-expanding intravascular volume)
- Hb target ≥80 g/L if known ischaemic heart disease
- Airway protection if obtunded with active haematemesis

**Endoscopy (OGD):**
- Within 24 hours for all admitted patients (once resuscitated)
- Within 12 hours for high-risk features (haemodynamic instability, suspected variceal bleeding, cirrhosis)
- Haemostasis: adrenaline injection + thermal coagulation / haemoclips for ulcers; banding or sclerotherapy for varices

**Non-variceal UGIB:** High-dose PPI (omeprazole 80 mg IV bolus then 8 mg/hour for 72 hours) after successful haemostasis reduces rebleeding. H. pylori eradication before discharge.

**Variceal UGIB:**
- **Terlipressin IV immediately** (vasopressin analogue — reduces portal pressure). Start before endoscopy.
- **Endoscopic band ligation** — first-line for oesophageal varices
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** (IV ceftriaxone) for 5 days — reduces SBP and 5-day mortality
- **TIPSS** (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) — for refractory bleeding; reduces portal pressure
- Sengstaken-Blakemore tube — tamponade as bridge to definitive therapy

## Oesophageal Cancer

Two histological types:

- **Squamous cell carcinoma**: Mid-oesophagus. Risk factors: smoking, alcohol, achalasia. More common in Asia and Africa.
- **Adenocarcinoma**: Lower oesophagus/GOJ. Risk factors: GORD, Barrett's, obesity. Rising incidence in Western countries — now the more common type in the UK.

**Presentation**: Progressive dysphagia (solids first, then liquids), odynophagia, weight loss. Typically late presentation.

**Investigation**: OGD + biopsy (diagnosis), CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (staging), EUS (local T/N staging), PET-CT (distant metastases).

**Management**: Curative intent — Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy ± neoadjuvant chemotherapy (FLOT or CROSS protocol). Most present with advanced disease — palliative chemotherapy and endoscopic stenting for dysphagia.

## Complications

**Peptic ulcer**: Haemorrhage (most common — endoscopic haemostasis), perforation (peritonitis — surgical emergency), gastric outlet obstruction (fibrotic pyloric scarring — bilious vomiting).

**GORD**: Barrett's oesophagus, oesophageal stricture, adenocarcinoma.

**Acute UGIB**: Death (~5–10% overall; higher for variceal), rebleeding, aspiration pneumonia.

## Clinical Insight

All gastric ulcers must be biopsied and followed up to confirm healing. A small malignant gastric ulcer can appear to heal on repeat endoscopy — healing does not exclude cancer. Repeat OGD at 6–8 weeks is mandatory for all gastric ulcers unless initial biopsies are comprehensively negative and the patient is low-risk.

The urea breath test gives false negatives if the patient is on a PPI. PPIs suppress H. pylori activity without eradicating it — the organism becomes temporarily undetectable. Stop PPIs 2 weeks before testing. Performing the test the day after the patient's last omeprazole gives a false negative and a false sense of security.

Restrictive transfusion strategy (Hb threshold 70 g/L) in acute UGIB improves survival compared with liberal transfusion. Over-transfusion raises portal pressure and worsens variceal bleeding. In a haemodynamically stable patient with Hb of 72 g/dL, the correct management is to hold the transfusion, not prescribe it.
