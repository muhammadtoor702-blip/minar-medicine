## Overview

Upper gastrointestinal disease encompasses conditions of the oesophagus, stomach, and duodenum — from the common (peptic ulcer disease, GORD) to the surgical emergency (acute upper GI bleed). These conditions share overlapping symptoms and risk factors, making systematic clinical assessment essential. The critical skill is recognising when upper GI symptoms require urgent investigation versus empirical management, and understanding that ALARM features mandate endoscopy regardless of how convincingly benign the history sounds.

---

## Peptic Ulcer Disease

### Pathophysiology

Peptic ulcers result from an imbalance between aggressive factors (gastric acid, pepsin, *H. pylori*, NSAIDs) and defensive factors (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, mucosal blood flow).

***H. pylori*** is a Gram-negative, urease-producing, microaerophilic spiral bacterium responsible for ~90% of duodenal ulcers and ~70% of gastric ulcers worldwide. It colonises the gastric antrum and causes injury through multiple mechanisms: the CagA virulence protein is directly injected into epithelial cells, disrupting tight junctions; VacA toxin induces vacuolation and apoptosis; urease-produced ammonia is directly cytotoxic; and H. pylori-induced hypergastrinaemia drives excess acid secretion. Global seroprevalence exceeds 50% — the majority never develop ulcers, but the combination with NSAIDs or other cofactors is synergistic and dangerous.

**NSAIDs** inhibit COX enzymes, reducing prostaglandin synthesis. COX-1 is constitutively expressed in the gastric mucosa and maintains mucosal integrity via mucus secretion, bicarbonate secretion, and mucosal blood flow. Even parenteral or topical NSAIDs cause mucosal damage through systemic prostaglandin inhibition — there is no "safe" route of NSAID administration for the gastric mucosa.

**Duodenal ulcers**: First part of the duodenum, classically in the anterior wall. Pain 2–3 hours post-meal and at night, relieved by eating (food buffers acid) and antacids. Associated with acid hypersecretion and H. pylori. Zollinger-Ellison syndrome (gastrinoma) causes multiple, atypical, or refractory duodenal ulcers — suspect if ulcers distal to the first part of the duodenum, multiple simultaneous ulcers, family history of MEN1, or ulcers refractory to PPI therapy.

**Gastric ulcers**: Antrum most commonly. Pain may be worsened by eating. Malignancy must always be excluded — **every gastric ulcer must be biopsied and followed up to confirm healing** at 6–8 weeks. A gastric cancer can appear to heal with PPI therapy, giving false reassurance.

### ALARM Features Requiring Urgent Endoscopy

The ALARM mnemonic identifies features that mandate urgent investigation regardless of age:

- **A** — Anaemia (iron deficiency)
- **L** — Loss of weight (unintentional)
- **A** — Anorexia
- **R** — Recent onset or progressive dyspepsia (new in age >55)
- **M** — Melaena or haematemesis
- **S** — Swallowing difficulty (dysphagia or odynophagia)

Additional alarm features: epigastric mass, persistent vomiting, previous gastric surgery. In these patients, empirical PPI therapy without endoscopy is inappropriate.

### Diagnosis

**OGD (upper GI endoscopy)**: Gold standard — direct visualisation, biopsy (histology, CLO test for H. pylori), and therapeutic capability (haemostasis, dilation).

**H. pylori testing:**

| Test | Setting | Sensitivity/Specificity | Notes |
|------|---------|------------------------|-------|
| **¹³C Urea breath test (UBT)** | Community / primary care | ~95% / ~95% | Most accurate non-invasive test overall; stop PPI 2 weeks before |
| **Stool antigen test** | Community | ~94% / ~97% | Equivalent to UBT; useful when UBT unavailable |
| **CLO test (biopsy urease)** | At endoscopy | ~95% / ~95% | Rapid, performed at endoscopy; false-negative if PPI-suppressed |
| **Serology (IgG)** | Rarely used | High sensitivity, low specificity | Cannot distinguish active from past infection; not recommended for confirming eradication |

Stop PPIs 2 weeks and antibiotics 4 weeks before non-invasive testing to avoid false negatives. PPIs suppress H. pylori metabolic activity, reducing urease production and rendering the organism temporarily undetectable — a false-negative UBT in a patient on omeprazole is a genuine and common diagnostic error.

### Management

**H. pylori eradication — Triple therapy (first-line):**

Lansoprazole 30 mg BD + clarithromycin 500 mg BD + amoxicillin 1 g BD for 7 days. Eradication rates ~80–85%. If penicillin-allergic: replace amoxicillin with metronidazole 400 mg BD.

Confirm eradication with UBT or stool antigen 4 weeks after completing therapy (stop PPI 2 weeks before testing). This is not optional — failed eradication requires quadruple therapy.

**Quadruple therapy (second-line / clarithromycin resistance suspected):**

PPI + bismuth subcitrate + tetracycline + metronidazole for 10–14 days. Bismuth impairs H. pylori adhesion and has direct bactericidal effects on the cell wall — it is particularly valuable when clarithromycin resistance is suspected.

**NSAID-induced ulcers**: Stop NSAID if clinically possible. If NSAID cannot be stopped: switch to a selective COX-2 inhibitor (celecoxib) AND add a PPI. H. pylori eradication reduces ulcer risk from NSAIDs but does not fully protect — the two mechanisms are additive, not overlapping. A patient who is both H. pylori-positive and NSAID-dependent requires both eradication and PPI co-prescription.

## Gastro-Oesophageal Reflux Disease (GORD)

### Pathophysiology

GORD results from excessive reflux of gastric contents into the oesophagus, primarily via transient lower oesophageal sphincter (LOS) relaxations. Normally, the LOS maintains a resting pressure of 15–25 mmHg above gastric pressure; transient relaxations unrelated to swallowing are the key mechanism in most GORD — not a permanently weakened sphincter.

**Risk factors**: Obesity (increased intra-abdominal pressure), pregnancy, hiatus hernia, large meals, lying flat after eating, alcohol, caffeine, smoking, systemic sclerosis (LOS incompetence), pregnancy.

**Hiatus hernia**: Herniation of the stomach through the diaphragmatic hiatus. ~95% are sliding (GOJ above the diaphragm, prone to reflux); ~5% are rolling/para-oesophageal (fundus rolls through a defect beside a normally placed GOJ — risk of volvulus and strangulation).

**Los Angeles (LA) grading** of oesophagitis severity at endoscopy: A (mucosal breaks <5mm), B (≥5mm not extending between folds), C (extending between folds but <75% of circumference), D (≥75% of circumference). Grade C/D predicts Barrett's formation.

### Barrett's Oesophagus

Specialised intestinal metaplasia — replacement of stratified squamous oesophageal epithelium by intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells. This is the consequence of chronic acid (and bile) exposure causing mucosal reprogramming. The significance: it is premalignant, with an annual risk of progression to oesophageal adenocarcinoma of **0.1–0.4% per year** — low in absolute terms, but enough to justify surveillance given the dismal prognosis of oesophageal cancer at diagnosis.

**Surveillance intervals (BSG):**
- No dysplasia: endoscopy every 2–5 years
- Low-grade dysplasia: endoscopy every 6 months, or endoscopic ablation
- High-grade dysplasia / early adenocarcinoma: endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) or radiofrequency ablation (RFA) — curative endoscopic treatment rather than oesophagectomy for superficial lesions

### Management

**Lifestyle**: Weight loss is the most effective intervention. Elevate head of bed, avoid late meals, reduce alcohol and caffeine. Alginate preparations (Gaviscon) provide symptomatic relief through a physical raft mechanism.

**PPIs** — most effective pharmacological treatment. Use standard dose (omeprazole 20 mg or lansoprazole 30 mg) for 4–8 weeks for oesophagitis; step down to lowest effective maintenance dose or on-demand therapy. H₂-receptor antagonists (ranitidine equivalent — now famotidine, ranitidine withdrawn) are less effective for healing oesophagitis but can supplement PPIs for nocturnal symptoms.

**Antireflux surgery** (laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication — 360° wrap of fundus around the LOS) for young patients with documented GORD refractory to PPI or with significant hiatus hernia causing mechanical symptoms. Requires pH monitoring and manometry before surgery to confirm diagnosis and exclude dysmotility.

## Dysphagia and Oesophageal Dysmotility

**Dysphagia** (difficulty swallowing) must always be investigated urgently.

| Location | Type | Causes |
|---------|------|--------|
| **Oropharyngeal** | Structural | Pharyngeal carcinoma, post-cricoid web (Plummer-Vinson syndrome — iron deficiency in women), Zenker's diverticulum, goitre |
| **Oropharyngeal** | Neuromuscular | Stroke, MND, bulbar palsy, myasthenia, Parkinson's |
| **Oesophageal** | Structural | Carcinoma, peptic stricture (GORD), Schatzki ring, extrinsic compression (nodes, aorta) |
| **Oesophageal** | Motility | Achalasia, systemic sclerosis, diffuse oesophageal spasm |

**Progressive dysphagia** starting with solids and progressing to liquids suggests mechanical obstruction (carcinoma) — urgent OGD. **Dysphagia for solids and liquids equally from the outset** suggests a motility disorder.

**Achalasia**: Failure of LOS relaxation due to loss of inhibitory neurons (nitric oxide-producing) in Auerbach's plexus. The LOS cannot relax normally on swallowing, and peristalsis is absent or disordered in the oesophageal body. Idiopathic; rarely secondary (Chagas disease from *Trypanosoma cruzi* destroying myenteric plexus). Presentation: dysphagia for solids and liquids, regurgitation of undigested food (not bile-stained — from oesophagus, not stomach), weight loss, nocturnal cough (aspiration). Diagnosis: manometry (high-resolution manometry — absent peristalsis + incomplete LOS relaxation is diagnostic); barium swallow shows "bird's beak" narrowing at the GOJ. OGD to exclude pseudoachalasia from carcinoma at the GOJ. Treatment: pneumatic dilation, laparoscopic Heller myotomy, or peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM). Achalasia is a risk factor for oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma.

## Acute Upper GI Bleeding

### Causes and Frequency

| Cause | Frequency | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| Peptic ulcer (duodenal or gastric) | ~50% | Most common overall; H. pylori and NSAIDs |
| Oesophageal/gastric varices | ~15% | Cirrhosis; high mortality — 30% per episode |
| Mallory-Weiss tear | ~10% | Mucosal tear at GOJ from severe vomiting; usually self-limiting |
| Erosive oesophagitis/gastritis | ~10% | |
| Malignancy | ~5% | |
| Dieulafoy lesion | Rare | Large submucosal artery, usually in gastric fundus, erodes mucosa without ulceration — causes sudden, massive, apparently unexplained haemorrhage; diagnosis at endoscopy when other causes excluded |
| Angiodysplasia | Rare | Elderly; may be recurrent; diagnosed at endoscopy |

### Clinical Presentation

**Haematemesis** — fresh red blood (rapid active bleeding) or "coffee grounds" (slower, oxidised blood mixed with gastric acid).

**Melaena** — black, tarry, foul-smelling stool from digested blood. Requires approximately 200 mL of blood in the upper GI tract. Can be mistaken for iron supplement stools — examine for the characteristic smell and sticky consistency.

**Haematochezia** (fresh rectal bleeding) can occur from a very rapid UGIB — these patients are haemodynamically unstable and often require simultaneous resuscitation and urgent endoscopy.

**Haemodynamic assessment**: Tachycardia, hypotension, and cool peripheries indicate haemodynamic compromise. Orthostatic hypotension (drop in systolic >20 mmHg on standing) indicates significant blood loss even with a normal lying BP.

### Risk Stratification

**Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS) — pre-endoscopy**:

Score 0 predicts no need for endoscopic intervention or transfusion — these patients may be managed as outpatients:
- Haemoglobin ≥13.0 g/dL (men) or ≥12.0 g/dL (women)
- Systolic BP ≥110 mmHg
- Pulse <100/min
- BUN <45 mg/dL (urea <16 mmol/L)
- No melaena, no syncope, no hepatic disease, no heart failure

Any single criterion failing this = inpatient management required.

**Rockall Score — post-endoscopy** (predicts mortality):

| Variable | 0 pts | 1 pt | 2 pts | 3 pts |
|---------|-------|------|-------|-------|
| Age | <60 | 60–79 | ≥80 | — |
| Shock | None | Pulse >100, SBP ≥100 | — | SBP <100 |
| Co-morbidity | None | — | IHD, HF, major organ failure | Metastatic malignancy, hepatic failure, renal failure |
| Diagnosis | Mallory-Weiss/no lesion | Other | — | Upper GI malignancy |
| Endoscopic stigmata | Clean base/flat spot | — | Active bleeding, clot, visible vessel | — |

Rockall score mortality: 0 = 0.2%, 1 = 2.4%, 2 = 5.6%, 3 = 11%, 4 = 24.6%, 5 = 39.6%, ≥8 = >40%. Score ≥3 indicates high risk for adverse outcome.

### Management

**Resuscitation first — simultaneous with assessment:**
- IV access × 2 large-bore cannulae (16G or larger), crossmatch 4–6 units
- IV crystalloid for haemodynamic instability; **not blood** until crossmatched
- **Restrictive transfusion strategy: transfuse to Hb ≥70 g/dL** — liberal transfusion raises portal pressure and increases rebleeding in variceal haemorrhage. Target Hb ≥80 g/dL in known ischaemic heart disease.
- Airway protection if obtunded with active haematemesis — intubation before endoscopy

**Endoscopy (OGD):**
- Within 24 hours for all admitted patients (once resuscitated and haemodynamically stable)
- Within 12 hours for high-risk features: haemodynamic instability after resuscitation, suspected variceal bleeding, ongoing haemorrhage
- Haemostasis techniques: adrenaline injection + thermal coagulation (bipolar probe/argon plasma) or haemoclips for ulcers; band ligation or injection sclerotherapy for varices

**Non-variceal UGIB:** High-dose IV PPI (omeprazole 80 mg bolus then 8 mg/hour for 72 hours) after successful endoscopic haemostasis — reduces rebleeding rate for high-risk stigmata. H. pylori eradication before discharge is mandatory in all H. pylori-positive UGIB — eradication dramatically reduces rebleeding.

**Variceal UGIB:**
- **Terlipressin 2 mg IV immediately** (synthetic vasopressin analogue — splanchnic vasoconstriction → reduced portal blood flow → reduced portal pressure). Start before endoscopy and continue for 2–5 days. The only vasopressin analogue shown to reduce 30-day mortality.
- **Endoscopic band ligation** — first-line for oesophageal varices at endoscopy
- **IV ceftriaxone 1 g daily for 5 days** — antibiotic prophylaxis in all patients with variceal bleeding; reduces SBP, infection, and 5-day mortality (NNT ~4 for preventing SBP)
- **TIPSS** (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) — for refractory or recurrent variceal bleeding not controlled by endoscopy
- Sengstaken-Blakemore tube — balloon tamponade as temporary bridge to TIPSS/surgery in exsanguinating haemorrhage
- **Secondary prophylaxis**: non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol 20–40 mg BD, titrate to heart rate 55–60 bpm) + endoscopic band ligation. Both together are superior to either alone.
- **Primary prophylaxis** of varices: propranolol 20–40 mg BD alone, or endoscopic band ligation alone, reduces first bleed risk in medium/large varices.

## Oesophageal Cancer

Two distinct histological types with different aetiology, location, and epidemiology:

- **Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)**: Mid-oesophagus. Risk factors: smoking, alcohol, achalasia, caustic ingestion, Plummer-Vinson syndrome, hot beverage consumption. Globally more common; predominates in Asia and Africa.
- **Adenocarcinoma (OAC)**: Lower oesophagus and gastro-oesophageal junction (GOJ). Risk factors: GORD, Barrett's oesophagus, obesity, male sex. Rising incidence in Western countries — now the more common type in the UK.

**Presentation**: Progressive dysphagia (solids first, then liquids — always structural), odynophagia, weight loss. Typically late presentation because the oesophagus distends before symptoms cause significant obstruction — most patients present with ≥50% luminal narrowing.

**Alarm symptom to know**: A Siewert type I tumour (>1 cm above the GOJ) is classified as oesophageal; Siewert type III (>2 cm below) is gastric; Siewert type II (true GOJ tumour) is intermediate — classification matters for surgical approach.

**Investigation**: OGD + biopsy (diagnosis), CT chest/abdomen/pelvis (staging), EUS (local T/N staging — most accurate for depth of invasion), PET-CT (occult distant metastases).

**Staging and management**:
- Stages I–II: Curative intent — neoadjuvant chemotherapy (FLOT regimen for adenocarcinoma; CROSS protocol — chemoradiotherapy — for SCC and some OAC) followed by Ivor Lewis oesophagectomy (right thoracotomy + laparotomy)
- Stage III: Downstaging attempt with neoadjuvant therapy; surgery if downstaged
- Stage IV: Palliative — systemic chemotherapy, endoscopic stenting for dysphagia relief, radiotherapy for pain control
- Overall 5-year survival ~15–25% (majority present stage III/IV)

## Zollinger-Ellison Syndrome

A gastrin-secreting tumour (gastrinoma) arising in the pancreas, duodenum, or stomach drives massive gastric acid hypersecretion, producing multiple, atypical, refractory peptic ulcers. Gastrinoma is a neuroendocrine tumour; 60% are malignant. ~20% are associated with MEN1 (multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 — parathyroid, pituitary, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours).

**Suspect ZES when**:
- Multiple simultaneous ulcers
- Ulcers distal to the first part of the duodenum
- Ulcers refractory to standard PPI therapy
- Diarrhoea from acid inactivation of pancreatic lipase (steatorrhoea)
- Family history of MEN1 or recurrent/early-onset peptic ulceration

**Diagnosis**: Fasting serum gastrin >1000 pg/mL (after stopping PPI for at least 1 week) is diagnostic. Hypochlorhydria (from PPIs or gastric atrophy) also raises gastrin — check gastric pH; should be <2 in ZES. If gastrin 100–1000 pg/mL with gastric pH <2, secretin stimulation test confirms diagnosis.

**Management**: High-dose PPI (omeprazole 60 mg/day, titrate to control symptoms and gastric pH); surgical resection if localised and no MEN1; somatostatin analogues (octreotide/lanreotide) for unresectable tumours.

## Complications

**Peptic ulcer**: Haemorrhage (most common — endoscopic haemostasis), perforation (free air under diaphragm on erect CXR; peritonitis — surgical emergency; Boey score predicts mortality), gastric outlet obstruction (fibrotic pyloric scarring from duodenal ulcer healing — persistent projectile vomiting, succussion splash, hypochloraemic hypokalaemic metabolic alkalosis).

**GORD**: Barrett's oesophagus, oesophageal stricture (benign — amenable to endoscopic dilation), adenocarcinoma.

**Acute UGIB**: 30-day mortality ~5–10% overall; ~20–30% for variceal bleeding per episode. Rebleeding, aspiration pneumonia, ischaemic complications.

## Clinical Insight

All gastric ulcers must be biopsied at endoscopy and followed with repeat OGD at 6–8 weeks to confirm healing. A gastric adenocarcinoma can ulcerate, appear benign, and partially heal with PPI therapy. Healing does not exclude malignancy. Performing a single diagnostic endoscopy without follow-up for a gastric ulcer — or attributing a gastric ulcer to NSAIDs without biopsy — is a clinical and medicolegal error.

The urea breath test gives a false negative if the patient is taking a PPI. PPIs suppress H. pylori metabolic activity without eradicating the organism — it becomes temporarily undetectable. Stop PPIs 2 weeks before testing. A negative UBT in a patient on omeprazole taken the day before is meaningless and provides false reassurance.

The restrictive transfusion threshold in acute UGIB (Hb 70 g/dL) saves lives — particularly in variceal bleeding, where over-transfusion raises portal venous pressure and precipitates rebleeding. Transfusing a haemodynamically stable patient with Hb of 72 g/dL to "make them safer" is the wrong decision. Check coagulation separately — PT/INR prolongation requires FFP, not more red cells.
