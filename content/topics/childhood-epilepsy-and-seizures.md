## Overview

Epilepsy affects approximately 1 in 200 children and is defined as a tendency to have unprovoked recurrent seizures. The vast majority of childhood epilepsies are well-controlled with a single antiseizure medication (ASM). The clinical challenge is threefold: making an accurate diagnosis (many paroxysmal events in children are not epileptic), choosing the right ASM for the specific epilepsy syndrome (phenotype-genotype matching increasingly important), and managing the paediatric emergency of status epilepticus — which carries a mortality of 3–5% in children and a neurological morbidity risk that rises with duration of seizure activity. Stopping seizures is time-critical: every minute of uncontrolled seizure activity causes progressive neuronal injury.

## Pathophysiology

### Mechanisms of Seizure Generation

A seizure occurs when there is excessive, synchronous, self-sustaining electrical discharge from a neuronal network. The balance between excitation (principally glutamatergic) and inhibition (principally GABAergic) is disrupted:
- **Too much excitation:** Mutations in sodium channel genes (SCN1A — Nav1.1) → increased neuronal excitability (Dravet syndrome — gain-of-function in embryonic glutamatergic neurons)
- **Too little inhibition:** GABA receptor mutations, loss of GABAergic interneurons → reduced inhibitory tone
- **Synchronisation:** Thalamo-cortical networks amplify focal discharge into generalised seizures (absence epilepsy — thalamo-cortical 3 Hz spike-and-wave)

### Status Epilepticus — Progressive Physiology

**Operational definition:** Seizure lasting ≥5 minutes OR two or more seizures without recovery of consciousness between them.

**Rationale for the 5-minute threshold:** Seizures that last >5 minutes rarely terminate spontaneously (the same mechanisms that terminate normal seizures — GABA receptor internalisation, extracellular K+ normalisation — are exhausted). The longer a seizure continues, the harder it becomes to terminate pharmacologically.

**Time-dependent neurological injury:**
- 0–5 min: Seizure begins; compensated phase (MAP and CBF rise to meet increased cerebral metabolic demand)
- 5–30 min: GABA-A receptor internalisation (synaptic → extrasynaptic → reduced drug target); catecholamine surge
- 30 min+: Decompensated — hyperthermia, lactic acidosis, hypoglycaemia, rhabdomyolysis, cerebral oedema, neuronal apoptosis. Systemic effects: hypoxia, hypotension, respiratory failure → secondary brain injury layered on top of primary seizure injury

### Epilepsy Syndromes — Key Types

**Childhood absence epilepsy (CAE):** Age 4–10 years; girls > boys. Brief (5–30 second) staring spells with sudden onset and offset; no post-ictal confusion; ~100+ episodes per day. EEG: classic 3 Hz spike-and-wave during absence. First-line treatment: **ethosuximide** (CHASES trial: superior to valproate and lamotrigine for absence control with better tolerability); valproate is second-line; lamotrigine third-line. >90% remission by age 12.

**Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME):** Age 12–20. Myoclonic jerks on waking (often dropping things), GTCS, often absence seizures. EEG: 4–6 Hz polyspike-and-wave. Lifelong condition — requires lifelong treatment. **Valproate** most effective; avoid valproate in females of childbearing potential (teratogenicity — 10× risk of neural tube defects, spina bifida; valproate exposure in utero associated with autism, developmental delay — VALPROATE IS VALPROATE PREGNANCY PREVENTION PROGRAMME).

**West syndrome (Infantile spasms):** Onset <1 year (peak 3–8 months). **Epileptic spasms** (sudden brief flexion or extension of trunk + extremities, often in clusters, typically on waking). Often associated with hypsarrhythmia on EEG (high-amplitude, chaotic, disorganised background). Cause: structural (tuberous sclerosis, hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy), metabolic (pyridoxine deficiency), or unknown. Treatment: **ACTH (tetracosactide)** or **vigabatrin** (tuberous sclerosis) — UK INFANT trial showed no difference; initiate ASAP; poor neurodevelopmental prognosis without early treatment.

**Dravet syndrome:** SCN1A mutation (Nav1.1 haploinsufficiency); onset <1 year. Prolonged febrile seizures → multiple seizure types; cognitive and developmental regression. **Avoid sodium channel blockers** (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin — worsen seizures by blocking the remaining functional Nav1.1, further reducing inhibitory interneuron function). Use: valproate, clobazam, stiripentol. **Fenfluramine** (FenfluramineDravet) and **cannabidiol (Epidyolex)** — new licensed agents for Dravet syndrome.

**FIRES (febrile infection-related epilepsy syndrome):** Devastating new-onset refractory status epilepticus in school-age children following febrile illness; often no structural lesion; CSF and MRI often normal; autoimmune? Treatment: high-dose anaesthetic agents + immunotherapy (IVIG, steroids, plasmapheresis, rituximab); ketogenic diet.

## Clinical Presentation

### Seizure Classification (ILAE 2017)

**Focal onset:** Begins in one hemisphere; may or may not impair awareness; may secondarily generalise.
- Focal aware (previously "simple partial"): consciousness preserved; may have motor, sensory, autonomic, or psychic features
- Focal with impaired awareness (previously "complex partial"): impaired consciousness; automatisms (lip-smacking, hand-picking)

**Generalised onset:** Both hemispheres simultaneously from the start:
- **Tonic-clonic** (GTCS): tonic phase (stiffening, eye deviation, apnoea) → clonic phase (rhythmic jerking) → post-ictal phase (confusion, drowsiness)
- **Absence:** Brief staring, eye fluttering, immediate recovery
- **Myoclonic:** Brief (<1s) muscle jerks; often bilateral; no loss of consciousness
- **Atonic (drop attacks):** Sudden loss of muscle tone → fall

**Unknown onset:** When the onset is not observed.

### Differential Diagnosis of Seizures

**Non-epileptic events to exclude:**
- **Febrile seizures** (simple: <15 min, generalised, single episode; complex: prolonged, focal, or multiple in 24h)
- **Breath-holding attacks:** Provoked by frustration/pain; expiratory apnoea → cyanosis → brief LOC; often age 6–18 months
- **Reflex anoxic seizures:** Vagally-mediated anoxic events; triggered by minor head bumps; asystole on ECG
- **Syncope with anoxic convulsion:** Vasovagal episode; tonic/clonic movements may occur during syncope; confused for epilepsy
- **Cardiac arrhythmia** (long QT syndrome): ECG essential in any child with unexplained loss of consciousness
- **Psychogenic non-epileptic attacks (PNEA):** Typically in older children/adolescents; movements out-of-phase; pelvic thrusting; eyes closed (epileptic seizures: eyes open); normal post-ictal EEG

## Diagnosis

**EEG:** Essential for epilepsy syndrome classification — does NOT diagnose or exclude epilepsy (a normal EEG does not mean the child doesn't have epilepsy; a single abnormal EEG does not diagnose epilepsy). Sleep-deprived EEG, prolonged EEG, video-EEG telemetry for diagnostic certainty.

**MRI brain:** For all children with focal-onset epilepsy, epilepsy not responding to first ASM, and neurodevelopmental regression. MRI (not CT) — superior resolution for cortical dysplasia, hippocampal sclerosis, heterotopia.

**Genetics:** SCN1A, CDKL5, PRRT2, ARX, and other gene panels — increasingly important for syndrome diagnosis and treatment choice. Array CGH (chromosomal microarray) for developmental epilepsies.

**Blood/metabolic:** Glucose (hypoglycaemia), sodium (hyponatraemia), calcium, magnesium, organic acids, amino acids (for metabolic epilepsies in infants).

## Management

### Status Epilepticus — Protocol (APLS 2021)

**Phase 1 (0–10 minutes) — Benzodiazepine:**

First benzodiazepine (if no IV access):
- **Buccal midazolam** 0.3 mg/kg (max 10 mg): buccal mucosa absorption; faster onset than rectal diazepam; first-line in community
- **Intranasal midazolam** 0.3 mg/kg (via mucosal atomiser): equivalent efficacy to buccal; useful if buccal access difficult
- **Rectal diazepam** 0.5 mg/kg (max 10 mg): used if buccal midazolam unavailable; slower onset

If IV access available:
- **IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg** (max 4 mg): first-line IV benzodiazepine; longer duration than diazepam; less respiratory depression

**Repeat benzodiazepine at 10 minutes** if no cessation (same drug and dose).

**Phase 2 (10–20 minutes) — Second-line antiseizure drug:**

After failure of two benzodiazepine doses, give one of:
- **IV levetiracetam 40–60 mg/kg** (max 3000 mg over 10 minutes): now first-line in many centres; no need for cardiac monitoring; safe in liver disease; ECLIPSE trial confirmed non-inferiority to phenytoin; minimal respiratory depression
- **IV phenytoin 20 mg/kg** (max 2 g over 20 minutes): requires cardiac monitoring (bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmia risk); extravasation can cause severe tissue necrosis (use fosphenytoin where available — water-soluble prodrug of phenytoin, given 15–20 mg phenytoin equivalents/kg over 5 min)
- **IV sodium valproate 20–40 mg/kg** over 10 minutes: avoid in liver disease, metabolic disorders, suspected mitochondrial disease; not recommended in Dravet syndrome

**Phase 3 (20–60 minutes) — Refractory status epilepticus: PICU**

Failure of second-line drug → call PICU / paediatric anaesthetics:
- **Rapid sequence intubation** (propofol or thiopental induction + rocuronium)
- **IV thiopental infusion** (3–5 mg/kg/h) — traditional
- **IV propofol** (caution in children — prolonged propofol infusion syndrome)
- **IV midazolam infusion** (0.1–0.4 mg/kg/h) — often used first in PICU
- **IV phenobarbital** as an alternative second-line if levetiracetam/phenytoin/valproate not available: 20 mg/kg loading dose

### Long-Term Epilepsy Management

**Principles of ASM choice:**
- Identify the epilepsy syndrome (EEG + clinical features)
- Choose ASM appropriate for that syndrome
- Start at low dose, titrate up (minimise side effects)
- Aim for monotherapy; add second drug only if first fails at maximum tolerated dose
- Discuss SUDEP (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy): risk 1:1000 epileptic children per year; higher with GTCS; increased with poor medication adherence; reduce by optimising seizure control, nocturnal supervision

**First-line ASMs by seizure type:**
- Absence: Ethosuximide (focal absence) or valproate
- GTCS: Valproate (most effective), levetiracetam, lamotrigine
- Focal seizures: Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, levetiracetam
- Infantile spasms: ACTH, vigabatrin, prednisolone

**Ketogenic diet:** High-fat, low-carbohydrate diet → ketosis; mechanism uncertain (may alter mitochondrial function, reduce glutamate); 30–40% of refractory epilepsy patients achieve >50% seizure reduction. Supervised by specialist dietitian.

**Vagal nerve stimulator (VNS):** Implanted cervical vagal nerve stimulator; stimulates vagus → inhibits seizure propagation; reduces seizure frequency by >50% in 30–50% of refractory patients.

**Epilepsy surgery:** For drug-resistant focal epilepsy with identifiable structural lesion; cortical resection, hemispherectomy, corpus callosotomy; 50–70% seizure-free at 2 years for well-selected candidates.

## Complications

- **Status epilepticus:** Neuronal injury, aspiration pneumonia, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis, AKI, respiratory failure; mortality 3–5% in children
- **SUDEP:** Sudden unexplained death in epilepsy — most important long-term risk; poorly understood mechanism; possibly respiratory arrest post-GTCS
- **ASM side effects:** Valproate — weight gain, hair loss, liver toxicity, teratogenicity; carbamazepine — hyponatraemia, rash (Stevens-Johnson syndrome with HLA-B*1502 in Asian patients — genetic test before starting in Asian patients); phenytoin — gingival hypertrophy, hirsutism, cerebellar atrophy; lamotrigine — rash (dose-related, slow titration reduces risk); vigabatrin — irreversible peripheral visual field constriction (regular ophthalmological monitoring)
- **Cognitive effects:** Poorly controlled epilepsy + ASM side effects → learning difficulties, school underperformance
- **Psychological:** Depression, anxiety common in adolescents with epilepsy; PNEA (psychogenic non-epileptic attacks) may co-exist with true epilepsy

## Clinical Insight

The most dangerous mistake in status epilepticus is failing to identify that IV phenytoin/phenobarbital will not work in Dravet syndrome. The SCN1A mutation in Dravet syndrome means that the normal sodium channels (Nav1.1) are already haploinsufficient in GABAergic interneurons. Blocking sodium channels further with phenytoin (or carbamazepine) removes the remaining inhibitory function from these interneurons → paradoxical worsening of seizures, potentially fatal. In any child with a history of prolonged febrile seizures from infancy + multiple seizure types + known or suspected Dravet syndrome, use levetiracetam or valproate — never phenytoin or carbamazepine.

The transition from Phase 1 to Phase 2 must happen at 10 minutes — not 20, not "when we get round to it." The APLS guideline uses clear time points because time-dependent neurological injury is real. In many resuscitation rooms, the second benzodiazepine dose is given at the right time but the second-line drug (levetiracetam/phenytoin) is delayed because it requires drawing up or calculating a weight-based dose. Precalculate the second-line drug dose while giving the first benzodiazepine. When the seizure persists at 10 minutes, the drug is already drawn up.

Post-ictal confusion lasts minutes to hours — persisting confusion beyond 30–60 minutes should prompt consideration of non-convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE), where electrical seizure activity continues without visible convulsion. NCSE in children after convulsive status epilepticus is common and requires EEG to diagnose — the child appears to be "recovering" but is actually in continuous non-convulsive seizure activity causing ongoing neuronal damage.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** Dravet syndrome, 25 minutes of continuous GTCS. Buccal midazolam 10 mg (correct dose) given 20 minutes ago — no effect. SpO₂ 88%, HR 148, no IV access.

**Step 1 — Airway + oxygen (immediately):**
- Recovery position (risk of aspiration)
- High-flow O₂ via non-rebreathe mask or BVM
- SpO₂ 88% → this is a secondary concern; controlling the seizure is primary

**Step 2 — Assessment of timing:**
- Seizure ongoing for 25 minutes → already in Phase 2/3 territory
- One benzodiazepine given 20 minutes ago with no effect
- **This is refractory status epilepticus** — Phase 3 management needed

**Step 3 — Establish access and second-line drug:**
- Intraosseous access (IO) — faster than peripheral IV in a convulsing child; tibial IO in under 1 minute
- **IV/IO levetiracetam 60 mg/kg** (max 3000 mg) infused over 10 minutes — first-line second-line drug
- **Do NOT give phenytoin in Dravet syndrome** — sodium channel blockade paradoxically worsens Dravet seizures (Nav1.1 pathway)

**Step 4 — Simultaneously: call PICU + anaesthetics:**
- 25+ minutes of status = will likely need general anaesthesia; do not wait for levetiracetam to work before calling
- Prepare for RSI if seizure not aborting

**Step 5 — If levetiracetam fails (20 minutes after start = now ~35 min total):**
- **IV/IO midazolam infusion** or **IV phenobarbital 20 mg/kg** (appropriate in Dravet — does NOT block sodium channels)
- RSI + intubation + thiopental/propofol infusion for refractory SE in PICU

**Step 6 — Monitoring:**
- Glucose (hypoglycaemia worsens seizures — check immediately; treat if <60 mg/dL with IV/IO dextrose)
- Temperature (Dravet syndrome: fever-triggered — active cooling if febrile)
- Post-status EEG: exclude NCSE when convulsive activity stops

**Exam trigger:** *Dravet syndrome + status epilepticus + buccal midazolam failed + no IV access* → IO access + levetiracetam (NOT phenytoin — contraindicated in Dravet) + PICU call. The distractor is giving IV phenytoin because the protocol says "phenytoin after two benzodiazepines." In Dravet syndrome, phenytoin worsens the seizure.
