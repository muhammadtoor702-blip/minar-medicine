## Overview

Labour is the process by which regular, painful contractions lead to progressive cervical effacement and dilation, ultimately resulting in delivery of the fetus, placenta, and membranes. Understanding the mechanics and timeline of normal labour is essential — not just to reassure patients, but because recognising deviation from normal is how you prevent disasters.

The **Bishop score** assesses cervical favourability before induction of labour. Five components: dilation (0-3 cm = 0, 4-6 cm = 1, 7-8 cm = 2, ≥9 cm = 3), effacement (<30% = 0, 30-50% = 1, 60-70% = 2, ≥80% = 3), consistency (firm = 0, medium = 1, soft = 2), position (posterior = 0, mid = 1, anterior = 2), and fetal station (-3 = 0, -2 = 1, -1/0 = 2, +1/+2 = 3). A score ≥8 means the cervix is ripe — induction will likely succeed without prior cervical ripening. A score ≤6 suggests prostaglandin ripening is needed first.

## Pathophysiology

Labour onset involves a complex interplay of fetal, placental, and maternal hormones. Rising fetal cortisol signals maturity and drives prostaglandin synthesis in the decidua and myometrium. Oestrogen/progesterone ratio shifts in favour of oestrogen → gap junctions form between myometrial cells → coordinated contractions become possible. Oxytocin receptors proliferate in the myometrium, making it increasingly sensitive.

Effective labour requires **four Ps**: powers (uterine contractions + maternal pushing), passages (bony pelvis + soft tissue), passenger (fetal size, lie, presentation, position), and psyche (maternal cooperation and pain management). When labour stalls, ask which P is responsible — this guides intervention.

## Clinical Presentation

Labour is divided into three stages:

**First stage** — onset of regular contractions to full cervical dilation:
- *Latent phase*: 0–4 cm; irregular or irregular contractions; duration variable (can be days), not always in hospital. Frustratingly slow — don't diagnose failure to progress here too early.
- *Active phase*: 4–10 cm; regular contractions every 2-3 min; expected rate ≥0.5–1.0 cm/hour in nulliparous women (slightly faster in multiparous). The WHO safe labour guideline uses a 4-hour action line — if cervical progress falls 4 hours behind the alert line, reassess and consider augmentation.

**Second stage** — full dilation to delivery of baby:
- *Passive phase*: fully dilated but no urge to push; allow descent of the presenting part.
- *Active phase*: active pushing begins once urge is felt or passive phase has been 1–2 hours. Maximum duration 2 hours for nulliparous women (3 hours with epidural), 1 hour for multiparous (2 hours with epidural) before considering obstetric intervention.

**Third stage** — delivery of placenta and membranes (usually within 30 minutes). The key decision is active versus physiological management.

## Diagnosis

**CTG interpretation (NICE 2017 framework):** Every CTG trace has four features — baseline rate, variability, accelerations, and decelerations. Classify each feature as reassuring, non-reassuring, or abnormal, then categorise the overall trace:

| Category | Definition |
|----------|-----------|
| Normal | All four features reassuring |
| Suspicious | One non-reassuring feature |
| Pathological | Two or more non-reassuring features, OR one abnormal feature |

**Baseline rate**: Normal 110–160 bpm. Tachycardia (>160) suggests hypoxia, infection, maternal fever, or medications. Bradycardia (<110) sustained is sinister.

**Variability**: Normal 5–25 bpm. Reduced variability (<5 bpm for >50 minutes) is non-reassuring — the fetus isn't responding dynamically to its environment. Sinusoidal pattern (smooth, undulating, 2–5 cycles/min, no accelerations) = fetal anaemia or severe hypoxia — emergency.

**Accelerations**: Rise ≥15 bpm above baseline for ≥15 seconds. Reassuring — indicates an intact sympathetic nervous system. Absence of accelerations alone does not classify the trace as pathological if other features are normal.

**Decelerations — the critical distinctions:**

| Type | Timing | Cause | Significance |
|------|--------|-------|--------------|
| Early | Mirror contraction exactly; nadir at contraction peak | Head compression (vagal) | Benign — normal in active labour |
| Late | Begin after contraction peak; return after contraction ends | Uteroplacental insufficiency → fetal hypoxia | Pathological |
| Variable | Abrupt onset, V-shaped or W-shaped, variable timing | Cord compression | Depends on depth/duration; with atypical features = non-reassuring |
| Prolonged | Single deceleration >3 minutes | Multiple causes | Emergency if >10 min or below 80 bpm |

**Fetal scalp sampling**: Indicated for suspicious or pathological CTG to assess fetal acid-base status. pH <7.20 = fetal acidaemia → deliver immediately. pH 7.20–7.25 = borderline → repeat in 30 minutes. pH >7.25 = reassuring → continue monitoring. **Fetal scalp stimulation** is a quick non-invasive alternative — an acceleration in response to gentle scalp rubbing is strongly reassuring (negative predictive value for acidaemia is high).

## Management

**Active management of third stage**: This is standard of care in the UK. Administer **oxytocin 10 IU IM** with delivery of the anterior shoulder (or immediately after delivery), then **controlled cord traction** (Brandt-Andrews manoeuvre) once signs of placental separation appear (cord lengthening, gush of blood, uterus rising). Active management reduces PPH risk by ~60% compared with physiological management.

**Induction of labour (IOL)**:

*Indications*: Post-term pregnancy (≥42 weeks — stillbirth risk doubles), pre-eclampsia, prelabour rupture of membranes (PROM) at term, IUGR, maternal diabetes (timing depends on control and complications), previous stillbirth.

*Process*:
1. Assess Bishop score
2. If unfavourable: **dinoprostone** (prostaglandin E2) pessary or gel — placed in posterior fornix, softens and ripens cervix over 6–24 hours
3. Reassess Bishop score; if ≥8: **ARM** (artificial rupture of membranes) using an amnihook under sterile conditions
4. If contractions inadequate after ARM: **Syntocinon** (IV oxytocin) infusion, titrated according to protocol
5. **Continuous CTG** mandatory once on Syntocinon

*Risks*: Uterine hyperstimulation (tachysystole — >5 contractions in 10 min; treat by removing pessary/stopping oxytocin and tocolysis with terbutaline 0.25 mg SC), failed induction requiring CS, cord prolapse after ARM.

**Shoulder dystocia**: The anterior shoulder becomes impacted behind the pubic symphysis after delivery of the head. The turtle sign (head retracts back against the perineum) is your warning. This is an obstetric emergency. Use the **HELPERR** mnemonic:

- **H** — Call for Help (senior obstetrician, paediatrician, extra midwives)
- **E** — Evaluate for Episiotomy (creates room for manoeuvres, doesn't release the bony impaction)
- **L** — Legs — McRoberts manoeuvre (hyperflexion of maternal hips — flattens lumbar lordosis, rotates symphysis superiorly, increasing functional AP diameter)
- **P** — Pressure — suprapubic pressure (downward and lateral toward the fetal face, dislodges anterior shoulder)
- **E** — Enter — internal rotational manoeuvres: Rubin II (pressure on the posterior aspect of the anterior shoulder to rotate baby) and Woods screw (pressure on the anterior aspect of the posterior shoulder — combined = screw effect)
- **R** — Remove the posterior arm (sweep arm across chest and deliver)
- **R** — Roll to all-fours position (Gaskin manoeuvre)

If all manoeuvres fail: **Zavanelli manoeuvre** (cephalic replacement + emergency CS) or deliberate clavicle fracture.

Complication to know: **Erb's palsy** — C5/C6 traction injury causing "waiter's tip" posture (arm adducted and internally rotated, forearm pronated, wrist flexed). Klumpke's palsy (C8/T1) is much rarer.

## Complications

**Postpartum haemorrhage (PPH)**:
- *Definition*: Primary PPH = >500 mL within 24 hours of delivery (>1000 mL = major PPH, >2000 mL = massive PPH)
- *The Four Ts*:

| T | Cause | Frequency |
|---|-------|-----------|
| **Tone** | Uterine atony | 70% |
| **Trauma** | Lacerations, haematoma, uterine rupture | 20% |
| **Tissue** | Retained placenta/membranes | 9% |
| **Thrombin** | Coagulopathy (DIC, HELLP, pre-existing) | 1% |

**Management ladder** — work through this rapidly and in parallel:
1. **Oxytocin** infusion (40 IU in 500 mL at 125 mL/hour)
2. **Ergometrine** 0.5 mg IM/IV (contraindicated in hypertension — severe vasoconstriction)
3. **Carboprost** (15-methyl PGF2α) 250 mcg IM every 15 minutes, up to 8 doses — **contraindicated in asthma** (bronchoconstriction)
4. **Tranexamic acid** 1 g IV (antifibrinolytic — reduces mortality if given within 3 hours, WOMAN trial)
5. **Intrauterine balloon tamponade** (Bakri balloon)
6. **Surgical**: B-Lynch suture (compressive uterine suture), uterine artery ligation, internal iliac artery ligation
7. **Hysterectomy** — life-saving last resort

Activate **massive haemorrhage protocol** early: 1:1:1 ratio of packed red cells, FFP, and platelets. Involve haematology, anaesthetics, and senior obstetrics from the start.

Perineal lacerations: 1st degree (skin only), 2nd degree (perineal muscle), 3rd degree (external anal sphincter involved — 3a <50%, 3b >50%, 3c internal sphincter), 4th degree (anorectal mucosa). Third and fourth degree tears require repair in theatre.

## Clinical Insight

The CTG in this scenario shows **early decelerations** — they mirror contractions precisely, with the nadir exactly at the contraction peak, and they recover before the contraction ends. This is head compression through a vagal reflex (pressure on the fetal occiput during contractions). It is completely benign and expected in active labour, especially as the fetal head descends. The key distinguishing feature from late decelerations is timing: early decels start and end with the contraction; late decels start after the contraction peak and persist after the contraction ends.

A common exam trap: students label any deceleration as "pathological." Ask yourself: does it start late, or mirror the contraction? With good baseline variability and accelerations present in this trace, this is a normal CTG — no action needed beyond routine monitoring.

Remember that **oxytocin (Syntocinon) augmentation must always be accompanied by continuous CTG** — oxytocin increases contraction frequency and strength, raising the risk of uterine hyperstimulation and late decelerations.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**Working through the case**: A 28-year-old primigravida at 39+2 weeks is in active labour — 6 cm dilated, fully effaced, station -1. Contractions every 3 minutes. She is in the **active phase of first stage** with normal progress. The CTG shows baseline 145 bpm (normal), good variability (reassuring), accelerations present (reassuring), and decelerations that start with each contraction and return before the contraction ends — these are **early decelerations**.

Classification: **Normal CTG**. All four features are reassuring. Early decelerations are not counted as a non-reassuring feature in NICE 2017 guidance.

Management: Continue routine midwifery-led care. Encourage mobilisation or preferred position. Review CTG every 30 minutes. Discuss pain relief options. Plan: repeat vaginal examination in 4 hours (or sooner if she has urge to push or is in significant distress).

The second stage maximum is 2 hours of active pushing for a nulliparous woman without epidural — failure to deliver beyond this should prompt obstetric review and consideration of instrumental delivery (ventouse preferred as first attempt if >35 weeks; forceps in specific indications or if ventouse fails).

At delivery: actively manage third stage — oxytocin 10 IU IM, controlled cord traction, inspect placenta for completeness, inspect perineum systematically for lacerations.

**Exam trigger:** The classic presentation is a term primigravida in active labour with decelerations on CTG that mirror contractions — the answer is early decelerations, benign, no action needed. The most common distractor is mistaking early decelerations for late decelerations and incorrectly categorising the CTG as pathological; remember that the key distinction is timing relative to the contraction peak, not the presence of the deceleration itself.
