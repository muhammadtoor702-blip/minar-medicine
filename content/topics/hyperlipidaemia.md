## Overview

Hyperlipidaemia encompasses elevated total cholesterol, LDL, triglycerides, or a reduction in HDL, and represents one of the most important modifiable cardiovascular risk factors. Elevated LDL is causally and dose-dependently related to atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) — the relationship is continuous, consistent across populations, and independent of other risk factors. Management is guided by overall cardiovascular risk, not absolute lipid levels in isolation, because the benefit of LDL reduction scales with baseline cardiovascular risk.

---

## Pathophysiology

### LDL and Atherogenesis

LDL carries cholesterol from the liver to peripheral tissues via LDL receptors. When LDL is elevated, it infiltrates the arterial intima — particularly at points of disturbed flow (bifurcations, curves). Once in the intima, LDL undergoes **oxidative modification**, becoming oxidised LDL (oxLDL). Macrophages engulf oxLDL voraciously via scavenger receptors (not subject to feedback regulation), becoming cholesterol-laden **foam cells** — the cellular hallmark of the early atherosclerotic lesion (fatty streak). Progressive lipid accumulation, smooth muscle cell migration, and fibrous cap formation produce a **mature atherosclerotic plaque** susceptible to rupture.

### HDL and Reverse Cholesterol Transport

HDL transports cholesterol from peripheral tissues back to the liver for excretion — the process of **reverse cholesterol transport**. Higher HDL is associated with lower cardiovascular risk, though HDL-raising interventions have not consistently reduced cardiovascular events in trials. HDL provides additional anti-atherogenic effects via anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms.

### Key Lipoproteins and Their Clinical Significance

| Lipoprotein | Clinical Significance |
|------------|----------------------|
| **LDL** | Primary atherogenic particle; the main therapeutic target |
| **HDL** | Protective; reduced in metabolic syndrome, diabetes, obesity |
| **Triglycerides (TG)** | Elevated TG associated with pancreatitis risk; independent modest CVD risk factor |
| **VLDL** | TG-carrying lipoprotein; contributes to atherogenesis |
| **Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]** | Modified LDL + apolipoprotein(a); independent CVD risk; largely genetically determined; not reduced by statins |
| **Non-HDL cholesterol** | Total cholesterol minus HDL; captures all atherogenic lipoproteins; a better CVD risk predictor than LDL alone |

**Lipoprotein(a):** Approximately 20–25% of the population has elevated Lp(a) (>50 mg/dL or >125 nmol/L). It is not measured in a standard lipid panel and is not lowered by statins. Lp(a) contributes to premature atherosclerosis, calcific aortic stenosis, and thrombosis via its homology with plasminogen (inhibiting fibrinolysis). PCSK9 inhibitors reduce Lp(a) by ~25%.

### Familial Hypercholesterolaemia (FH)

FH is an **autosomal dominant** disorder causing severely elevated LDL from birth due to:
- **LDL receptor mutations** (most common, ~85%) — impaired LDL clearance from circulation
- **ApoB mutations** — LDL cannot bind to its receptor
- **PCSK9 gain-of-function mutations** — accelerated LDL receptor degradation

**Heterozygous FH** (prevalence 1:200–1:500) — one abnormal gene; LDL typically 190–400 mg/dL; premature CAD in men 40–50 years, women 50–60 years. **Homozygous FH** (prevalence 1:300,000) — both alleles mutated; LDL >400–600 mg/dL; CAD and aortic stenosis in childhood.

FH is severely **underdiagnosed** — only ~10–15% of patients with FH in the UK are identified. Cascade screening (testing first-degree relatives of an index case) is the most efficient detection strategy — each child has a 50% chance of inheriting FH.

### Secondary Causes of Dyslipidaemia

Always exclude secondary causes before attributing dyslipidaemia to a primary disorder:

| Cause | Lipid Pattern | Notes |
|------|--------------|-------|
| **Hypothyroidism** | ↑LDL, ↑TG | Check TFTs in all new presentations |
| **Type 2 diabetes** | ↑TG, ↓HDL, small dense LDL | Increased atherogenicity per LDL particle |
| **CKD and nephrotic syndrome** | ↑LDL, ↑TG | Reduced LDL receptor expression; urinary HDL loss |
| **Alcohol excess** | ↑TG, ↑HDL | Alcohol stimulates hepatic VLDL production |
| **Cushing's syndrome** | ↑LDL, ↑TG | Glucocorticoid-mediated |
| **Drugs** | Variable | Corticosteroids, thiazides, beta-blockers (↑TG, ↓HDL); isotretinoin; antipsychotics (second-generation) |
| **Cholestasis** | ↑cholesterol | Lipoprotein X accumulation |
| **Obesity** | ↑TG, ↓HDL | Insulin resistance |

## Clinical Presentation

Hyperlipidaemia is **asymptomatic** until it causes cardiovascular disease or the specific clinical manifestations of extreme lipid elevation. Clinical signs — when present — provide important diagnostic information:

**Signs of severe dyslipidaemia:**

| Sign | Significance | Notes |
|-----|-------------|-------|
| **Tendon xanthomata** | Pathognomonic of FH | Firm, painless deposits over Achilles tendons and extensor tendons of the fingers; feel like nodular thickening of the tendon |
| **Xanthelasma** | Lipid deposits around the eyelids | Less specific than tendon xanthomata; occurs in normolipidaemic individuals; however, in a young person with elevated LDL, strongly suggestive of FH |
| **Corneal arcus** | Lipid ring at the corneal margin | Not significant if over 50 years; significant if present under 45 — suggests severe dyslipidaemia |
| **Eruptive xanthomata** | Small yellowish papules on buttocks, shoulders, extensor surfaces | Indicate severe hypertriglyceridaemia — TG typically >900 mg/dL; risk of pancreatitis |
| **Lipaemia retinalis** | Creamy/white appearance of retinal vessels | TG typically >1800 mg/dL; fundoscopy reveals white retinal vessels |
| **Palmar xanthomata** | Yellowish deposits in palmar creases | Characteristic of Type III (familial dysbetalipoproteinaemia, APOE2/E2 genotype) |

## Diagnosis

### Lipid Profile

**Fasting lipid profile:** Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, and **non-HDL cholesterol** (total minus HDL — captures all atherogenic lipoproteins including VLDL, IDL, and Lp(a); better predictor of CV risk than LDL alone, especially when TG are elevated).

A non-fasting sample is acceptable for screening — TG should be measured fasting if >3.4 mmol/L (300 mg/dL) on random testing.

### Cardiovascular Risk Assessment

- **QRISK3** (UK primary care) — estimates 10-year risk of ASCVD; incorporates age, sex, BP, cholesterol ratio, smoking, diabetes, AF, chronic kidney disease, and deprivation
- **SCORE2** (European) — estimates 10-year fatal and non-fatal CVD risk

Risk stratification drives LDL targets — a 70-year-old with multiple risk factors benefits far more from LDL reduction than a 40-year-old with isolated hyperlipidaemia.

### Familial Hypercholesterolaemia Diagnosis

**Clinical diagnosis** using Dutch Lipid Clinic Network (DLCN) criteria or Simon Broome criteria, incorporating:
- Fasting LDL level
- Clinical findings (tendon xanthomata, corneal arcus, xanthelasma)
- Family history (premature CVD in first-degree relatives; similar lipid levels in relatives)
- Genetic testing (confirms mutation; important for cascade screening)

**Threshold:** LDL >190 mg/dL with a first-degree relative with similar levels or premature CVD should trigger FH evaluation. Tendon xanthomata at any LDL level = definite FH.

### Secondary Cause Screen

Before starting therapy, always check: TFTs, fasting glucose/HbA1c, U&E, LFTs, urine albumin:creatinine ratio.

**Baseline before starting statin:** ALT and CK (to identify pre-existing liver disease or myopathy).

## Management

### Lifestyle — Mandatory for All

Lifestyle measures reduce LDL by approximately 10–20% and should be initiated in all patients regardless of whether drug therapy is planned:

- **Diet:** Reduce saturated fat (red meat, full-fat dairy, coconut oil) — replace with unsaturated fats (olive oil, nuts, avocado, oily fish); increase soluble fibre (oats, legumes, psyllium, flaxseed) — soluble fibre binds bile acids in the gut, forcing hepatic cholesterol utilisation; plant sterols and stanols (2g/day) reduce LDL by ~10%
- **Exercise:** Regular aerobic exercise raises HDL, reduces TG, and modestly lowers LDL
- **Weight loss:** 10kg reduction reduces LDL by ~15 mg/dL and TG substantially
- **Alcohol reduction:** Dramatically lowers triglycerides (alcohol stimulates hepatic TG synthesis)
- **Smoking cessation:** Raises HDL, reduces oxidative modification of LDL, reduces overall ASCVD risk

### Risk Categories and LDL Targets (ESC 2019)

| Risk Category | Definition | LDL Target |
|-------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Very high** | Established CVD; DM with organ damage or ≥3 risk factors; CKD stage G3b–G5; FH + risk factor; SCORE2 ≥10% | <55 mg/dL AND ≥50% reduction from baseline |
| **High** | Markedly elevated single risk factor; CKD G3a; SCORE2 5–10% | <70 mg/dL AND ≥50% reduction |
| **Moderate** | SCORE2 1–5% | <100 mg/dL |
| **Low** | SCORE2 <1% | <115 mg/dL |

In very high-risk patients (e.g., post-ACS), both the absolute target (<55 mg/dL) AND the relative reduction (≥50% from baseline) must be achieved.

### Statins — First-Line Therapy

Statins inhibit **HMG-CoA reductase** — the rate-limiting enzyme of hepatic cholesterol synthesis. Reduced intracellular cholesterol upregulates LDL receptor expression on hepatocytes, increasing LDL clearance from the circulation. Statins are more effective when given **at night** (hepatic cholesterol synthesis peaks at night).

**High-intensity statins** (reduce LDL by ≥50%):
- Atorvastatin 40–80mg OD
- Rosuvastatin 20–40mg OD

**Moderate-intensity statins** (reduce LDL 30–49%):
- Simvastatin 20–40mg OD; atorvastatin 10–20mg; rosuvastatin 5–10mg

**Indications for statin therapy:**
- All patients with established ASCVD (secondary prevention)
- Primary prevention when 10-year QRISK3 ≥10%
- All patients with FH (regardless of risk score)
- All patients with diabetes aged >40 (or younger with additional risk factors)

**Side effects:**
- **Myopathy** — myalgia in 5–10% of patients (dose-dependent; often resolves with dose reduction or switching statin); check CK if symptomatic. **Rhabdomyolysis** is rare but serious — CK >10× ULN + systemic symptoms (myoglobinuria, AKI)
- **Elevated transaminases** — usually transient, mild, and asymptomatic; significant hepatotoxicity is uncommon; routine liver monitoring after initiation no longer recommended (unless symptomatic)
- **New-onset diabetes** — small increased risk (~10–15% relative risk with high-intensity statins); does not outweigh cardiovascular benefit in high-risk patients

**Risk of myopathy increased by:**
- High statin dose
- Co-prescription of **fibrates** — especially gemfibrozil; CYP2C8-mediated inhibition of statin metabolism dramatically increases plasma statin concentrations. **Never combine gemfibrozil with a statin.** Fenofibrate is the safe fibrate to use alongside statins when both are required.
- **CYP3A4 inhibitors** — clarithromycin, azole antifungals (itraconazole, ketoconazole), HIV protease inhibitors, grapefruit juice (with simvastatin and lovastatin primarily)
- Untreated hypothyroidism — myopathy risk is significantly higher; always check TFTs before starting statin
- Alcohol excess

### Ezetimibe

Selectively inhibits **NPC1L1**, the intestinal cholesterol transporter, reducing dietary and biliary cholesterol absorption. Reduces LDL by approximately 15–20% when added to a statin.

- **Indication:** Add to maximum tolerated statin when LDL target is not achieved
- **Evidence:** IMPROVE-IT trial — ezetimibe added to simvastatin post-ACS reduced the composite of cardiovascular events by a modest but significant amount; demonstrated "lower is better" principle
- **Side effects:** Generally well-tolerated; rare transaminase elevation; check for drug interactions

### PCSK9 Inhibitors (Evolocumab, Alirocumab)

PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) is a hepatic enzyme that binds to LDL receptors on the hepatocyte surface and tags them for lysosomal degradation. Inhibiting PCSK9 allows LDL receptors to recycle to the cell surface, dramatically increasing LDL clearance from the circulation.

- **Effect:** Reduce LDL by 50–60% **on top of statin therapy** — the largest LDL reduction of any currently available drug class
- **Administration:** SC injection every 2 weeks (alirocumab) or monthly (evolocumab)
- **Evidence:**
  - **FOURIER trial** (evolocumab): reduced LDL from ~92 mg/dL to ~30 mg/dL; 15% relative risk reduction in major ASCVD events
  - **ODYSSEY OUTCOMES** (alirocumab): reduced LDL from ~87 mg/dL to ~40 mg/dL; 15% RRR; mortality benefit in patients with baseline LDL >100 mg/dL
- **Indications:** Very high-risk patients not at LDL target on maximum statin + ezetimibe; statin-intolerant patients with FH or established CVD; recurrent ASCVD events on optimal medical therapy
- **Side effects:** Injection site reactions; mild flu-like symptoms; well-tolerated overall
- **Lp(a) reduction:** PCSK9 inhibitors also reduce Lp(a) by ~25% — a unique benefit not shared by statins

### Inclisiran (siRNA)

A small interfering RNA that silences PCSK9 synthesis in the liver. Administered as two SC injections in the first year, then twice-yearly. Equivalent LDL reduction to PCSK9 inhibitor antibodies with dramatically less frequent dosing — may improve adherence.

### Hypertriglyceridaemia

**TG 175–885 mg/dL (2–10 mmol/L):** Lifestyle modification first — reduce alcohol, refined carbohydrates, and simple sugars; treat secondary causes (diabetes, hypothyroidism, CKD); weight loss; omega-3 fatty acids (4g/day for moderate hypertriglyceridaemia). Fenofibrate if lifestyle insufficient and TG >500 mg/dL.

**TG >885 mg/dL (>10 mmol/L):** Significant risk of **acute pancreatitis** — the dominant complication of severe hypertriglyceridaemia (TG above this level causes chylomicronaemia and can precipitate pancreatitis via hydrolysis of TG to toxic fatty acids in pancreatic capillaries). Urgent treatment: fenofibrate, strict dietary fat restriction (<20g/day), alcohol abstinence.

**TG >1800 mg/dL (>20 mmol/L) or symptomatic:** Hospitalise. Specialist management including plasmapheresis in refractory cases.

## Complications

- **Premature ASCVD** — MI, stroke, PAD; particularly in FH (by age 40–50 in untreated heterozygous FH)
- **Acute pancreatitis** — from severe hypertriglyceridaemia (TG >885 mg/dL); pancreatitis from hypertriglyceridaemia is often more severe than gallstone-related pancreatitis
- **Calcific aortic stenosis** — Lp(a) has a specific association with aortic valve calcification
- **Statin-associated myopathy** — rhabdomyolysis can cause AKI (myoglobin precipitation in renal tubules)

## Clinical Insight

Tendon xanthomata are pathognomonic of familial hypercholesterolaemia. A patient with LDL >190 mg/dL and Achilles tendon xanthomata has FH until proven otherwise — this is not a matter of probability. Cascade screening of all first-degree relatives is mandatory: each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the condition, and untreated FH kills in the fifth decade. One identified case should trigger a family cascade.

Lp(a) is not measured in a standard lipid panel and is not reduced by statins. In patients with premature ASCVD, recurrent events on optimal statin therapy, a family history of premature CVD unexplained by standard lipid levels, or calcific aortic stenosis before age 65, measure Lp(a). Elevated levels (>50 mg/dL) are an independent indication for maximising LDL reduction (which indirectly reduces ASCVD risk) and may justify PCSK9 inhibitor therapy.

Never combine gemfibrozil with a statin. The pharmacokinetic interaction via CYP2C8 dramatically increases statin plasma concentrations and rhabdomyolysis risk. If both a statin and a fibrate are required (e.g., combined hyperlipidaemia with TG >500 mg/dL not controlled by statin alone), fenofibrate is the safe choice — it does not significantly inhibit statin metabolism.

## Scenario Walkthrough

**The patient:** A 46-year-old asymptomatic man. Father died of MI at 51. Total cholesterol 313 mg/dL, LDL 217 mg/dL, HDL 35 mg/dL, TG 301 mg/dL. Firm nodular thickenings of the Achilles tendons bilaterally. Xanthelasma around the left eye.

**Step 1 — Recognise the pathognomonic sign: tendon xanthomata = familial hypercholesterolaemia:** Tendon xanthomata — firm, lobulated deposits of cholesterol within tendon substance (Achilles, extensor tendons of the hand, patellar tendon) — are **pathognomonic of FH**. They do not occur in polygenic hypercholesterolaemia or dietary hyperlipidaemia. Their presence, combined with LDL 217 mg/dL, makes this **definite FH** by the Dutch Lipid Clinic Network criteria (8+ points: LDL >190 mg/dL = 5 points + tendon xanthomata = 6 points total). Xanthelasma (periorbital deposits) can occur in FH but are less specific — they also occur in normolipidaemic individuals.

**Step 2 — Calculate ASCVD risk — this patient is HIGH RISK regardless of SCORE2:** FH with LDL 217 mg/dL + premature family history (father, MI at 51 = <55 in a first-degree male relative = premature) = **high-risk FH**. In the absence of established CVD, this is high-risk; if he has any established ASCVD or DM, he is very high-risk. The LDL target for high-risk FH is **<70 mg/dL** (≥50% reduction from baseline), meaning LDL needs to drop from 217 mg/dL to <70 mg/dL — a reduction of more than 67%. Start pharmacological therapy immediately — lifestyle alone will not achieve this.

**Step 3 — Exclude secondary causes first:** Before prescribing, rule out reversible causes of hyperlipidaemia: **TFTs** (hypothyroidism causes elevated LDL and TG); **HbA1c and fasting glucose** (uncontrolled T2DM elevates TG); **LFTs** (cholestatic liver disease); **U&E** (nephrotic syndrome causes massive elevation of LDL due to increased hepatic lipoprotein synthesis). In this patient, TG 301 mg/dL also raises the possibility of secondary hypertriglyceridaemia (alcohol, DM, hypothyroidism) — check all of the above before attributing everything to FH.

**Step 4 — Start high-intensity statin immediately:** Atorvastatin 40–80mg OD or rosuvastatin 20–40mg OD. High-intensity statin reduces LDL by ≥50%. Starting from 217 mg/dL, a 50% reduction yields ~108 mg/dL — still above the <70 mg/dL target. Therefore, plan from the outset to add ezetimibe once the statin is established. Give the statin at night (hepatic cholesterol synthesis peaks nocturnally). Check LFTs and CK at baseline; check fasting lipids and LFTs at 8–12 weeks.

**Step 5 — Add ezetimibe when statin alone is insufficient:** At the 3-month review, if LDL is not at <70 mg/dL on high-intensity statin alone, add **ezetimibe 10mg OD** (NPC1L1 inhibitor; reduces LDL by a further 15–20%). The combination of atorvastatin 80mg + ezetimibe achieves approximately 65–70% LDL reduction. If LDL is still not at target → refer to specialist lipid clinic; consider PCSK9 inhibitor (evolocumab or alirocumab SC injection) — reduces LDL by 50–60% on top of statin + ezetimibe. FH is one of the strongest indications for PCSK9 inhibitor therapy.

**Step 6 — TG 301 mg/dL — address alongside LDL management:** TG 301 mg/dL (moderately elevated; risk of pancreatitis rises significantly above 885 mg/dL, but 301 mg/dL still warrants attention). Advise: reduce alcohol, refined carbohydrates, and simple sugars; control weight; treat any secondary causes found. If TG remains >500 mg/dL after lifestyle and statin therapy → add **fenofibrate** (NOT gemfibrozil, which is dangerous with statins). High-dose omega-3 (4g/day) can reduce TG by 20–30%.

**Step 7 — Cascade screening of first-degree relatives — mandatory:** FH is autosomal dominant — each first-degree relative has a 50% chance of inheriting it. The patient's children, siblings, and parents must all have fasting lipid profiles measured. Genetic testing identifies the causative variant (LDLR, ApoB, PCSK9 mutations in >80% of FH cases) and enables cascade screening of at-risk relatives. This is not optional — FH in an untreated family member will cause a fatal MI in their 40s or 50s, and identifying and treating them can be lifesaving.

**Exam trigger:** *LDL 217 mg/dL + Achilles tendon xanthomata + premature family history of CVD* → **familial hypercholesterolaemia**. Tendon xanthomata are pathognomonic — the diagnosis is definite. Start high-intensity statin immediately (atorvastatin 40–80mg). LDL target <70 mg/dL (high risk). Add ezetimibe if not at target. **Cascade screen all first-degree relatives** — mandatory. **Never combine gemfibrozil with a statin** (use fenofibrate if a fibrate is needed). The distractor is treating this as simple dietary hyperlipidaemia and recommending lifestyle measures alone — the tendon xanthomata make FH definite and demand immediate, intensive pharmacological therapy.
