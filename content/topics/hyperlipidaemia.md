## Pathophysiology

**Lipid transport:**
- **LDL** — delivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues. High LDL → endothelial uptake → foam cells → atherosclerotic plaque. "Lousy" cholesterol.
- **HDL** — reverse cholesterol transport from periphery back to liver. "Healthy" cholesterol.
- **Triglycerides (TG)** — elevated in obesity, diabetes, alcohol, hypothyroidism, CKD. Markedly elevated TG (>10 mmol/L) → acute pancreatitis.
- **Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)]** — independent cardiovascular risk factor, not affected by statins.

**Atherogenesis:** LDL infiltrates the endothelium → oxidised → taken up by macrophages → foam cells → fatty streak → fibrous plaque → vulnerable plaque → rupture → ACS.

**Familial Hypercholesterolaemia (FH):**
- Autosomal dominant. LDL receptor mutation → severely impaired LDL clearance.
- LDL often >7–8 mmol/L at baseline, not explained by diet alone.
- Clinical features: tendon xanthomata (Achilles, extensor tendons of fingers), xanthelasma, corneal arcus <45 years, premature CAD (men <55, women <65), strong family history.
- **Simon Broome criteria** or **Dutch Lipid Clinic criteria** for diagnosis.
- Requires aggressive treatment (often high-intensity statin + ezetimibe ± PCSK9 inhibitor).

**Secondary causes of dyslipidaemia:**
- Hypothyroidism (↑LDL, ↑TG)
- Diabetes (↑TG, ↓HDL)
- CKD (↑TG, ↓HDL)
- Nephrotic syndrome (↑LDL, ↑TG)
- Alcohol (↑TG, ↑HDL)
- Cushing's syndrome
- Drugs: corticosteroids, thiazides, beta-blockers, retinoids

→ **Always check TFTs, glucose, renal function, LFTs before starting lipid-lowering therapy**

## Clinical Reasoning

**Most dyslipidaemia is asymptomatic.** It is a risk factor, not a disease in itself. The job is cardiovascular risk assessment.

**Physical signs (often absent but important when present):**
- Tendon xanthomata → strongly suggests FH
- Xanthelasma → lipid deposits around eyes (not specific to FH)
- Corneal arcus <45 years → associated with dyslipidaemia
- Eruptive xanthomata → hypertriglyceridaemia (TG usually >10 mmol/L)
- Lipaemia retinalis → TG >20 mmol/L

*Inner Circle note: The "normal" LDL level cited in lab values may be 160 mg/dL (4.1 mmol/L), but the treatment target is <100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) for high-risk patients — don't let the lab reference range mislead you.*

## Workup

**Fasting lipid panel:**
- Total cholesterol, LDL, HDL, triglycerides
- Non-fasting acceptable for initial screening (if TG not elevated)
- Calculate non-HDL cholesterol (total − HDL) — better risk marker than LDL alone

**Secondary cause screen:** TFTs, fasting glucose/HbA1c, U&E, LFTs, urine ACR

**SCORE2 or QRISK3 tool:** 10-year cardiovascular risk calculation for primary prevention decisions

**Baseline before statin:** LFTs and CK (to monitor for statin side effects)

## Management

### 1. Lifestyle (essential, always first)

- **Dietary:** Reduce saturated fat, increase omega-3 (oily fish), increase soluble fibre (oats, legumes), reduce refined carbohydrates (for TG)
- **Weight loss:** Each 10% body weight loss reduces LDL ~15%, raises HDL, lowers TG
- **Exercise:** ≥150 min/week moderate intensity — raises HDL
- **Alcohol reduction:** Markedly reduces TG
- **Smoking cessation:** Raises HDL, reduces cardiovascular risk

### 2. Statins — First-line Pharmacotherapy

**Mechanism:** HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors → ↓ hepatic cholesterol synthesis → ↑ LDL receptor upregulation → ↑ LDL clearance from blood.

**High-intensity statins** (reduce LDL by ≥50%): Atorvastatin 40–80mg, Rosuvastatin 20–40mg.

**Indications:**
- All patients with established CVD (very high risk) — regardless of baseline LDL
- Primary prevention if 10-year QRISK ≥10% (NICE) or SCORE ≥5% (ESC)
- All diabetics >40 years (Inner Circle)
- All patients with FH

**Side effects:**
- **Myopathy** — myalgia (most common, ~5–10%), rarely rhabdomyolysis. Check CK if symptomatic.
- Raised LFTs — usually transient, clinically significant hepatotoxicity rare
- New-onset diabetes — small increased risk (outweighed by CV benefit)
- **Rhabdomyolysis risk increases with:** high-dose statins, fibrate co-prescription, CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, antifungals, grapefruit juice), hypothyroidism (always check TFTs)

### 3. Ezetimibe

**Mechanism:** Inhibits intestinal cholesterol absorption (NPC1L1 transporter). Reduces LDL by ~15–20%.

**Use:** Add to statin if target not reached (IMPROVE-IT trial — modest additional CV benefit over statin alone).

### 4. PCSK9 Inhibitors (Evolocumab, Alirocumab)

**Mechanism:** Monoclonal antibodies against PCSK9 → prevents LDL receptor degradation → more LDL cleared from blood. Reduce LDL by ~50–60%.

**Use:** Very high-risk patients not at target on maximum statin + ezetimibe, or statin-intolerant. FH. Expensive but highly effective (FOURIER, ODYSSEY OUTCOMES trials).

### 5. Managing Hypertriglyceridaemia

- TG 2–10 mmol/L: Lifestyle (alcohol, diet, exercise), treat secondary causes, fibrates or omega-3 fatty acids
- TG >10 mmol/L: Risk of **acute pancreatitis** → urgent treatment with fibrates (fenofibrate) + strict lifestyle
- Statins have modest TG-lowering effect and remain first-line for overall CV risk reduction

**Fibrates (fenofibrate, gemfibrozil):**
- PPAR-α agonists → ↑ LPL activity → ↓ TG, ↑ HDL
- **Never combine gemfibrozil with statins** → rhabdomyolysis risk. Fenofibrate is safer.

## High Yield Pearls

- Statin myopathy risk increases with: high dose + fibrate combination + CYP3A4 inhibitors + hypothyroidism
- Tendon xanthomata = Familial Hypercholesterolaemia until proven otherwise — screen family
- TG >10 mmol/L → risk of acute pancreatitis — urgent treatment required
- PCSK9 inhibitors: most powerful LDL-lowering agents available, especially for FH or statin-intolerant patients
- Lp(a) is an independent CV risk factor not reduced by statins — consider PCSK9 inhibitor or specific Lp(a) lowering agents
- Always exclude secondary causes (hypothyroidism, nephrotic syndrome, diabetes) before starting statin therapy
